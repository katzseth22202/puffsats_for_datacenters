\begin{abstract}
PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion is a non-nuclear kinetic analogue of Project Orion's nuclear pulse propulsion. Small, low-cost satellites (``PuffSats'') fly an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop that closes in 2.2 or 3.3 years. They return on retrograde orbits at Earth-relative speeds of \qtyrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds methalox's specific combustion energy by more than a hundredfold.

A suborbital rocket lifts the target vehicle above the atmosphere, and PuffSat pulses supply the remaining $\deltav$. Each arriving PuffSat disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the target. The hot gas either strikes a pusher plate or expands through a pulsed nozzle that can thrust in any direction. We argue that each target vehicle can send more new PuffSat mass toward Jupiter than the incoming mass it consumes. Repeated across generations, this surplus would sustain exponential growth in launch capacity with no further orbital-class chemical boosters. We also sketch a variant that adds an Oberth maneuver (a velocity change at perihelion, where high speed multiplies the energy gained) and returns PuffSats at Earth-relative speeds above \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}.

Finally, we apply the architecture to orbital data centers. Data centers are now wartime targets, so we argue they belong in orbits distant enough that an attack takes days rather than minutes to arrive. AI training and coding inference tolerate the seconds of light delay to Earth that this adds. Reaching those orbits takes a large $\deltav$, and under the stated assumptions PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can deliver mass there more cheaply than fully reusable rockets such as SpaceX Starship.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} is a proposed non-nuclear propulsion system that uses the kinetic energy of small, low-cost satellites to accelerate a target vehicle. Each satellite, called a PuffSat, flies an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop and returns on a retrograde heliocentric orbit. The return speed relative to Earth is \qtyrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds methalox's specific combustion energy by more than a hundredfold. The Jupiter flyby reverses each PuffSat's orbital direction and sends it sunward. The loop therefore closes in 2.2 or 3.3 years (two or three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods), rather than the 5.5 years of two minimum-energy legs. PuffSats depart with an Earth-relative hyperbolic excess speed of \qtyrange{12.0}{14.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. New Horizons had an excess speed of \SI{12.6}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and reached Jupiter in 404 days~\cite{guo2008newhorizons_design}, and Ulysses had an excess speed of \SI{11.3}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and reached it in 16 months~\cite{marsden1992ulysses_jupiter}. \autoref{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists} gives the trajectory details, and the calculations are in the companion code repository~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}.

The concept builds on Project Orion~\cite{projorion}, which proposed using nuclear explosions to deliver repeated impulses to a spacecraft, and on pellet-beam propulsion~\cite{singer1979interstellar,willis1994momentum,nordley2001interstellar,nordley2015massbeam,kare2001sailbeam,davoyan2023pelletbeam,whitmire_jackson1977laser_ramjet,matloff1979runway,kare1997buzzbomb,greason2020runway,greason2022windpellet,winglee_magbeam,landis2004particlebeam}. PuffSats reach their extreme velocities through gravity assists, which are flight-proven, rather than through nuclear explosions or through beam launchers that have not been built. A conventional suborbital rocket lifts the target vehicle above the atmosphere, and PuffSat pulses supply the remaining $\deltav$. As each PuffSat approaches, its working mass disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the target vehicle. The collision converts relative kinetic energy into hot gas. This gas either strikes a pusher plate, transferring an impulse to the target vehicle, or enters a chamber and expands through a pulsed nozzle. The nozzle directs the exhaust to produce thrust, including thrust toward the incoming PuffSats. 

Each PuffSat must preserve its payload and electronics through the interplanetary flight despite radiation, micrometeorites, and temperature extremes. Its structural components must avoid striking the target vehicle. \autoref{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture} sketches a satellite design addressing these requirements. The target vehicle's hardware must survive repeated pulses while capturing the arriving flow. \autoref{sec:pusher_plates} and \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles} discuss pusher plates and pulsed nozzles.

PuffSat streams are designed to fly as tight formations, so that a target vehicle meeting the first PuffSat needs only small corrections to meet the rest. PRISMA demonstrated autonomous relative navigation to 8 cm in orbit~\cite{damico2011prisma_safe}, and the VISORS mission requires centimeter-level alignment between two CubeSats~\cite{guffanti2023autonomous}. A PuffSat formation would need similar precision across thousands of PuffSats. A formation that holds its shape can still arrive off target, so the target vehicle must measure each PuffSat's offset and correct between successive impacts. \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation} addresses these guidance, navigation, and control problems.

Launched payloads can include replacement PuffSats as well as useful cargo. If each returning generation enables a greater mass of PuffSats to be sent onto new Jupiter trajectories, the returning mass grows exponentially from cycle to cycle. Under that condition, arriving PuffSats propel both cargo and the next generation, allowing the cycle to continue without further orbital-class chemical boosters after conventional launches seed it. The initial launch expense is then spread over an increasing delivered mass. \autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth} examines this growth condition.

Large orbital data centers provide a potential near-term market for very heavy lift~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher,nvidia2025_starcloud,foust2026_starcloud_fcc}. Iran's 2026 strikes on terrestrial data centers~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike} show that data centers are military targets. China's 2007 destruction of the FY-1C weather satellite~\cite{nasa_odqn2007_fengyun,kan2007_asat} confirms that direct-ascent suborbital missiles can reach sun-synchronous orbit in minutes. In \autoref{sec:data_center_attacks}, we argue for orbits distant enough that an attack takes days to arrive, leaving time to respond. Because each cluster is co-located~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}, only its link to Earth slows down, and AI training and long-running coding inference can tolerate the added round-trip latency of \qtyrange{2.6}{10}{\second}~\cite{anthropic_message_batches,openai_batch_api}. Reaching them takes more $\deltav$, which favors PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion over conventional rockets. Launch must also be cheap enough for orbital computing to compete with data centers on the ground. Starcloud's chief executive puts that price near \$500 per kilogram~\cite{bennici2026_johnston}. Google's Suncatcher team sets a stricter bar, finding that at \$200 per kilogram, launch spread over a satellite's life costs about as much as a terrestrial data center's energy~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} argues that PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can deliver mass to these orbits for less than fully reusable rockets such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship}.

\autoref{sec:oberth_solar_maneuvers} sketches a variant that adds an Oberth maneuver near the Sun to the Earth--Jupiter loop, producing Earth-relative speeds above \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. This paper complements the long-term conceptual roadmap of \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim} by focusing on near-term applications.

\section{PuffSat Satellite Architecture}\label{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture}
\subsection{Deployment Rocket}\label{sec:deployment_rocket}
PuffSats do not fly the Jupiter loop alone. A large deployment rocket carries them out, through the Jupiter flyby, and back toward Earth, and releases them only on the final approach. Until then it supplies their power and keeps their water liquid.

Sunlight can do that job at Jupiter. Juno has run on about \SI{500}{\watt} from three \SI{9}{\meter} arrays since it arrived in 2016~\cite{bolton2017juno,juno_solar_record}. A rocket carrying many PuffSats needs proportionally less. Heat leaks through its surface, which grows as the square of its size, while the mass it keeps warm grows as the cube. Its panels can therefore be a smaller fraction of its mass than Juno's. The flyby does pass through Jupiter's shadow, so the rocket carries a battery to keep its electronics and the PuffSats' water warm until it returns to sunlight.

Radiation is the harder problem. The flyby turns the trajectory within Jupiter's orbital plane, and Jupiter's equator is tilted only \ang{3.1} from that plane. The rocket therefore crosses the equatorial radiation belts that Juno's polar orbit was designed to pass under~\cite{bolton2017juno,katz2026aim}. The PuffSats themselves are the shield. Their mass is mostly water and other light volatiles, enough to surround the electronics with \qtyrange{50}{100}{\centi\meter} of material. Electrons, and the bremsstrahlung X-rays they emit as they stop, set most of the dose~\cite{roussos2021jovian_belts,katz2026aim}. Water also slows the neutrons that energetic protons and photons knock out of nuclei. Boron carbide at about \SI{0.5}{\percent} of the shield's mass then captures them without the penetrating \SI{2.2}{\mega\electronvolt} gamma ray that hydrogen capture emits~\cite{katz2026aim}. No full dose budget for the pass has been computed, so the shield thickness is a requirement rather than a result.

Solar cells are the part of the power system the belts damage most, because a panel has to face open space to work. At Jupiter's distance sunlight delivers about \SI{50}{\watt\per\square\meter}, a twenty-seventh of its flux at Earth. A non-imaging concentrator, a mirror or lens that funnels light from a wide aperture onto a small target without forming an image, shrinks the cell area by its concentration ratio~\cite{winston2005nonimaging}. A ratio of 27 puts Earth-strength sunlight back on the cell. Seen from Jupiter, the Sun is a disk only \ang{0.05} in radius, so the concentrated beam stays narrow. Even with \ang{1} of pointing error it stays within about \ang{5} of its axis~\cite{winston2005nonimaging,katz2026aim}. A beam that narrow can enter a mirrored cavity and turn two corners on its way to cells inside the shield (\autoref{fig:mirrored_light_cavity}).

The bends do the shielding, because only light turns the corners. Jupiter's magnetic field cannot steer a particle around one. Even at the equatorial cloud tops, where the dipole field is \SI{0.417}{\milli\tesla}~\cite{connerney2018jrm09}, a \SI{10}{\mega\electronvolt} electron circles with a radius of about \SI{85}{\meter} and strays only about \SI{1}{\centi\meter} over a \SI{3}{\meter} cavity~\cite{katz2026aim}. X-rays from outside are stopped too, because a metal reflects them only below a glancing angle of about \ang{0.24}~\cite{alsnielsen2011xray}. Some electrons do scatter back off the first mirror, \SI{9}{\percent} of them at \SI{1}{\mega\electronvolt} on aluminum at normal incidence~\cite{miller1970backscatter}. Those that stop in it radiate bremsstrahlung. The second bend hides the cells from the first mirror, so this secondary radiation must scatter again to reach them. Each aluminum fold reflects about nine tenths of visible light~\cite{hass1961aluminum}, so two folds pass about \SI{81}{\percent}, and the concentration ratio is raised to cover the loss. Neither the cavity's throughput, including mirror darkening under the belt dose, nor the dose at the cells has been computed, so both are requirements rather than results.

The rocket releases the PuffSats as far as \SI{1.3}{\astronomicalunit} from the Sun. From there the coast to Earth lasts about 16 days~\cite{katz2026aim}. Each PuffSat is a bag of water around an inner bag of explosive emulsion, and the water holds the emulsion's temperature through the coast. Just before release, a deployment mechanism spins and orients each PuffSat and kneads the emulsion. Kneading mixes in the sensitizers, which stay out until late in flight so that the charge is hard to set off by accident~\cite{maranda2025comprehensive}. It also mixes in slow-acting thickeners that lock the sensitizing bubbles in place, and it lets each PuffSat fly without a pump.

Release from the deployment rocket (\autoref{sec:deployment_rocket}) spaces the PuffSats so that they arrive over the few minutes of a push. A PuffSat released with a small velocity change drifts ahead of or behind its neighbors by that change times the coast time. Spreading a four-minute push this way takes at most \SI{4.4}{\meter\per\second} per PuffSat~\cite{katz2026aim}. The rocket pays that change, not the PuffSat. It can push PuffSats off in matched forward and backward pairs, so that its own momentum does not change, or burn between releases. Either way costs it under \SI{10}{\meter\per\second}. The stream is aimed at Earth, so the rocket must then leave it. A \SI{7}{\meter\per\second} burn at release moves its arrival \SI{10000}{\kilo\meter} to the side, clear of the atmosphere~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\section{Data Centers, Anti-Satellite Weapons, And Launch Costs}\label{sec:data_center_attacks}
Data centers are now military targets. In March 2026, Iranian drones struck Amazon Web Services data centers in the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. Project Suncatcher~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher} and Starcloud~\cite{foust2026_starcloud_fcc} both plan sun-synchronous orbits, Starcloud in shells at \qtyrange{600}{850}{\kilo\meter}. In 2007, China destroyed the Fengyun-1C weather satellite at \qty{865}{\kilo\meter} with a ground-launched missile~\cite{kan2007_asat,nasa_odqn2007_fengyun}, and China and Russia both now field such weapons~\cite{dia2022_space_security}. A sun-synchronous orbit passes over nearly every latitude~\cite{nasa_orbit_catalog}, so attackers get repeated chances and the interceptor arrives within minutes.

One reason satellite attacks are rare is that the military benefits aren't worth the cost of hitting dispersed targets~\cite{samson2025_swf_counterspace,hallex2020_proliferated_constellations}. The director of the US Space Development Agency says ``it will cost more to shoot down a single satellite than it will cost to build that single satellite''~\cite{hadley2023_tournear}. An orbital data center cannot spread out that way, because its nodes need high-bandwidth links to each other. Suncatcher's illustrative cluster puts 81 satellites, \qty{2.3}{\mega\watt} of computing, within a \qty{1}{\kilo\meter} radius~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. Nor can it hide. Homing on radar and infrared returns is within reach of many countries~\cite{wright2005_physics_space_security}. Starcloud's panels would span about \qty{4}{\kilo\meter} on a side~\cite{nvidia2025_starcloud}, and a data center sheds nearly all its power as heat, so a seeker has a conspicuous radar and infrared target.

Distance does not hide a data center, but it buys warning time. Reaching a Lagrange point takes \qty{11.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} against \qty{3.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second} to reach \qty{650}{\kilo\meter}~\cite{katz2026aim}. Even at \qty{14}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, an interceptor needs half a day to reach Earth--Moon L4/L5 and two days to reach Sun--Earth L1. That is time to disperse a cluster, or to launch interceptors based with it, like the escorts the U.S. Space Force plans for its own satellites~\cite{easley2025_ussf_framework}. In September 2026, the Secretary of the Air Force said that the United States has weapons in orbit, without saying what they are~\cite{ap2026_meink_space_weapons}. Whether they include a defense of this kind is not public.

Latency decides which workloads go where. High Earth orbit at \qtyrange{20000}{80000}{\kilo\meter} adds \qtyrange{0.13}{0.53}{\second} of round-trip delay and still gives \qty{0.5}{\hour} to \qty{14}{\hour} of warning, so it suits latency-sensitive inference. AI training and batch inference, including long-running coding agents, can go to the Lagrange points. The \qtyrange{2.6}{10}{\second} round trip there is far inside the \qty{24}{\hour} turnaround that batch inference services already offer~\cite{anthropic_message_batches,openai_batch_api}. Sun--Earth L1 has no eclipses at all~\cite{nasa_lagrange_points}, but the James Webb Space Telescope's always sunny halo orbit at Sun--Earth L2 shows other libration-point orbits can also avoid ever falling into shadow~\cite{nasa_webb_orbit}. The cost is exposure to solar particle events outside the magnetosphere~\cite{li2019vanallen}. Suncatcher's proton test ran a commercial tensor processing unit to 15~krad(Si) without hard failures~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}.

Starship~\cite{starship} can reach high orbits, but launch costs near term are uncompetitive with terrestrial data centers. Starcloud's chief executive puts competitive pricing near \$500 per \si{\kilo\gram}~\cite{bennici2026_johnston}. SpaceX's IPO underwriters, who have reason to be optimistic~\cite{michaely1999_underwriter}, forecast \$183 (Goldman Sachs~\cite{pethokoukis2026_goldman}) and about \$500 (Morgan Stanley~\cite{investing2026_ms_spacex}) per \si{\kilo\gram} to low Earth orbit in 2030. Sun--Earth L1 lies a further \qtyrange{3.2}{4.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} out, paid for with propellant from two to four tanker flights. Each kilogram delivered there costs 3.5 to 4.6 kilograms launched to low Earth orbit~\cite{katz2026aim}, turning the forecasts into \$640--840 and \$1750--2300 per \si{\kilo\gram}. High circular orbits are generally harder still. Circularizing at \qty{22000}{\kilo\meter} takes \qty{1.45}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, and leaving takes \qty{1.46}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, against about \qty{30}{\meter\per\second} to leave Sun--Earth L1~\cite{katz2026aim}.

These estimates are floors. They count the tanker propellant but not the logistics and operations of orbital refueling, which SpaceX lists as a material challenge~\cite{spacex2026_s1a}. A ship back from L1 enters at \qty{11.1}{\kilo\meter\per\second} rather than \qty{8.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, about 2.7 times the peak heating~\cite{katz2026aim}, on a heat shield SpaceX's chief executive called Starship's biggest challenge~\cite{musk2024_heatshield}. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} argues that PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can meet the \$500 per \si{\kilo\gram} target in high orbits.
