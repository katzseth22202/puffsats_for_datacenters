\begin{abstract}\label{sec:abstract}
% Elevator pitch. The hook, then just enough context to know what the paper is.
% The siting argument, its citations, and the cost case belong in the body.
% See docs/brief_guide.md and CONTEXT.md.
High-thrust, high-specific-impulse propulsion is a long-standing goal of rocket propulsion. Nuclear-pulse concepts such as Project Orion~\cite{projorion} showed in principle how externally supplied, high-energy pulses could escape the traditional chemical-propulsion tradeoff between thrust and specific impulse, but at the unacceptable cost of using nuclear explosives.

This paper introduces \textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion}, a non-nuclear analogue of Orion in which small satellites, dubbed PuffSats, are sent on Jupiter-gravity-assist trajectories that return them to Earth at \SIrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, corresponding to \SIrange{1.5}{2.3}{\giga\joule\per\kilogram} of kinetic energy. Shortly before intercepting the target vehicle, each PuffSat disperses into gas, which strikes a pusher plate. Additional propellant carried by the target vehicle is injected into and mixed with the resulting plasma, increasing the momentum transferred per kilogram of returning PuffSat mass. Alternatively, the incoming material can mix with injected propellant inside a pulsed nozzle chamber, allowing the resulting expansion to produce directed thrust, including thrust toward the oncoming PuffSat.

We develop one candidate PuffSat architecture and evaluate its performance. As a motivating application, we consider the launch of large orbital data centers and examine whether such a system could achieve launch costs competitive with near-term fully reusable launch vehicles such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship}.

The broader architectural implications of PuffSat propulsion are explored in the companion paper \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Pushing a spacecraft with mass supplied from outside it is an old idea with one persistent
obstacle. Singer proposed an unguided pellet stream thrown by an electromagnetic mass driver in
1979~\cite{singer1979interstellar}. Nordley replaced the unguided pellets with self-steering
particles~\cite{nordley2001interstellar}, Kare replaced the mass driver with laser-driven miniature
sails~\cite{kare2001sailbeam}, and Davoyan is working on accelerating pellets by laser
ablation~\cite{davoyan2023pelletbeam}. Nordley and Crowl survey the family~\cite{nordley2015massbeam}.
Every one of these designs needs an accelerator that has never been built, at a scale set by
interstellar ambitions. That missing accelerator, rather than the impact physics, is where the family
has stalled.

PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion removes the accelerator. A PuffSat reaches hypervelocity on a Jupiter
gravity assist, so the closing speed is bought with orbital mechanics and paid for by the same
conventional rockets that launched it. Two prerequisites replace the accelerator, cheap bulk launch
and precise terminal guidance, and both have flight heritage. The companion white paper \textit{Aim
Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim} compares this architecture to each of the designs above in its
section ``Comparison To Pellet Beam Propulsion Prior Art,'' and that comparison is not repeated here.

The design problems that remain are not the ones the pellet beam family faced. A PuffSat must arrive
as gas, with no solid fragment large enough to damage the plate it hits. It must be guided onto that
plate at the end of a transfer measured in years. A large swarm of PuffSats must fly in formation.
The collision must transfer energy and momentum efficiently enough for the architecture to beat the
rocket that launched the PuffSats.
% Each of the four challenges above wants an \autoref pointer to the section that takes it on.
% Attach them once those sections exist; today only sec:background does.

This paper sketches one PuffSat architecture, sized to launch large orbital data centers at a price
competitive with fully reusable launch vehicles. Where those data centers sit is not a free choice.
Iran's drone strikes on Amazon's Gulf data centers in March 2026 established that data centers are
wartime targets~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. Those were drones against
buildings, so they say nothing about orbital reach. China's destruction of the FY-1C weather
satellite at \SI{865}{\kilo\meter} in January 2007 says a great deal~\cite{kelso2007asat,nasa_odqn_2007}.
A direct-ascent interceptor arrives at sun-synchronous altitude in minutes, which leaves no window to
disperse a cluster or to engage the inbound vehicle. A transfer to an Earth-Moon Lagrange point takes
days, at a cost of \SI{2.6}{\second} of round-trip light time to an Earth customer. Distance does not
hide a data center, and a published halo orbit is as predictable as a sun-synchronous one. What
distance buys is the time an active response needs.

\autoref{sec:background} develops that siting argument and the latency ladder behind it. The launch
cost this architecture has to beat depends on where the cluster sits, so the siting question and the
cost target are settled together.
