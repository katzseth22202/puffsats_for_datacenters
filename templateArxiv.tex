\begin{abstract}
PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion is a non-nuclear kinetic analogue of Project Orion's nuclear pulse propulsion. Small, low-cost satellites (``PuffSats'') fly an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop that closes in 2.2 or 3.3 years. They return on retrograde orbits at Earth-relative speeds of \qtyrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds methalox's specific combustion energy by more than a hundredfold.

A suborbital rocket lifts the target vehicle above the atmosphere, and PuffSat pulses supply the remaining $\deltav$. Each arriving PuffSat disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the target. The hot gas either strikes a pusher plate or expands through a pulsed nozzle that can thrust in any direction. We argue that each target vehicle can send more new PuffSat mass toward Jupiter than the incoming mass it consumes. Repeated across generations, this surplus would sustain exponential growth in launch capacity with no further orbital-class chemical boosters. We also sketch a variant that adds an Oberth maneuver (a velocity change at perihelion, where high speed multiplies the energy gained) and returns PuffSats at Earth-relative speeds above \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}.

Finally, we apply the architecture to orbital data centers. Data centers are now wartime targets, so we argue they belong in orbits distant enough that an attack takes days rather than minutes to arrive. AI training and coding inference tolerate the seconds of light delay to Earth that this adds. Reaching those orbits takes a large $\deltav$, and under the stated assumptions PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can deliver mass there more cheaply than fully reusable rockets such as SpaceX Starship.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} is a proposed non-nuclear propulsion system that uses the kinetic energy of small, low-cost satellites to accelerate a target vehicle. Each satellite, called a PuffSat, flies an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop and returns on a retrograde heliocentric orbit. The return speed relative to Earth is \qtyrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds methalox's specific combustion energy by more than a hundredfold. The Jupiter flyby reverses each PuffSat's orbital direction and sends it sunward. The loop therefore closes in 2.2 or 3.3 years (two or three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods), rather than the 5.5 years of two minimum-energy legs. PuffSats depart with an Earth-relative hyperbolic excess speed of \qtyrange{12.0}{14.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. New Horizons had an excess speed of \SI{12.6}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and reached Jupiter in 404 days~\cite{guo2008newhorizons_design}, and Ulysses had an excess speed of \SI{11.3}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and reached it in 16 months~\cite{marsden1992ulysses_jupiter}. \autoref{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists} gives the trajectory details, and the calculations are in the companion code repository~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}.

The concept builds on Project Orion~\cite{projorion}, which proposed using nuclear explosions to deliver repeated impulses to a spacecraft, and on pellet-beam propulsion~\cite{singer1979interstellar,willis1994momentum,nordley2001interstellar,nordley2015massbeam,kare2001sailbeam,davoyan2023pelletbeam,whitmire_jackson1977laser_ramjet,matloff1979runway,kare1997buzzbomb,greason2020runway,greason2022windpellet,winglee_magbeam,landis2004particlebeam}. PuffSats reach their extreme velocities through gravity assists, which are flight-proven, rather than through nuclear explosions or through beam launchers that have not been built. A conventional suborbital rocket lifts the target vehicle above the atmosphere, and PuffSat pulses supply the remaining $\deltav$. As each PuffSat approaches, its working mass disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the target vehicle. The collision converts relative kinetic energy into hot gas. This gas either strikes a pusher plate, transferring an impulse to the target vehicle, or enters a chamber and expands through a pulsed nozzle. The nozzle directs the exhaust to produce thrust, including thrust toward the incoming PuffSats. 

Each PuffSat must preserve its payload and electronics through the interplanetary flight despite radiation, micrometeorites, and temperature extremes. Its structural components must avoid striking the target vehicle. \autoref{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture} sketches a satellite design addressing these requirements. The target vehicle's hardware must survive repeated pulses while capturing the arriving flow. \autoref{sec:pusher_plates} and \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles} discuss pusher plates and pulsed nozzles.

PuffSat streams are designed to fly as tight formations, so that a target vehicle meeting the first PuffSat needs only small corrections to meet the rest. PRISMA demonstrated autonomous relative navigation to 8 cm in orbit~\cite{damico2011prisma_safe}, and the VISORS mission requires centimeter-level alignment between two CubeSats~\cite{guffanti2023autonomous}. A PuffSat formation would need similar precision across thousands of PuffSats. A formation that holds its shape can still arrive off target, so the target vehicle must measure each PuffSat's offset and correct between successive impacts. \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation} addresses these guidance, navigation, and control problems.

Launched payloads can include replacement PuffSats as well as useful cargo. If each returning generation enables a greater mass of PuffSats to be sent onto new Jupiter trajectories, the returning mass grows exponentially from cycle to cycle. Under that condition, arriving PuffSats propel both cargo and the next generation, allowing the cycle to continue without further orbital-class chemical boosters after conventional launches seed it. The initial launch expense is then spread over an increasing delivered mass. \autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth} examines this growth condition.

Large orbital data centers provide a potential near-term market for very heavy lift~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher,nvidia2025_starcloud,foust2026_starcloud_fcc}. Iran's 2026 strikes on terrestrial data centers~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike} show that data centers are military targets. China's 2007 destruction of the FY-1C weather satellite~\cite{nasa_odqn2007_fengyun,kan2007_asat} confirms that direct-ascent suborbital missiles can reach sun-synchronous orbit in minutes. In \autoref{sec:data_center_attacks}, we argue for orbits distant enough that an attack takes days to arrive, leaving time to respond. Because each cluster is co-located~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}, only its link to Earth slows down, and AI training and long-running coding inference can tolerate the added round-trip latency of \qtyrange{2.6}{10}{\second}~\cite{anthropic_message_batches,openai_batch_api}. Reaching them takes more $\deltav$, which favors PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion over conventional rockets. Launch must also be cheap enough for orbital computing to compete with data centers on the ground. Starcloud's chief executive puts that price near \$500 per kilogram~\cite{bennici2026_johnston}. Google's Suncatcher team sets a stricter bar, finding that at \$200 per kilogram, launch spread over a satellite's life costs about as much as a terrestrial data center's energy~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} argues that PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can deliver mass to these orbits for less than fully reusable rockets such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship}.

\autoref{sec:oberth_solar_maneuvers} sketches a variant that adds an Oberth maneuver near the Sun to the Earth--Jupiter loop, producing Earth-relative speeds above \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. This paper complements the long-term conceptual roadmap of \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim} by focusing on near-term applications.

\section{Data Centers, Anti-Satellite Weapons, And Launch Costs}\label{sec:data_center_attacks}

Two precedents bear on orbital data centers. In March 2026, Iranian drones struck Amazon Web Services data centers in the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain, the first deliberate military attack on hyperscale cloud infrastructure~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. That establishes intent. In January 2007, China destroyed the Fengyun-1C weather satellite at \qty{865}{\kilo\meter} with a missile launched from the ground~\cite{kan2007_asat,nasa_odqn2007_fengyun}. That establishes reach, and China and Russia both now field ground-launched weapons against low Earth orbit~\cite{dia2022_space_security}. Attacks on energy infrastructure follow the same pattern. Russian missiles and drones damaged about half of Ukraine's large substations over 2022--23~\cite{iea2024_ukraine_winter}, and Ukrainian drones have repeatedly damaged Russian refineries and ports~\cite{iea2026_omr_april}. In March 2026, attacks in the Middle East and restrictions on tankers through the Strait of Hormuz cut global oil supply by 10.1 million barrels a day~\cite{iea2026_omr_april}. Russia's Shahed-type drones cost tens of thousands of dollars each~\cite{hollenbeck2025_csis_shahed}.

The leading proposals fly where these weapons reach. Project Suncatcher~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher} and Starcloud~\cite{foust2026_starcloud_fcc} both plan dawn--dusk sun-synchronous orbits, Starcloud in shells at \qtyrange{600}{850}{\kilo\meter}. That band contains the altitude of the Fengyun-1C intercept. Iran has already launched satellites to \qty{550}{\kilo\meter} and \qty{750}{\kilo\meter}~\cite{aljazeera2024_chamran}.

An orbital data center is also a better target than other satellites. Its nodes must fly close together to keep high-bandwidth links between them. Suncatcher's illustrative cluster puts 81 satellites within a \qty{1}{\kilo\meter} radius, \qtyrange{100}{200}{\meter} apart, about \qty{2.3}{\mega\watt} of computing at \qty{28}{\kilo\watt} each~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. If each satellite presents \qty{10}{\square\meter} to a cloud of \qty{1}{\gram} pellets, about \qty{300}{\kilo\gram} of pellets puts one expected hit on every satellite in that disk~\cite{katz2026aim}. A reference study models \qty{500}{\kilo\gram} of pellets lofted to \qty{600}{\kilo\meter} on a Nodong-class missile~\cite{wright2005_physics_space_security}. The same study calls such an unguided weapon marginal, because the cloud lands kilometers from its aim point. Homing, below, removes that limit. A proliferated constellation is the opposite case. SpaceX has launched nearly 6,000 Starlink satellites and can build 55 a week~\cite{spacex2024_sustainability}. The director of the Space Development Agency says of his own proliferated constellation that ``it will cost more to shoot down a single satellite than it will cost to build that single satellite''~\cite{hadley2023_tournear}. Starcloud's filing spreads its satellites over many shells~\cite{foust2026_starcloud_fcc}, so no single strike reaches all of them, but each cluster within a shell is exposed in the same way.

The cluster is hard to hide. Starcloud's solar and cooling panels would be about \qty{4}{\kilo\meter} on a side~\cite{nvidia2025_starcloud}, and a data center radiates nearly all of its input power as heat. A satellite in sunlight reflects light and runs warm, both usable for homing, and simple homing systems built from commercial sensors are within reach of many countries~\cite{wright2005_physics_space_security}. A target this large and this hot needs no advanced seeker, and a decoy would have to mimic gigawatts of thermal output. The radiators sit beside the computing they cool, so a seeker that homes on the hottest part arrives at the hardware. That matters, because the area that must be hit to disable a satellite may be much smaller than its total area~\cite{wright2005_physics_space_security}. A sun-synchronous orbit passes over nearly every latitude as Earth turns beneath it~\cite{nasa_orbit_catalog}, so any state with a medium-range missile gets repeated chances to fire. That amounts to a veto on deployment.

Distance does not hide a data center, since a published orbit is equally predictable at any altitude. What distance buys is time, and a higher price for the shot. \autoref{tab:data_center_sites} compares four candidate sites. A direct-ascent interceptor reaches sun-synchronous orbit minutes after launch, which leaves no window to respond. The Fengyun-1C interceptor rode a road-mobile launcher~\cite{kan2007_asat}. A shot at a Lagrange point needs near escape speed, \qty{11.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} from \qty{200}{\kilo\meter}, against the \qty{3.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second} of a lob to \qty{650}{\kilo\meter}. On solid propellant at \qty{250}{\second} of specific impulse, that is 22 times the mass ratio~\cite{katz2026aim}. An attacker who pays still more can arrive sooner. At \qty{14}{\kilo\meter\per\second} a vehicle reaches Earth--Moon L4/L5 in half a day, but still needs two days to reach Sun--Earth L1. Hours to days are enough to disperse a cluster or to send interceptors out. Sun--Earth L1 is therefore the site that turns a no-notice strike into one that can be answered.

Its latency costs little. Round-trip light time to Earth from the Lagrange points is \qtyrange{2.6}{10}{\second}. Batch inference services already accept turnaround of up to \qty{24}{\hour}~\cite{anthropic_message_batches,openai_batch_api}. Training tolerates the delay too, because each training step exchanges data inside the cluster, and Earth only supplies the data and receives finished checkpoints. Bandwidth is the harder limit. A link budget falls as the square of distance, so at Sun--Earth L1 the same terminal carries about 5600 times less data than at \qty{20000}{\kilo\meter}. The site suits work that moves little data per unit of computing. Grouping every cluster at one site might seem to repeat the concentration problem. It does not, because the problem in sun-synchronous orbit is the missing warning. With two days of warning, one stockpile of interceptors can cover every cluster at Sun--Earth L1. No nation has yet deployed such a defense in space.

The Lagrange points also do better than high Earth orbit on the rest of the table. Sun--Earth L1 sees the Sun without interruption, while high Earth orbits have eclipse seasons that a data center must ride out on batteries. L1 is unstable, but holding station there has cost about \qty{1}{\meter\per\second} a year. Reaching L1 also takes \qtyrange{0.3}{1.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} less $\deltav$ than reaching a circular orbit at \qtyrange{20000}{80000}{\kilo\meter}. The main cost is radiation. The lower half of the \qtyrange{20000}{80000}{\kilo\meter} band lies in the outer Van Allen belt, and the Lagrange points lie beyond both the belts and the magnetosphere~\cite{li2019vanallen}. Out there the steady cosmic-ray dose is near 12~rad a year~\cite{zeitlin2013_msl_rad}, below the 150~rad(Si) a year that Suncatcher expects in its own orbit behind \qty{10}{\milli\meter} of aluminum~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. What the sites give up is the magnetosphere's deflection of solar particle events, whose acute dose shielding and redundant computing must absorb. Suncatcher's proton test ran a commercial tensor processing unit to 15~krad(Si) with no hard failures, though its high-bandwidth memory showed irregularities after 2~krad(Si)~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. Shielding against natural radiation is a better-understood problem than defeating a missile.

Both PuffSats and Starship~\cite{starship} can reach any of these sites. SpaceX's IPO underwriters, who have reason to project optimistic numbers~\cite{michaely1999_underwriter}, have forecast Starship's cost to low Earth orbit. Goldman Sachs expects \$183 per \si{\kilo\gram} by 2030~\cite{pethokoukis2026_goldman}. Morgan Stanley sees about \$500 per \si{\kilo\gram} by 2030 and below \$150 per \si{\kilo\gram} by 2040, through reuse of both stages~\cite{investing2026_ms_spacex}. Reusing the second stage means reusing its heat shield, so both forecasts assume a heat shield that works. A commercial space executive and two former NASA thermal-protection engineers wrote a rebuttal, and one of the engineers is an originator of PICA~\cite{nasa_spinoff2013_pica}. They argue that with its current tiles Starship's cost per flight will stay well above \$100--250 per \si{\kilo\gram}~\cite{miller2026_tps}. SpaceX's registration statement lists durable reusable heat shields among its material challenges~\cite{spacex2026_s1a}, and its chief executive called the heat shield Starship's biggest remaining challenge in 2024~\cite{musk2024_heatshield}. In August 2026 he said he would consider the problem solved, while that flight's ship still awaited close inspection~\cite{spacex2026_q2_call}.

High orbits cost Starship more. Sun--Earth L1 lies \qtyrange{3.2}{4.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} beyond low Earth orbit, and Starship buys that with propellant carried up on tanker flights. At \qty{380}{\second} of specific impulse, an \qty{85}{\tonne} ship~\cite{gunter_starship} delivering \qty{100}{\tonne} burns \qtyrange{252}{356}{\tonne} of propellant, two to four tanker flights. Each kilogram delivered to L1 then costs 3.5 to 4.6 kilograms launched to low Earth orbit~\cite{katz2026aim}. Goldman's \$183 becomes \$640 to \$840 per \si{\kilo\gram} at L1, and Morgan Stanley's 2030 figure becomes \$1750 to \$2300. These are floors. They leave out propellant transfer in orbit, which SpaceX also lists among its material challenges~\cite{spacex2026_s1a}, and they leave out the ship's return. Coming home from L1, it enters the atmosphere at \qty{11.1}{\kilo\meter\per\second} rather than \qty{8.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. Peak heating grows roughly as the cube of entry speed, so its heat shield takes about 2.7 times the peak heating~\cite{katz2026aim}. Starcloud's chief executive says orbital computing becomes competitive near \$500 per \si{\kilo\gram}~\cite{bennici2026_johnston}, and Google's Suncatcher team sets a stricter bar at \$200~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. On its own underwriters' forecasts, Starship misses both at the defensible sites. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} argues that PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can deliver mass to them for less.
