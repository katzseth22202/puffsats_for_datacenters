\begin{abstract}
PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion is a non-nuclear kinetic analogue of Project Orion's nuclear pulse propulsion. Small, low-cost satellites (``PuffSats'') fly an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop that closes in 2.2 or 3.3 years. They return on retrograde orbits at Earth-relative speeds of \qtyrange{57}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds methalox's specific combustion energy by more than a hundredfold.

A suborbital rocket lifts the target vehicle above the atmosphere, and PuffSat pulses supply the remaining $\deltav$. Each arriving PuffSat disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the target. The hot gas either strikes a pusher plate or expands through a pulsed nozzle that can thrust in any direction. We argue that each target vehicle can send more new PuffSat mass toward Jupiter than the incoming mass it consumes. Repeated across generations, this surplus would sustain exponential growth in launch capacity with no further orbital-class chemical boosters. We also sketch a variant that adds an Oberth maneuver (a velocity change at perihelion, where high speed multiplies the energy gained) and returns PuffSats at Earth-relative speeds above \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}.

Finally, we apply the architecture to orbital data centers. Data centers are now wartime targets, so we argue they belong in orbits distant enough that an attack takes days rather than minutes to arrive. AI training and coding inference tolerate the seconds of light delay to Earth that this adds. Reaching those orbits takes a large $\deltav$. If the pulsed chamber reaches its simulated efficiency, and at prices that are our hypotheses, PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion could deliver mass there for less than the near-term launch prices forecast for SpaceX Starship.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} is a proposed non-nuclear propulsion system that uses the kinetic energy of small, low-cost satellites to accelerate a target vehicle. Each satellite, called a PuffSat, flies an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop and returns on a retrograde heliocentric orbit. The return speed relative to Earth is \qtyrange{57}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds methalox's specific combustion energy by more than a hundredfold. The Jupiter flyby reverses each PuffSat's orbital direction and sends it sunward. The loop therefore closes in 2.2 or 3.3 years (two or three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods), rather than the 5.5 years of two minimum-energy legs. PuffSats depart with an Earth-relative hyperbolic excess speed of \qtyrange{12.0}{14.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. New Horizons had an excess speed of \SI{12.6}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and reached Jupiter in 404 days~\cite{guo2008newhorizons_design}, and Ulysses had an excess speed of \SI{11.3}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and reached it in 16 months~\cite{marsden1992ulysses_jupiter}. \autoref{fig:jupiter_loop} draws the loop, \autoref{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists} gives the trajectory details, and the calculations are in the companion code repository~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}.

The concept builds on Project Orion~\cite{projorion}, which proposed using nuclear explosions to deliver repeated impulses to a spacecraft, and on pellet-beam propulsion~\cite{singer1979interstellar,willis1994momentum,nordley2001interstellar,nordley2015massbeam,kare2001sailbeam,davoyan2023pelletbeam,whitmire_jackson1977laser_ramjet,matloff1979runway,kare1997buzzbomb,greason2020runway,greason2022windpellet,winglee_magbeam,landis2004particlebeam}. PuffSats reach their extreme velocities through gravity assists, which are flight-proven, rather than through nuclear explosions or through beam launchers that have not been built. A conventional suborbital rocket lifts the target vehicle above the atmosphere, and PuffSat pulses supply the remaining $\deltav$. As each PuffSat approaches, its working mass disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the target vehicle. The collision converts relative kinetic energy into hot gas. This gas either strikes a pusher plate, transferring an impulse to the target vehicle, or enters a chamber and expands through a pulsed nozzle. The nozzle directs the exhaust to produce thrust, including thrust toward the incoming PuffSats. 

Each PuffSat must preserve its payload and electronics through the interplanetary flight despite radiation, micrometeorites, and temperature extremes. Its structural components must avoid striking the target vehicle. \autoref{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture} sketches a satellite design addressing these requirements. The target vehicle's hardware must survive repeated pulses while capturing the arriving flow. \autoref{sec:pusher_plates} and \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles} discuss pusher plates and pulsed nozzles.

PuffSat streams are designed to fly as tight formations, so that a target vehicle meeting the first PuffSat needs only small corrections to meet the rest. A formation that holds its shape can still arrive off target, so the target vehicle must measure each PuffSat's offset and correct between successive impacts. \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation} addresses these guidance, navigation, and control problems.

Launched payloads can include replacement PuffSats as well as useful cargo. If each returning generation enables a greater mass of PuffSats to be sent onto new Jupiter trajectories, the returning mass grows exponentially from cycle to cycle. Under that condition, arriving PuffSats propel both cargo and the next generation, allowing the cycle to continue without further orbital-class chemical boosters after conventional launches seed it. The initial launch expense is then spread over an increasing delivered mass. \autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth} examines this growth condition.

Large orbital data centers provide a potential near-term market for very heavy lift~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher,nvidia2025_starcloud,foust2026_starcloud_fcc}. Iran's 2026 strikes on terrestrial data centers~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike} show that data centers are military targets. China's 2007 destruction of the FY-1C weather satellite~\cite{nasa_odqn2007_fengyun,kan2007_asat} confirms that direct-ascent suborbital missiles can reach sun-synchronous orbit in minutes. In \autoref{sec:data_center_attacks}, we argue for orbits distant enough that an attack takes days to arrive, leaving time to respond. Because each cluster is co-located~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}, only its link to Earth slows down, and AI training and long-running coding inference can tolerate the added round-trip latency of \qtyrange{2.6}{10}{\second}~\cite{anthropic_message_batches,openai_batch_api}. Reaching them takes more $\deltav$, which favors PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion over conventional rockets. Launch must also be cheap enough for orbital computing to compete with data centers on the ground. Starcloud's chief executive puts that price near \$500 per kilogram~\cite{bennici2026_johnston}. Google's Suncatcher team sets a stricter bar, finding that at \$200 per kilogram, launch spread over a satellite's life costs about as much as a terrestrial data center's energy~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} argues that, if its pulsed chamber reaches the simulated efficiency, PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion could deliver mass to these orbits for less than the near-term launch prices forecast for SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship}. At the cheapest Starship price quoted, it would not.

\autoref{sec:oberth_solar_maneuvers} sketches a variant that adds an Oberth maneuver near the Sun to the Earth--Jupiter loop, producing Earth-relative speeds above \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. This paper complements the long-term conceptual roadmap of \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim} by focusing on near-term applications.

\section{PuffSat Satellite Architecture}\label{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture}
\subsection{Deployment Rocket}\label{sec:deployment_rocket}
PuffSats do not fly the Jupiter loop alone. A large deployment rocket carries them out, through the Jupiter flyby, and back toward Earth, and releases them only during the final approach to Earth. Until then it supplies their power and keeps their water liquid.

Sunlight can do that job at Jupiter. Juno has run on about \SI{500}{\watt} from three \SI{9}{\meter} arrays since it arrived in 2016~\cite{bolton2017juno,juno_solar_record}. A rocket carrying many PuffSats needs proportionally less. Heat leaks through its surface, which grows as the square of its size, while the mass it keeps warm grows as the cube. Its panels can therefore be a smaller fraction of its mass than Juno's. The flyby does pass through Jupiter's shadow, so the rocket carries a battery to keep its electronics and the PuffSats' water warm until it returns to sunlight.

Radiation is the harder problem. The flyby turns the trajectory within Jupiter's orbital plane, and Jupiter's equator is tilted only \ang{3.1} from that plane. The rocket therefore crosses the equatorial radiation belts that Juno's polar orbit was designed to pass under~\cite{bolton2017juno,katz2026aim}. The PuffSats themselves are the shield. Their mass is mostly water and other light volatiles, enough to surround the electronics with \qtyrange{50}{100}{\centi\meter} of material. Electrons, and the bremsstrahlung X-rays they emit as they stop, set most of the dose~\cite{roussos2021jovian_belts,katz2026aim}. Water also slows the neutrons that energetic protons and photons knock out of nuclei. Boron carbide at about \SI{0.5}{\percent} of the shield's mass then captures them without the penetrating \SI{2.2}{\mega\electronvolt} gamma ray that hydrogen capture emits~\cite{katz2026aim}. No full dose budget for the pass has been computed, so the shield thickness is a requirement rather than a result.

Solar cells are the part of the power system the belts damage most, because a panel has to face open space to work. At Jupiter's distance sunlight delivers about \SI{50}{\watt\per\square\meter}, a twenty-seventh of its flux at Earth. A non-imaging concentrator, a mirror or lens that funnels light from a wide aperture onto a small target without forming an image, shrinks the cell area by its concentration ratio~\cite{winston2005nonimaging}. A ratio of 27 puts Earth-strength sunlight back on the cell. Seen from Jupiter, the Sun is a disk only \ang{0.05} in radius, so the concentrated beam stays narrow. Even with \ang{1} of pointing error the focused light stays within about \ang{5} of its axis~\cite{winston2005nonimaging,katz2026aim}. A beam that narrow can enter a mirrored cavity and turn two corners on its way to cells inside the shield (\autoref{fig:mirrored_light_cavity}).

The bends do the shielding, because only light turns the corners. Jupiter's magnetic field cannot steer a particle around one. Even at the equatorial cloud tops, where the dipole field is \SI{0.417}{\milli\tesla}~\cite{connerney2018jrm09}, a \SI{10}{\mega\electronvolt} electron circles with a radius of about \SI{85}{\meter} and strays only about \SI{1}{\centi\meter} over a \SI{3}{\meter} cavity~\cite{katz2026aim}. X-rays from outside are stopped too, because a metal reflects them only below a glancing angle of about \ang{0.24}~\cite{alsnielsen2011xray}. Some electrons do scatter back off the first mirror, \SI{9}{\percent} of them at \SI{1}{\mega\electronvolt} on aluminum at normal incidence~\cite{miller1970backscatter}. Those that stop in it radiate bremsstrahlung. The second bend hides the cells from the first mirror, so this secondary radiation must scatter again to reach them. Each aluminum fold reflects about nine tenths of visible light~\cite{hass1961aluminum}, so two folds pass about \SI{81}{\percent}, and the concentration ratio is raised to cover the loss. Neither the cavity's throughput, including mirror darkening under the belt dose, nor the dose at the cells has been computed, so both are requirements rather than results.

The rocket releases the PuffSats as far as \SI{1.3}{\astronomicalunit} from the Sun. From there the coast to Earth lasts about 16 days~\cite{katz2026aim}. Each PuffSat is a bag of water around an inner bag of explosive emulsion, and the water holds the emulsion's temperature through the coast. Just before release, a deployment mechanism spins and orients each PuffSat and kneads the emulsion. Kneading mixes in the sensitizers, which stay out until late in flight so that the charge is hard to set off by accident~\cite{maranda2025comprehensive}. It also mixes in slow-acting thickeners that lock the sensitizing bubbles in place, and it lets each PuffSat fly without a pump.

\subsection{Pusher-Plate PuffSats}\label{sec:pusher_plate_puffsats}
After release, each PuffSat spins. Redundant Kevlar~49 wires, each \SI{100}{\micro\meter} across, tether its electronics to the working mass. Three of them hold each sphere, and together they break at \SI{71}{\newton} against the \SI{20}{\newton} the spin pulls~\cite{kevlar_technical_guide,katz2026aim}. The wires are long enough that the spin holds the electronics outside the target vehicle's diameter, so the electronics pass it by and only the wires cross the plate (\autoref{fig:tethered_necklace}). The wires run centimeters apart, so freeing the electronics takes a separate micrometeoroid cut on every wire, about once in \num{e11} over a sixteen-day coast~\cite{forward_hoyt1995hoytether,katz2026aim}. Thin wires also load the plate less. Each strikes it as a narrow line of low areal density, and a locally thicker ablative coating where the wires will hit protects the plate from them~\cite{katz2026aim}. The working mass is a necklace of spheres, oriented to explode into a disk of gas ahead of the pusher plate, the shape the plate couples to best~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026,katz2026aim}. Each sphere is a bag of water around an inner bag of explosive emulsion, shaped to detonate into that disk. Each sphere carries a clock that fires a small primary charge. A one-gram ice bullet fired by the target vehicle can also set it off. Its shock reaches the emulsion at about \SI{2}{\giga\pascal}, twice what sets off a sensitive secondary explosive~\cite{souers_thresholds,katz2026aim}.

    \label{fig:tethered_necklace}

The spin axis points along the stream, so the Sun sits \SIrange{44}{50}{\degree} off it for the whole coast. Side heating across the spin's effective gravity drives a slow convective loop that evens out each sphere's temperature. A pattern of high- and low-emittance patches sets the mean~\cite{katz2026aim} (\autoref{fig:sidewall_convection}).

Sunlight arrives at about \ang{45} to the axis, so over each turn the sunward cap is warmed and the shaded cap is not. The spin's effective gravity points away from the axis, across that temperature difference. Water heated from the side has no threshold to cross, so it turns over in a slow loop around the inner bag.}
    \label{fig:sidewall_convection}
\end{figure}

Each sphere sits inside a multilayer blanket of the aluminum--Mylar--aluminum laminate that Echo~II flew~\cite{nasa_echo_ii_1966}. The blanket stays folded inside the deployment rocket until release. It is then stretched over the interior bags at a standoff of about a centimeter. Stretching yields the laminate's aluminum, so the blanket holds its shape without gas pressure, as Echo~II did~\cite{nasa_echo_ii_1966,katz2026aim}. The emittance pattern is printed on its outer sheet. The blanket is also a Whipple shield, a thin sacrificial layer that breaks up a particle before it reaches the wall behind~\cite{whipple_shield}. NASA's shield-design rule sets the bumper mass that shatters a grain of given size and density~\cite{jsc_mmod_handbook}. By that rule, a grain smaller than about \SI{185}{\micro\meter} shatters on the laminate's \SI{37}{\gram\per\square\meter}, and its debris spreads out before it reaches the bag~\cite{jsc_mmod_handbook,katz2026aim} (\autoref{fig:sphere_layers}a). Each further sheet shocks the debris cloud again~\cite{cour_palais1990multishock}.

Larger micrometeoroids still punch through, so some PuffSats will be lost. Grün's flux model puts a grain above \SI{185}{\micro\meter} into about one \SI{10}{\kilogram} sphere in 170 over a sixteen-day coast~\cite{grun1985collisional,katz2026aim}. A loss found early costs little, because the formation adjusts and a spare moves into the empty slot~\cite{katz2026aim}. A loss found too close to impact for that falls to the target vehicle. It makes up the missing momentum with an unplanned burn from a propellant reserve, which keeps it on the trajectory the remaining PuffSats are aimed at. That loss rate sizes the reserve~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Each PuffSat also flies through gas left by the pulses before it. At closing speeds near \SI{60}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, even this thin gas heats the sphere. It needs no heat shield, because the dose arrives late (\autoref{fig:plume_timeline}). The blanket and the bag may fail in the last few hundred milliseconds, at no cost. A freed sphere drifts only \SI{9}{\centi\meter} outward before impact, and the necklace's center stays put (\autoref{fig:tethered_necklace})~\cite{katz2026aim}. The gas is too thin to steer the sphere. In a ballistic model, drag moves its landing point on the plate by under \SI{20}{\micro\meter}~\cite{katz2026aim}. The wires fare worse. They lose their strength about \SI{240}{\milli\second} before impact, before the charges fire~\cite{kevlar_technical_guide,katz2026aim}, so nothing they carry may be needed once the gas arrives.

The curve is sketched through those quoted points, not computed. The Kevlar wires lose their strength about \SI{240}{\milli\second} before impact, before the charges fire.     \label{fig:plume_timeline}

Blanket shreds that survive the gas must still cross the water the plate sprays ahead of itself. That spray is sized to stop the whole dispersed PuffSat, with five to ten times its mass over the same footprint~\cite{Katz_water_injected_plate_2026}. A fragment loses half its speed each time it sweeps up about its own mass per unit area of gas. The blanket is a laminate of about \SI{37}{\gram\per\square\meter}~\cite{nasa_echo_ii_1966}, far less than the spray over the same area, so a shred slows many times over inside the spray rather than at the plate. Stopping it turns its kinetic energy, about \SI{1.8}{\giga\joule\per\kilogram} at \SI{60}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, into heat in the shocked gas around it. The fragment vaporizes if it keeps a few percent of that heat. Fragment survival through the spray has not been computed, so how much solid reaches the plate is not yet known.

Closed-cell foam, possibly polyethylene, fills the outer bag~\cite{rodriguez_perez2005_polyolefin_foams}. The foam's acoustic impedance is far below water's, so the blast cavitates the water at the foam strands~\cite{cole1948underwater} (\autoref{fig:sphere_layers}b). Gas held in crevices on the foam's surface seeds the cavitation~\cite{atchley1989crevice}. This spreads the blast through the bag and breaks the water into small droplets. The droplets then vaporize as they mix with propellant released by the pusher plate~\cite{Katz_water_injected_plate_2026,katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Nozzle PuffSats}\label{sec:nozzle_puffsats}
A PuffSat bound for a pulsed nozzle keeps the tethered electronics of \autoref{sec:pusher_plate_puffsats}, so its electronics again pass outside the target vehicle. Its working mass changes. It must enter the nozzle's chamber through a narrow port, so it is a long, thin, solid rod rather than a necklace of bags. A \SI{2.5}{\kilogram} polyethylene rod ten times as long as it is wide is \SI{0.69}{\meter} long and \SI{6.9}{\centi\meter} across~\cite{crc_handbook,katz2026aim}. The port is \SI{10.9}{\centi\meter} across, which leaves \SI{2}{\centi\meter} of clearance all round (\autoref{fig:rod_port_plug}). A larger rod would be easier to aim, but its chamber would outweigh the vehicle (\autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}).

That clearance shapes the rod. The pusher plate needed a disk of gas, and an explosive to make one. The chamber confines the gas itself, so the rod carries no explosive and only has to arrive. It carries no liquid either, because liquid sloshes in its bag and moves the working mass away from where the tethers hold it. A solid rod stays centered in the circle its spinning electronics packages trace. \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation} takes up how the rod is steered into the port. The rod is metallized under a clear coat, and its spin averages the sunlight over its surface. It sits near \SI{299}{\kelvin}, where polyethylene holds its shape~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Inside the chamber the rod strikes a plug. The chamber's gas alone is too thin to stop it, and the rod could reach the far wall intact. A projectile erodes into a target over about its own length times the square root of their density ratio~\cite{birkhoff1948lined}, and that sets the plug's depth. The plug is polyethylene foam at \SI{475}{\kilogram\per\cubic\meter}, half the density of the solid, inside a solid polyethylene skin. It is \SI{0.98}{\meter} deep and as wide as the port, \SI{10.9}{\centi\meter}, and it masses \SI{4.4}{\kilogram}. That width lets the rod strike up to \SI{3.5}{\centi\meter} off center. A shock that crushes a porous solid does extra work closing the voids, so the plug ends hotter and more of it joins the gas as vapor~\cite{zeldovich_raizer,katz2026aim}. Foam needs no cryogenics and cannot boil off while it waits. The penetration law was derived for dense targets, so the foam plug's depth is an extrapolation that needs a shock-physics simulation.

The plug does not sit at the port, or the blast would destroy the port. It stands one chamber radius inside, \SI{1.68}{\meter}, with its front face near the chamber's center. The plug outweighs the rod, so after impact the fireball's rear runs back toward the port at about \SI{9}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. Its pressure falls as the cube of the distance, from tens of gigapascals at \SI{0.3}{\meter} to about \SI{0.2}{\giga\pascal} at the plug's stand-off. There the port sees no more than the rest of the wall~\cite{katz2026aim}. That load lasts microseconds, but it is four times the peak the shell is sized for, and the shell's response has not been checked. The rod crosses the charge on its way in. The gas erodes about \SI{15}{\percent} of its length, into the charge where the energy is wanted, and cannot turn it~\cite{katz2026aim}. Polyethylene wires run through the plug's axis. They tie it to poles on the outer face of the sliding door and to winches outside the shell near the throat.

The port is cut in a sliding door in the ship's face, which trims out the last error in the rod's position. Trackers on the ship follow the incoming rod and place it to about \SI{1}{\centi\meter} at \SI{7}{\kilo\meter}, a tenth of a second before arrival. The door moves the port onto the predicted intercept at up to \SI{10}{g}, and the winches pay their wires in and out so the plug follows it. The door locks \SI{20}{\milli\second} before arrival. The pulse then presses the door into its backing, so friction holds it without a strong latch~\cite{katz2026aim}. \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation} takes up how the rod is steered close enough for the door to meet it. We assume here that, once the door has moved, the rod passes within the \SI{2}{\centi\meter} clearance.

Everything the rod and plug bring into the chamber leaves through the nozzle as exhaust. The wall caps the gas temperature, and at a fixed temperature exhaust speed falls as the inverse square root of the gas's mean particle mass. The materials are therefore chosen from light, non-toxic elements, hydrogen and carbon in the rod, the plug and the chamber's charge. Methane also adds no oxygen, which would burn a carbon hot face into carbon monoxide. The polyethylene plug raises a methane charge's mean particle mass by \SI{1.8}{\percent}, which costs about \SI{0.9}{\percent} of exhaust speed~\cite{katz2026aim}. It costs a hydrogen charge more, since every carbon atom is heavy against hydrogen. The \SI{2}{\centi\meter} clearance, the plug's size and its stand-off are requirements rather than results. The rod's motion on its tethers has not been simulated.

\section{Jupiter Gravity-Assist Details}\label{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists}
PuffSats fly a trajectory that loops from Earth out to Jupiter and back, and each new departure must find Earth and Jupiter where the last one found them. That forces the round trip to last a whole number of Earth--Jupiter \textbf{synodic periods}, the \SI{1.092}{\year} Earth takes to lap Jupiter and restore the same alignment~\cite{katz2026aim}. Three synodic periods (\SI{3.28}{\year}) and two (\SI{2.18}{\year}) both close, and \autoref{fig:jupiter_loop} draws both loops. A third option flies part of the outbound leg faster and parks it near Earth. If the departure burn is methalox, only the three-synodic cycle is practical. The other two need the higher specific impulse that PuffSat nozzles may reach (\autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}). The trajectory numbers below come from an eleven-cycle chain flown on real planetary ephemerides in the companion code repository~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}, and the parent paper works through them~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Three Synodic Periods}\label{sec:three_synodic_cadence}
Each returning batch of PuffSats splits into two waves. One wave cannot do both jobs. A pusher plate is pushed the way the PuffSats travel, and they return retrograde, against Earth's orbital motion (\autoref{fig:jupiter_loop}). The payload must leave for Jupiter prograde. So the growth wave lifts it the wrong way, and a second wave, met head-on, sends it the right way. The \textbf{growth wave} arrives first and pushes a payload lifted from the ground into a highly eccentric Earth orbit. It meets the payload at \SI{400}{\kilo\meter}, where the air still leaves its tethers cool~\cite{katz2026aim}. The chain flies a ten-day head start, which is the period of that orbit. The launch-unit ledger of \autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth} flies a twenty-day one, and the burns below are priced on it. Flown consistently, the twenty-day orbit is 0 to 2\% faster for the PuffSat chambers and 9\% faster for methalox~\cite{katz2026aim,Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. The \textbf{departure wave} arrives one orbit later and supplies the burn that sends the payload back toward Jupiter.

Between the two waves the payload has to turn around. The growth wave pushed it retrograde, the way its PuffSats travel, and the departure wave arrives from roughly the opposite direction. So the payload coasts to apoapsis, the orbit's far point, and burns there. At \SI{613000}{\kilo\meter} from Earth it moves at only about \SI{0.12}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so turning is cheap. It then returns to periapsis still moving at nearly escape speed, so the growth push is not lost to the turn. The departure burn starts where the payload is fastest, which is where the Oberth effect makes each unit of $\deltav$ buy the most energy. The same burn lifts its periapsis, the orbit's closest point, to \SI{600}{\kilo\meter}. The growth push had to maximize what reaches orbit on the first lift. The departure leg does not, so it passes higher, where drag cannot disturb the steered PuffSats. The whole reversal costs about 6\% of the departing mass in carried propellant~\cite{katz2026aim}. Panel (a) of \autoref{fig:earth_turnaround} draws the sequence.

The departure wave then meets the payload almost head-on at periapsis. In the payload's frame, the PuffSats' velocity makes an angle of \ang{150.4} with the thrust, where \ang{180} would be exactly head-on. Treating the collision as head-on misstates the delivered mass by 0.9\%~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. Because the payload arrives at periapsis already near escape speed, the four three-synodic cycles in the chain need a burn of only \SIrange{5.32}{5.54}{\kilo\meter\per\second}~\cite{katz2026aim}. That figure is at the parent paper's \SI{200}{\kilo\meter} reference altitude. At \SI{600}{\kilo\meter} each burn needs \SIrange{107}{110}{\meter\per\second} more~\cite{katz2026aim}. A methalox stage at \SI{380}{\second} of specific impulse has an exhaust speed of \SI{3.73}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so it keeps $e^{-\deltav/3.73}$ of the vehicle, or 23 to 24\%. That is expensive but workable. The PuffSats come home at \SIrange{56.53}{57.43}{\kilo\meter\per\second} relative to Earth~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Two Synodic Periods}\label{sec:two_synodic_cadence}
The shorter loop needs a harder turn at Jupiter, so the flyby passes much closer (\autoref{fig:jupiter_loop}). The tightest passes sit at the \SI{4000}{\kilo\meter} altitude floor the chain allows, \num{1.056} Jupiter radii from the planet's center~\cite{katz2026aim}. They pass deeper into the radiation belts that \autoref{sec:deployment_rocket} shields against. In a model with both planets on circular orbits in one plane, a two-synodic cycle always closes~\cite{katz2026aim}. Real orbits are eccentric and inclined, and there Jupiter sometimes cannot bend the path far enough. The departure wave then needs a \textbf{deep-space maneuver}, a burn made in mid-coast rather than at a planet. The chain takes the two-synodic return whenever that maneuver stays under \SI{50}{\meter\per\second} and falls back to three synodic periods otherwise. Four of its eleven cycles fell back. Over two hundred years of real Earth--Jupiter geometry, only 45 of 91 two-synodic windows clear the altitude floor~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The mechanics repeat those of \autoref{sec:three_synodic_cadence}. The departure wave arrives at \ang{160.6} to the thrust, even closer to head-on, and treating it as head-on costs 0.5\%~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. The burn is larger, \SIrange{6.84}{7.17}{\kilo\meter\per\second} across the seven two-synodic cycles~\cite{katz2026aim}. On methalox that keeps 15 to 16\% of the vehicle, which is marginal. The largest burn shows what the Oberth effect buys. It leaves the payload \SI{14.39}{\kilo\meter\per\second} beyond Earth's pull, twice the burn~\cite{katz2026aim}. The PuffSats return faster, at \SIrange{61.83}{65.13}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so they push harder on the next outbound leg~\cite{katz2026aim}.

That burn needs an engine with a higher specific impulse than methalox. PuffSat nozzles (\autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}) supply it. A nuclear thermal rocket could make the head-on burn instead, though it does better on the three-synodic cycle (\autoref{sec:ntr_departure}).

\subsection{Fly And Park}\label{sec:fly_and_park_cadence}
The third option sends the outbound leg on two trajectories. The departure wave keeps the three-synodic clock. The growth wave flies a shorter, faster route and arrives months early. It pushes the payload into an eccentric orbit, and the payload waits there for the departure wave. We call this \textbf{fly-and-park}, following the parent paper~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Left on the twenty-day orbit, the parked payload would dip through the Van Allen radiation belts on every pass~\cite{li2019vanallen}. So a burn at apoapsis raises its periapsis above the outer belt, to about \SI{60000}{\kilo\meter} from Earth's center, for \SI{0.22}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. Before the departure wave arrives, a second apoapsis burn lowers the periapsis to \SI{600}{\kilo\meter}. The same burn turns the orbit so that the payload meets the incoming PuffSats nearly head-on. It costs \SIrange{0.22}{0.46}{\kilo\meter\per\second} depending on the turn. The park therefore adds \SIrange{0.44}{0.68}{\kilo\meter\per\second} in all~\cite{katz2026aim}. Panel (b) of \autoref{fig:earth_turnaround} draws the park. A park that lasts months has time to pay that with solar-electric engines or a lunar flyby.

The park buys two things. The growth wave need not arrive all at once, because each payload can wait in the park for the departure wave. The ground launches it pushes can then be spread over months rather than crowded into the days around a single wave. The faster route also returns PuffSats at about \SI{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so each one pushes more mass outbound. The best case returns 1.27 times the mass of a plain three-synodic cycle~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The faster route costs a larger departure burn, a median \SI{1.04}{\kilo\meter\per\second} more. On methalox that misses breaking even by about 5\%, and fly-and-park beats the plain three-synodic cycle at only 1 of 11 departure phases. At \SI{1200}{\second} of specific impulse it wins at 16 of 30, and at \SI{2214}{\second} it wins at 25 of 30~\cite{katz2026aim}. Those specific impulses are therefore requirements for the PuffSat nozzles of \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}, not results.

\section{Pusher Plates}\label{sec:pusher_plates}
A pusher plate works as Project Orion's did. Gas strikes a plate at the back of the vehicle and pushes it forward~\cite{projorion}. The plate pushes only the way the gas travels, so it suits the overtake, where PuffSats catch the vehicle from behind and their momentum already points forward. Met head-on, a plate would reverse that momentum and brake the vehicle. That is why the departure burn uses the nozzles of \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Spraying Water Into The Pulse}\label{sec:plate_water_spray}
Our plate differs from Orion's in the way that sets its performance. The vehicle sprays its own water into the arriving PuffSat gas ahead of the plate (\autoref{fig:plate_stack})~\cite{Katz_water_injected_plate_2026,katz2026aim}. Call the carried water the \textbf{slug}, and write $k$ for the slug ratio, the kilograms of slug per kilogram of arriving PuffSat, and $w$ for the closing speed. A PuffSat of mass $m$ that bounced perfectly off a bare plate would deliver an impulse of $2mw$. With the water added, the mixture's center of mass approaches the plate at only $w/(1+k)$, but the collision has heated it. The hot gas expands off the plate as well as bouncing, and the two motions add as energies rather than as speeds. If all the incoming kinetic energy leaves as backward exhaust at one speed, that speed is $w/\sqrt{1+k}$ and the impulse is~\cite{katz2026aim}
\begin{equation}
    J_{\max} = mw\left(1+\sqrt{1+k}\right).
    \label{eq:plate_ceiling}
\end{equation}
At $k=8.5$, the parent paper's working figure, the ceiling is $4.1\,mw$, about twice a bare bounce~\cite{katz2026aim}. The exhaust leaves at about a third of the closing speed, so the exhaust's specific impulse falls. That trade is worth making because the scarce mass is the PuffSat returning from Jupiter, not water lifted from the ground. The value $k=8.5$ comes from the parent's launch-mass budget rather than from optimizing the plate~\cite{katz2026aim}.

No real plate reaches the ceiling. Some gas spills past the rim, and some energy leaves as radiation. The parent writes the delivered impulse as $mw(\eta_{\mathrm{jet}}\sqrt{1+k}+1)$, where the jet efficiency $\eta_{\mathrm{jet}}$ collects those losses. Its performance target of $\eta_{\mathrm{jet}}=0.775$ gives $3.39\,mw$ per PuffSat~\cite{katz2026aim}. That target is a requirement, not a result.

Water is the most convenient spray, because it is cheap and stores without refrigeration. The collision heats the mixture enough to split water into hydrogen and oxygen, and energy held in broken bonds pushes nothing. We assume that energy comes back. The gas is still dense as it cools, and recombination needs three bodies to meet, so its rate per molecule rises as the square of the density. Atoms that recombine while the gas still presses on the plate return their bond energy to the push. This is an assumption. The parent's finite-rate calculation, at the slowest closing speed of \SI{45.58}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and $k=8.5$, returned 23 to 31\% of the stored bond energy by \SI{250}{\micro\second}~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026,katz2026aim}. That calculation layered the water against a flat wall. Mixing ahead of the plate changes how the gas cools, and which way it moves the return has not been computed.

Argon is the alternative~\cite{katz2026aim}. A single atom has no bonds to break, and argon is harmless in the atmosphere. It does ionize, so it trades one assumption for another, since its ions must recombine while the gas still presses on the plate. The parent's growth ledger takes that assumption and sprays argon. It charges water as if its bonds never re-form, and on that charge argon doubles the fleet 8 to 11\% faster (\autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth}). It costs more to handle, because it is carried as liquid argon. It also runs hotter at the plate. Temperature measures energy per particle, while the closing speed fixes energy per kilogram. Split water averages 6 atomic mass units per particle, since each molecule becomes two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen, and an argon atom has 40. A kilogram of argon therefore shares the same energy among about a sixth as many particles~\cite{katz2026aim}. Each atom arrives carrying about six times the energy of a water fragment, as a bowling ball carries more than a tennis ball thrown at the same speed. When the gas stops against the plate, that energy becomes heat, and the argon ends hotter.

\subsection{A Thin Ablative Film}\label{sec:plate_ablative_film}
As on Orion, a liquid ablative film is sprayed onto the plate before each pulse, and the hot gas consumes it instead of the steel beneath~\cite{projorion,ga5009_vol3}. Orion's film was about \SI{150}{\micro\meter} thick, matched to bomb plasma arriving at \SIrange{100}{200}{\kilo\meter\per\second}~\cite{ga5009_vol3,katz2026aim}. The parent paper sets the thickness by opacity instead of by ablated mass. The film must hold enough dispersed carbon to stop the pulse's light before it reaches the metal. At a fifth carbon by mass, a \SIrange{1}{2}{\micro\meter} coat has an optical depth $\tau$ of 1.5 to 3 at \SI{550}{\nano\meter}~\cite{bond2006_lac_absorption,katz2026aim}. Optical depth is unitless, and a coat passes a fraction $e^{-\tau}$ of the light, so this one passes \SIrange{5}{22}{\percent}.

We take about \SI{5}{\micro\meter}, and the margin is not the main reason. Between pulses, the vehicle shines near-infrared light on the plate and measures what the metal reflects back through the film (\autoref{fig:plate_stack}). A thinner remaining film returns more light, so the image maps where the pulse wore the coat away, and the sprayer refills only those zones~\cite{katz2026aim}. Carbon absorbs less at longer wavelengths~\cite{bond2006_lac_absorption}, so infrared light can cross a film a few microns thick and return. Much thicker films return too little light to read. The \SI{5}{\micro\meter} figure is a requirement set by that readout, not a measured limit.

The thin film is an optimization, and the plate does not depend on it. The parent's reference plate is \SI{5}{\meter} across, sized for a vehicle like a small private jet in its 200-mile case~\cite{katz2026aim}. A data-center vehicle may need a larger plate, and this paper does not size one. The parent's \SI{25}{\square\meter} reference plate meets pulses of about \SI{100}{\kilogram}~\cite{katz2026aim}. A \SI{5}{\micro\meter} film on it is about \SI{125}{\gram} of liquid, near 0.1\% of the pulse. Orion's full \SI{150}{\micro\meter} film would be about \SI{4}{\kilogram}, or 4\%. A larger plate carries more film, but it also meets heavier pulses, so the film's share should hold as long as pulse mass grows with plate area. The thin film shows what modern sensing adds to Orion's scheme. Orion's more conservative film works too, at a few percent of each pulse.

\subsection{Pulse Rate And Plate Mass}\label{sec:plate_pulse_rate}
The gas each pulse leaves behind limits the pulse rate. The next PuffSat flies through it, and the dose on its surface rises as the square of the rate, from \SI{75.6}{\kilo\joule\per\square\meter} at \SI{2}{\hertz} to \SI{302}{\kilo\joule\per\square\meter} at \SI{4}{\hertz}~\cite{katz2026aim}. At \SI{4}{\hertz} the PuffSats survive it, because most of the dose arrives in the last \SI{0.3}{\second} (\autoref{sec:pusher_plate_puffsats}).

A higher rate buys a lighter plate. Each pulse kicks the plate, which rides back toward the vehicle on its shock absorbers until the next one. For a vehicle of mass $M$ and mean acceleration $a$, pulsed at rate $\nu$, the plate's mass $m$ and the stroke $s$ it travels satisfy $ms\sim Ma/\nu^2$~\cite{katz2026aim}. At a fixed stroke, plate mass falls as the square of the pulse rate. It can fall only to the mass the plate needs to survive each pulse, of order tens of kilograms per square meter. At \SI{4}{\hertz} and a stroke of a few meters, the dynamics hold the plate near a tenth of the vehicle's mass, well above that survival floor~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Aiming Between Pulses}\label{sec:plate_aiming}
Between pulses the vehicle must stay lined up on the stream, since it takes up the error the PuffSats share (\autoref{sec:guidance_navigation}). The plate can help it steer. The parent's impact simulation finds that the plate couples best when the gas arrives as a flat disk~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026}. The disk can be only so narrow. In the parent's geometry sweep at \SI{16}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, the best survivable footprint covered about half the plate's radius. Squeezing it to three tenths gained almost nothing and loaded the plate past its strength~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026,katz2026aim}.

Tilting the plate slightly sends the rebounding gas off axis, and its recoil pushes the vehicle sideways. On its own, that push would also turn the vehicle. Moving the impact point off the plate's center cancels the turn, because the total force then passes through the center of mass (\autoref{fig:plate_aiming})~\cite{katz2026aim}. Shifting mass inside the vehicle or firing its reaction-control thrusters can do the same job. Pulse strength gives a second control. The flattest disk bounces best, so firing a PuffSat's charges slightly early weakens that pulse. Thrusters on the vehicle make the remaining corrections. They are required when a PuffSat is lost too late for the formation to fill its slot (\autoref{sec:pusher_plate_puffsats}).

\subsection{Plate Construction}\label{sec:plate_construction}
The plate can be made of ordinary steel. Its stroke already sets its mass well above what survival needs, so a lighter metal saves nothing~\cite{katz2026aim}. Steel conducts heat better than titanium, so the same pulse heats a steel face less, and the face cracks less over thousands of pulses~\cite{katz2026aim}. The parent paper builds the plate from separate petals that are clamped rather than welded. A weld halves the stress swing steel survives for two million cycles~\cite{iiw_fatigue_2008}, and clamped petals leave each one room to grow as it heats~\cite{katz2026aim}. Most of a plate's cost is forming, machining, inspection, and latches rather than metal~\cite{katz2026aim}. Rolling and cutting steel plate already scales well, and unit costs generally fall as cumulative production grows, an effect first measured in aircraft~\cite{wright1936_airplane_cost}. How far the cost of plates would fall is speculation.

Behind the plate sit gas springs (\autoref{fig:plate_stack}), as on Orion, whose first stage was a set of toroidal pneumatic bags~\cite{projorion}. Modern fibers make the bag walls much lighter, because wall mass falls in proportion to the fiber's strength per unit density. Vectran HT breaks at \SI{3.2}{\giga\pascal} at a density of \SI{1.4}{\gram\per\cubic\centi\meter}, and a Vectran wall has a ninth the mass of a steel one~\cite{vectran_engineering_brochure,katz2026aim}. Vectran also resists creep, which keeps the springs tuned under a steady preload. It outlasted aramids in the maker's fold-endurance test~\cite{vectran_engineering_brochure}. It keeps half its strength only up to \SI{145}{\celsius}, so the bags sit behind ceramic felt, and Kevlar is the fallback if the back of the plate runs too hot~\cite{vectran_engineering_brochure,katz2026aim}.

A gas spring is nearly lossless. Each pulse reverses the plate's velocity symmetrically, so the spring passes momentum to the vehicle while dissipating almost no energy~\cite{katz2026aim}. With a plate a tenth of the vehicle, pulses at \SI{4}{\hertz}, and a mean of \SI{3}{g}, a tuned spring peaks at \SI{3.6}{g} over a \SI{2.5}{\meter} stroke~\cite{katz2026aim}. A crewed vehicle would need Orion's second stage to smooth the vibration. Uncrewed data-center hardware needs only a cap on peak load. A single stage meets the parent's \SI{10}{g} cap, which allows a mean near \SI{6.4}{g}~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Reusing The Plate}\label{sec:plate_reuse}
The first plates need not be reused. A reusable plate adds a replaceable facing of \SI{1}{\milli\meter} steel tiles under the ablative film (\autoref{fig:plate_stack})~\cite{katz2026aim}. The tiles take the particle damage, and each is inspected and replaced on its own between flights. On the parent's \SI{25}{\square\meter} reference plate, a full facing weighs \SI{196}{\kilogram}, about 6\% of the plate~\cite{katz2026aim}. A data-center plate would carry a heavier facing, and the share it adds depends on how that plate's own mass scales.

The tiles must not weld to the plate. Clean metal can cold-weld in vacuum, and fretting, the small repeated sliding of a loaded joint, raises the adhesion by breaking the surface oxide~\cite{merstallinger_cold_welding}. The parent's baseline is an interleaf of Vespel SP-3, a polyimide filled with molybdenum disulfide, which showed no adhesion to stainless steel in ESA's fretting tests~\cite{merstallinger_cold_welding,katz2026aim}. The interleaf may be ground to powder over many pulses, so it must not shed carcinogenic dust or fibers. An asbestos gasket would be ruled out. The constraint matters if people replace the tiles, and less if robots do.
\section{Pulsed Rocket Nozzles}\label{sec:rocket_nozzles}
The departure wave meets the payload nearly head-on (\autoref{sec:three_synodic_cadence}). A pusher plate would reverse that wave's momentum and brake the vehicle (\autoref{sec:pusher_plates}), so the departure burn needs a device that turns the collision's heat into thrust toward the incoming PuffSats. That device is a pulsed nozzle. Each rod of \autoref{sec:nozzle_puffsats} enters a chamber through a narrow port and turns to hot gas there, and the gas leaves through a throat at the back~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The wall sets the limit. A pusher plate meets a pulse for a moment, but in a chamber the hot gas presses against the wall until it has drained through the throat. That takes 25 to \SI{56}{\milli\second} per pulse~\cite{katz2026aim}, so the gas cannot be made arbitrarily hot. A magnetic nozzle, a shaped field that turns the plasma with no wall in contact, would lift that limit~\cite{ahedo2010magnetic}. It appears far from maturity. The published figures for its efficiency bound it from above rather than measure it. The measured impulses come from half-joule laser shots, and the two pendulum methods used to take them disagree~\cite{koba2025measurement,katz2026aim}. The field's structure also runs to tens of tonnes on the Jupiter pulses~\cite{katz2026aim}. We therefore take the nearer-term route of a material chamber.

A pulse heats the wall surface faster than any coolant can reach it. Over a \SI{40}{\milli\second} pulse, heat soaks only about \SI{2}{\milli\meter} into a copper wall, so passages behind it would see nothing until the pulse was over. The surface takes the pulse alone. Two pairings of gas and wall survive that. The companion simulation solves both for the \SI{2.5}{\kilogram} rod at \SI{75}{\kilo\meter\per\second} in a \SI{20}{\cubic\meter} chamber, with the plug of \autoref{sec:nozzle_puffsats}~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026}. Their temperatures are requirements that the simulation meets, not measurements.

Hydrogen at \SI{5500}{\kelvin} peaks at \SI{818}{\bar} and runs on a bare liner of GRCop-84, a copper alloy NASA developed for rocket chambers. Copper conducts the pulse's heat inward faster than it builds up at the surface, which peaks at \SIrange{370}{1110}{\kelvin}, below copper's \SI{1356}{\kelvin} melting point~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026}. Hydrogen must never meet a graphite coat. Hot hydrogen dissolves carbon, the corrosion that ate the graphite fuel of the NERVA reactors~\cite{katz2026aim}. Methane at \SI{7000}{\kelvin} peaks at \SI{496}{\bar}. It would put more heat into copper than its charge can carry away, so it runs on Cr--Mo steel under \SI{0.2}{\milli\meter} of sprayed pitch, the tar left from refining coal or petroleum. The pitch chars and ablates, as charring heat shields do~\cite{pica_ablator_milos2010}. It holds the steel at \SIrange{395}{441}{\kelvin} and loses \SIrange{1.4}{5.6}{\kilogram} a pulse, which joins the exhaust. A recoat of about \SI{0.1}{\milli\meter} keeps the layer standing~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026}.

Behind either liner, a carbon-fiber overwrap carries most of the pressure. The vessel is stretched once past the steel's yield before service, so the carbon holds the steel in compression. The wall's mass tracks the peak pressure times the volume it holds. Sized for hydrogen at \SI{900}{\bar}, the \SI{20}{\cubic\meter} chamber's wall is near \SI{35}{\tonne}, against \SI{130}{\tonne} for a \SI{10}{\kilogram} rod's chamber. At the near-term peaks it falls to about \SI{32}{\tonne} for hydrogen at \SI{818}{\bar}, and to about \SI{19}{\tonne} for methane at \SI{496}{\bar}, which needs no copper liner~\cite{katz2026aim}.

No coolant passages are needed. After each pulse the ship sprays the next charge, liquid methane or liquid hydrogen, over the wall. The spray takes back the heat the wall kept and carries it into the chamber as part of the next pulse. Warming the \SI{68.8}{\kilogram} methane charge to \SI{500}{\kelvin} absorbs about \SI{100}{\mega\joule}, two to four times the \SIrange{28}{50}{\mega\joule} the pitch keeps. Warming the \SI{59.9}{\kilogram} hydrogen charge to room temperature absorbs about \SI{230}{\mega\joule}. That covers the heat copper keeps at the low and middle edges of the flux, but at the \SI{350}{\mega\joule} high edge the hydrogen must leave warmer~\cite{katz2026aim}. Liquid that meets a surface far above its boiling point can float on its own vapor and cool it poorly, so the spray rate is not assured. The warmed charge fills the chamber as gas. Thin films over both openings hold it in. The arriving rod punctures the entrance film, and the pulse bursts the throat film (\autoref{fig:chamber_cooling}).

Part of each pulse would leak back out the entrance. Left open, the port costs about \SI{10.5}{\percent} of thrust, and the pulse can close it~\cite{katz2026aim}. Two leaves sit in a slot through the sliding door, one on each side of the port, as in a knife-gate valve. A tap beside the port feeds chamber gas to a piston behind each leaf. The gate needs no timing signal, since it cannot start before the pulse, and by then the rod has passed. The leaves meet in \SIrange{2}{3}{\milli\second} and leave a gap of up to \SI{1}{\milli\meter}, which brings the leak to about \SI{1}{\percent}. Springs reopen them once the chamber falls below about \SI{25}{\bar}, \SIrange{115}{150}{\milli\second} after impact~\cite{katz2026aim}. The gate also thins the plume the next rod flies through, because only the gas that escapes before the leaves meet lies in its path.

The expansion ratio sets how much of each pulse comes back. At these temperatures much of the gas's energy is stored in broken molecules. It returns as thrust only if they recombine as the gas expands. Recombination needs three particles to meet, so its rate climbs steeply with density, and high chamber pressure buys it. Hydrogen's chemistry is done near an area ratio of 30, the exit area over the throat area, with \SI{91}{\percent} of its bond energy back. It keeps gaining after that, because most of its energy is heat rather than bonds. Methane's hydrogen atoms stop rejoining near an area ratio of 100, with only about half its bond energy back, and its carbon leaves still bound in heavy fragments~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026}. We take an area ratio of 300 for both. The exit is then \SIrange{7.5}{8.7}{\meter} across, in family with the 285:1 carbon--carbon extension the RL10B-2 upper stage deploys~\cite{lacoste2002cc_nozzles}. Methane needs it most. At an area ratio of 14 it falls behind the reactor of \autoref{sec:ntr_departure} even with the gate. Hydrogen's extension needs a material that hydrogen does not attack, and it has not been sized.

Hydrogen wins on the numbers, by about \SI{340}{\second} and a quarter of a year of doubling. Its tanks are already charged. Per pulse, hydrogen's tank masses about \SI{12}{\kilogram}, while methane's tank and lost pitch together mass 4 to \SI{8}{\kilogram}~\cite{katz2026aim}. We still propose methane as the near-term chamber, and we expect it to win on cost in practice. The costs the table leaves out all fall on hydrogen. The methane wall is a steel shell and a sprayed coat, with no copper liner to machine and bond. Between pulses the steel sits in methane rather than hydrogen, which sidesteps most of the concern over hydrogen embrittlement. Methane boils at \SI{112}{\kelvin} rather than \SI{20}{\kelvin}. A tank radiating to space can hold it near zero boil-off, without the cryogenic hardware hydrogen needs~\cite{katz2026aim}. Methane is also the cheaper fuel. The methane charge costs about \$13 of natural gas, and the hydrogen charge \$72 to \$94 before it is liquefied~\cite{doe2012_h2_low_cost_ng,eia2026_henry_hub_2025}. Over a departure's 630 to 780 pulses the gap is \$0.04 to \$0.06 million. That is a tenth to a sixth of the \$0.4 million the chamber costs per departure, so the purchase price does not decide the choice~\cite{katz2026aim}. Handling, boil-off and embrittlement are not priced here, so the choice is a judgment rather than a result. Hydrogen on copper is the faster chamber once hydrogen handling is in hand.

The chamber is also the moving mass of the shock absorber. A departure that carries only PuffSats has no crew to protect, and PuffSats can ride 10 to \SI{20}{g}. That allows a jerky ride, which the parent paper calls burst and coast~\cite{katz2026aim}. Each pulse drives the chamber back against a gas spring. A latch catches it at full compression, and a damper bleeds off the stored energy before the next rod arrives. Burst and coast does not make the absorber lighter than smooth running. The lighter methane chamber recoils faster, so its absorber stores 1.8 times what a \SI{35}{\tonne} chamber's would~\cite{katz2026aim}. What it buys is control. Each pulse starts with the chamber at rest, so the port sits where the sliding door expects it. The ship measures each pulse's impulse and trims the next charge to correct it~\cite{katz2026aim}.

It also buys time. At \SI{4}{\hertz} the gate reopens \SIrange{115}{150}{\milli\second} after impact and the door locks at \SI{230}{\milli\second}. That leaves about a tenth of a second to reload a plug and its wires, which has not been designed~\cite{katz2026aim}. At \SI{2}{\hertz} the reload gets about a third of a second more. The burn doubles, from \SIrange{159}{194}{\second} to about \SIrange{320}{390}{\second}, for a craft of \SI{100}{\tonne} that spends half its mass on propellant. The loss from burning in a fixed direction grows about as the square of the burn time. Scaled from the parent's \SI{27}{\meter\per\second} over \SI{320}{\second}, the three-synodic departure loses \SIrange{27}{40}{\meter\per\second} at \SI{2}{\hertz} against 7 to \SI{10}{\meter\per\second} at \SI{4}{\hertz}~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Two unknowns set how well the chamber competes with the reactor of \autoref{sec:ntr_departure}. The first is the chamber itself, which leaves on an escape trajectory. PuffSats could brake it and bring it back for reuse, at a cost in PuffSats that has not been priced. At the \SI{2.5}{\kilogram} rod's \SIrange{19}{32}{\tonne} wall it can instead be expended. The ledger of \autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth} throws one away every cycle and still beats methalox, but it does not price building a new chamber each time~\cite{katz2026aim}. Coupling has not been measured either. The simulation's losses are modeled, not tested~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\section{A Nuclear Thermal Departure}\label{sec:ntr_departure}
The head-on departure burn can also be made by a nuclear thermal rocket, a fission reactor that heats hydrogen and expels it through an ordinary nozzle. Nothing collides, so there is no coupling problem to solve, and the engine's performance rests on ground tests rather than a simulation. The Rover and NERVA programs tested 22 reactors in the 1960s, though none has flown~\cite{nasem2021nuclear}. NASA's Mars reference engine is carried at \SI{906}{\second} of specific impulse and \SI{111}{\kilo\newton} of thrust~\cite{borowski2013ntp,katz2026aim}. A reactor needs no projectiles, so the departure wave goes away and every returning PuffSat can push~\cite{katz2026aim}. NASA has made space fission a priority. The reactor it announced in 2026, SR-1 Freedom, powers electric thrusters rather than heating propellant. The national initiative announced with it keeps thermal rockets in scope and directs work on components common to both~\cite{ans2026_sr1,smith2026_nuclear_initiative}.

We require low-enriched fuel, below 20\% uranium-235. United States policy allows highly enriched uranium only when a mission would not be viable with other fuels~\cite{spd6_2020}, and low-enriched fuel keeps the launch in the lower approval tier described below. It costs mass. NASA's reference engine assumes a 93\% enriched core~\cite{borowski2013ntp}, while General Atomics caps its low-enriched reactor at \SI{3500}{\kilogram} for \SI{57}{\kilo\newton}~\cite{ga_ntp}. No nuclear thermal core of this fuel has been tested~\cite{nasem2021nuclear}. The reactor's thrust is also lower than the chamber's. Enough engines to finish the burn in the chamber's five minutes would weigh 37\% of the stack, so the best burn lasts 42 to 53 minutes instead. Much of it happens away from periapsis, where the Oberth effect gives less, and that costs about \SI{600}{\meter\per\second}. The reactor therefore does best on the three-synodic cycle, whose \SI{5.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second} burn is the smaller one. With its engines and hydrogen tanks expended every cycle, a low-enriched reactor doubles the fleet in 2.15 to \SI{2.53}{\year}, depending on tank mass. Methalox on the same model takes \SI{3.64}{\year}~\cite{katz2026aim}. Those figures do not yet charge the cooldown hydrogen or the full kick allowance described below, which lengthen the doubling by about 5\% and 1.2\%~\cite{katz2026aim}. Whether the pulsed chamber beats the reactor turns on what it costs to bring the chamber back after each burn and on how well a real pulse couples. \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles} takes up both.

The reactor lights only once the stack has escaped Earth, so it never goes critical on a bound orbit. At its \SI{600}{\kilo\meter} periapsis the stack is \SI{60}{\meter\per\second} short of escape. The burn is centered on periapsis, though, so ignition comes 21 to 26 minutes earlier, where the stack moves at only 7.7 to \SI{8.3}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. There each meter per second adds less orbital energy, and a methalox kick needs 75 to \SI{80}{\meter\per\second} to reach escape. Above that floor the kick's size is a safety choice. Each extra meter per second leaves the stack further past escape if it explodes early in the burn. We carry an allowance of \SI{150}{\meter\per\second}, 3.9\% of the stack in methalox, and leave the exact size to the safety review~\cite{katz2026aim}. Before ignition the core is fresh uranium, which is barely radioactive. If the kick fails, the reactor stays cold, and a fresh core on a bound orbit is no worse than one on the launch pad. That holds only if a cold core cannot go critical by accident. The United Nations principles ask that a reactor stay subcritical through ``rocket explosion, re-entry, impact on ground or water, submersion in water or water intruding into the core''~\cite{un1992nps}, and we adopt that as a design requirement.

In nominal operation the spent stage falls into Jupiter. The burn aims the whole stack at the planet. After separation the payload, not the stage, makes a correction of \SIrange{2.3}{10}{\meter\per\second} that moves it out to its flyby. The stage fires nothing after the burn, so its safe outcome needs no second device to work. If the payload's correction fails, the payload falls into Jupiter too~\cite{katz2026aim}. Jupiter's gravity widens the target to a capture radius of about \SI{265000}{\kilo\meter}. On circular, coplanar orbits, the burn can end about \SI{6}{\meter\per\second} off along its direction and the stage still hits~\cite{katz2026aim}. The engine's cooldown is the largest threat to that margin. After shutdown the core keeps making heat as its fission products decay, about \SI{8}{\mega\watt} a minute later and 0.7 to \SI{0.8}{\mega\watt} after an hour~\cite{way1948decay,katz2026aim}. The engine has no radiator, so it keeps pumping hydrogen through the core for hours. NASA's Mars studies budget that cooldown hydrogen at 3\% of the usable propellant~\cite{borowski2012neo}. Leaving through the nozzle, it would push the spent stage 90 to \SI{150}{\meter\per\second} off its aim. We therefore vent it through opposed nozzles, so that their thrusts cancel. No nuclear thermal rocket has flown such a vent, so it is a proposal~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Galileo and Cassini ended the same way. NASA steered Galileo into Jupiter in 2003, with two plutonium-powered generators aboard, so that it could never strike Europa~\cite{nasa_galileo}. Cassini carried three plutonium-238 generators~\cite{nasa_cassini_rtg} and was sent into Saturn in 2017 so that it could not contaminate Enceladus or Titan~\cite{nasa_cassini_finale}. The international planetary protection policy puts Jupiter in its Category II, bodies where ``there is only a remote chance that contamination carried by a spacecraft could compromise future investigations''~\cite{cospar_pp_moon}. The Outer Space Treaty asks states to explore celestial bodies ``so as to avoid their harmful contamination'' and does not define the term~\cite{outer_space_treaty}. We found no ruling on sending a fission reactor into a gas giant. Both precedents carried radioisotope sources, and a spent core carries fission products instead. We assume the treaty permits it, as it permitted Galileo and Cassini, but no agency has said so.

Two accidents remain~\cite{katz2026aim}. The first is a burn that stops early, an ordinary engine failure. The stage is then past escape but short of Jupiter, on a solar orbit that crosses Earth's. It needs more than a year to come back, and more likely years to decades. The chance of a hit on any one pass has not been computed. A full burn leaves 100 to 140 curies of caesium-137 and strontium-90 in each engine. Counting the shorter-lived products, a core stopped late in its burn holds about 3,200 curies a year after shutdown and 200 after ten~\cite{katz2026aim}. For scale, the Chernobyl accident released 30,000 to 40,000 times one engine's caesium-137~\cite{unscear2000annexj,katz2026aim}. In 1964 the SNAP-9A generator of a Transit satellite burned up in the upper atmosphere and spread \num{17000} curies of plutonium-238 around the world~\cite{hardy1972snap9a}. A returning stage would be a local hazard rather than a global one. Much of it would burn up high in the atmosphere, and pieces could reach the ground, as some of the Soviet reactor satellite Cosmos 954 did over northern Canada in 1978~\cite{healthcanada_cosmos954,katz2026aim}.

The second accident is an explosion early in the burn. With the full allowance the stack is 70 to \SI{75}{\meter\per\second} past escape at ignition, and a fragment thrown backward faster than that stays bound to Earth. NASA's standard breakup model, fitted to explosions of rocket stages and satellites in orbit, puts the median fragment speed at 36 to \SI{56}{\meter\per\second}~\cite{johnson2001breakup}. Drawn from that model, 4 to 8\% of fragments stay bound after an explosion at ignition. The burn keeps adding speed, so the share falls to 0.5 to 2.3\% a minute in and to almost none after five minutes~\cite{katz2026aim}. The fission inventory grows the other way, from almost nothing at ignition, so the hazard sits in the first minute or two of the burn. This is the accident closest to this paper's subject. Bound fragments pass through low Earth orbit at every perigee, and most climb past the high orbits of \autoref{sec:data_center_attacks} on the way out. Where they end up after the Moon and Sun reshape their orbits has not been computed~\cite{katz2026aim}.

In the United States, launching the reactor would need authorization under the 2019 presidential memorandum on space nuclear systems, NSPM-20. Its stated policy is that the country ``shall develop and use space nuclear systems when such systems safely enable or enhance space exploration or operational capabilities.'' A low-enriched reactor falls in its second tier. The head of the sponsoring agency authorizes that launch after an interagency nuclear safety review. It moves to the third tier if the safety analysis puts the chance of exposing anyone to more than 25 rem at one in a million or more. A third-tier launch needs the President or the White House science office. A rem is a unit of radiation dose, and 100 rem is one sievert. The analysis covers accidents after launch, ``for instance, in an unplanned reentry from Earth orbit or during an Earth flyby''~\cite{nspm20_2019}. NASA's nuclear flight safety handbook adds that an accidental disposal caused by an operational failure should still meet the mission's tier~\cite{nasa_hdbk_8715_26}. A burn that stops short of Jupiter is that case. The two accidents above are therefore what the review would score, and how often a burn stops early or explodes has not been computed~\cite{katz2026aim}.
\section{Guidance And Navigation}\label{sec:guidance_navigation}
Release from the deployment rocket (\autoref{sec:deployment_rocket}) spaces the PuffSats so that they arrive over the few minutes of a push. A PuffSat released with a small velocity change drifts ahead of or behind its neighbors by that change times the coast time. Spreading a four-minute push this way takes at most \SI{4.4}{\meter\per\second} per PuffSat~\cite{katz2026aim}. The rocket pays that change, not the PuffSat. It can push PuffSats off in matched forward and backward pairs, so that its own momentum does not change, or burn between releases. Either way costs it under \SI{10}{\meter\per\second}. The stream is aimed at Earth, so the rocket must then leave it. A \SI{7}{\meter\per\second} burn at release moves its arrival \SI{10000}{\kilo\meter} to the side, clear of the atmosphere~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Each stream flies home with four co-flyers of about \SI{50}{\kilogram}, released with it~\cite{katz2026aim}. Three take station \SI{500}{\kilo\meter} off the track, one outward from Earth and two out of the orbit plane (\autoref{fig:jupiter_coflyers}). None sits on the Earth side, where it would pass lower than the stream. The fourth flies outward at the stream's trailing quarter point as a spare. Taking station costs each about \SI{0.7}{\meter\per\second}, and after that they coast without diverting, which makes them the steadiest references in the formation. The Deep Space Network fixes the co-flyers' positions by delta-DOR, an angle measured between two distant stations and referenced to a quasar. The co-flyers broadcast synchronized one-way radio signals, and each PuffSat fixes its own position from the differences in their arrival times. A PuffSat transmits nothing, and a quartz crystal serves as its clock. We require the formation's center to be within \SI{20}{\meter} of the orbit that reaches the target, which single delta-DOR fixes meet from about two days out~\cite{katz2026aim}.

A rod's packages steer it until arrival on propane cold-gas thrusters at the tips of their lines~\cite{katz2026aim}. A filter predicts the rod's swing on those lines (\autoref{fig:tether_swing}). If GNSS proves too coarse for the rod, a dedicated constellation in medium Earth orbit, near the altitude GPS flies, can take over its final approach. Unlike GNSS it measures in both directions. Each satellite exchanges laser pulses with each package and with the ship, and adding the two one-way travel times cancels the offset between their clocks. This is the dual one-way scheme that keeps the co-flyers in step, so quartz oscillators suffice and no atomic clock is needed. Light also crosses the ionosphere almost undelayed, where radio is slowed by its free electrons. The parent paper describes this option in more detail, including a link that times ranges to about \SI{0.1}{\milli\meter} in its low-orbit case~\cite{katz2026aim}. The cost is a constellation built for the purpose and a laser transponder on every package, which then no longer only receives. We hold it in reserve rather than in the baseline. The co-flyers still carry each stream through the coast.

The same release spacing can open gaps of one to two seconds in the formation wherever a satellite would pass too close to it~\cite{katz2026aim}. After the push, most PuffSat debris, including the electronics that pass the plate, continues on heliocentric orbits that cross Jupiter's~\cite{katz2026aim}. Jupiter's gravity is more likely to perturb this debris than Earth is to meet it again, so a later strike on a satellite is unlikely.

\section{Exponential Mass Growth}\label{sec:exponential_mass_growth}
Growth per cycle is the PuffSat mass a cycle sends back to Jupiter, divided by the PuffSat mass that arrived to launch it. The cycle grows when that ratio exceeds one (\autoref{sec:introduction}). Both waves count in the mass that arrived, since the departure wave's PuffSats are spent on the burn. The fleet's doubling time is $\ln 2$ over the mean growth rate, $\sum \ln G_i / \sum T_i$, for cycle growths $G_i$ and cycle lengths $T_i$~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The parent paper prices that growth with a ledger that charges every mass and pays every burn the sections above name~\cite{katz2026aim,Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. It carries a fixed \SI{1500}{\tonne} \textbf{launch unit}, the mass one batch of ground launches places at the intercept, through the eleven cycles of the real-ephemeris chain. Seven are two-synodic and four are three-synodic (\autoref{sec:two_synodic_cadence}). Every figure below comes from the companion repository~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. The doubling times of \autoref{tab:nozzle_isp} come from the simpler synodic-lock model and bring the chamber back for free. The ledger does neither.

Each cycle runs in five steps~\cite{katz2026aim}.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The growth wave overtakes the unit at \SI{400}{\kilo\meter} and pushes it from rest to the cycle orbit's periapsis speed there, \SI{10.786}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. The pusher plate of \autoref{sec:pusher_plates} takes the push.
    \item At the \SI{613000}{\kilo\meter} apoapsis, methalox pays the reversal of \autoref{fig:earth_turnaround}, \SI{234}{\meter\per\second} on the 20-day orbit, and raises periapsis to \SI{600}{\kilo\meter} for \SI{1.7}{\meter\per\second} more. The reversal costs about 6\% of the stack. Every design pays it, methalox included.
    \item The \SI{150}{\tonne} plate and the slug's empty tank drop off. A hydrogen stack also drops its cryostats and loses its boil-off over the hold. The slug is already spent, so the stack that departs masses \SIrange{500}{600}{\tonne}, not the \SI{1500}{\tonne} lifted.
    \item The chamber of \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles} makes the departure burn from \SI{600}{\kilo\meter} as the departure wave arrives. The gap between the two waves is the unit's parking orbit, flown at 20 days.
    \item Each wave pays its own course correction in methalox. The growth wave pays its early-arrival burn, a mean of \SI{464}{\meter\per\second} at the 20-day split. The departure wave pays the chain's deep-space maneuver (\autoref{sec:two_synodic_cadence}).
\end{enumerate}

The departure hardware is charged and then \textbf{expended}. The chamber wall (\SI{32}{\tonne} for hydrogen and \SI{19}{\tonne} for methane, \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}), a \SI{2.19}{\tonne} nozzle extension scaled up from the RL10B-2's carbon--carbon one, the tanks and any Raptor engines leave with the stack, and each launch unit brings a fresh set~\cite{katz2026aim}. Expending is the pessimistic case for mass. Retrieving the chamber would free 4 to 7\% of the departing stack at the price of the PuffSats that brake it, so it is an upside the ledger does not take. The ledger counts mass, not money. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} prices the chamber built for every cycle.

Efficiencies are quoted net of chemistry, and the two devices need different yardsticks. The plate's 0.5, 0.7 and 1.0 are the share of the pulse's energy left after the plume spreads and spills past the rim. The chemical toll of \autoref{sec:plate_water_spray}, the energy held in broken bonds, is charged separately, pulse by pulse. A chamber is quoted against its \textbf{chemistry ceiling}, the most of the pulse any chamber of that gas could turn into directed exhaust. It is one minus the energy held in bonds at peak that the nozzle never returns. At the design area ratio of 300, methane holds 69\% of the pulse in bonds and gets back only 52\% of that, because its carbon never re-bonds in the nozzle. Its ceiling is $1 - 0.69 \times 0.48 = 0.669$. Hydrogen holds 36\% and gets back 94\%, for $1 - 0.36 \times 0.06 = 0.978$~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026,katz2026aim}. The simulated chambers of \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles} reach \num{0.538} and \num{0.858}, which is 80\% and 88\% of their ceilings. Those are modeled, not measured, so every efficiency in the tables below is a requirement.

The plate's slug ratio $k$ changes pulse by pulse. The slug and the PuffSats both have to be launched, so the ledger minimizes their weighted sum. The best schedule sprays hardest while the closing speed is high and tapers as it falls. We cap $k$ at 10, the top of the range the parent has checked \autoref{eq:plate_ceiling} over. Left free, the optimum opens at 50 to 100 and runs 3 to 10\% faster~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The ledger's plate sprays argon onto ice PuffSats, taking the alternative of \autoref{sec:plate_water_spray}. Argon has no bonds, so only the PuffSat's own water pays the chemical toll. Argon does ionize. At \SI{50}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and $k = 10$ the merge heats the gas with about \SI{100}{\mega\joule\per\kilogram}, and singly ionized argon holds \SI{38.1}{\mega\joule\per\kilogram} of it. The ledger assumes the ions recombine while the gas still presses on the plate. The Saha equation, which gives the ionized fraction of a gas in equilibrium, supports that. Argon is 99\% recombined by \SI{15700}{\kelvin} at \SI{1}{\giga\pascal} and by \SI{11800}{\kelvin} at \SI{10}{\mega\pascal}, and three-body recombination is fast at plate densities~\cite{katz2026aim}. It remains an assumption, as water's recombination is, and water stays as the comparison.

The incumbent flies the same unit and the same plate. Methalox needs no departure wave, so the whole batch pushes. It departs on Raptor 3 engines, SpaceX's published sea-level engine of \SI{280}{\tonne}-force for \SI{1525}{\kilogram}~\cite{spacex2024_raptor3}. No vacuum Raptor 3 has been published, so the ledger pairs that mass and thrust with the vacuum Raptor's \SI{380}{\second}~\cite{raptor_vacuum_isp,katz2026aim}. Methalox cannot fly the two-synodic burn (\autoref{sec:two_synodic_cadence}), so its chain flies nine three-synodic cycles in the same span.

\section{Data Centers, Anti-Satellite Weapons, And Launch Costs}\label{sec:data_center_attacks}
Data centers are now military targets. In March 2026, Iranian drones struck Amazon Web Services data centers in the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. Project Suncatcher~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher} and Starcloud~\cite{foust2026_starcloud_fcc} both plan sun-synchronous orbits, Starcloud in shells at \qtyrange{600}{850}{\kilo\meter}. In 2007, China destroyed the Fengyun-1C weather satellite at \qty{865}{\kilo\meter} with a ground-launched missile~\cite{kan2007_asat,nasa_odqn2007_fengyun}, and China and Russia both now field such weapons~\cite{dia2022_space_security}. A sun-synchronous orbit passes over nearly every latitude~\cite{nasa_orbit_catalog}, so attackers get repeated chances and the interceptor arrives within minutes.

One reason satellite attacks are rare is that the military benefits aren't worth the cost of hitting dispersed targets~\cite{samson2025_swf_counterspace,hallex2020_proliferated_constellations}. The director of the US Space Development Agency says ``it will cost more to shoot down a single satellite than it will cost to build that single satellite''~\cite{hadley2023_tournear}. An orbital data center cannot spread out that way, because its nodes need high-bandwidth links to each other. Suncatcher's illustrative cluster puts 81 satellites, \qty{2.3}{\mega\watt} of computing, within a \qty{1}{\kilo\meter} radius~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. Nor can it hide. Homing on radar and infrared returns is within reach of many countries~\cite{wright2005_physics_space_security}. Starcloud's panels would span about \qty{4}{\kilo\meter} on a side~\cite{nvidia2025_starcloud}, and a data center sheds nearly all its power as heat, so a seeker has a conspicuous radar and infrared target.

Distance does not hide a data center, but it buys warning time. Reaching a Lagrange point takes \qty{11.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} against \qty{3.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second} to reach \qty{650}{\kilo\meter}~\cite{katz2026aim}. Even at \qty{14}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, an interceptor needs half a day to reach Earth--Moon L4/L5 and two days to reach Sun--Earth L1. That is time to disperse a cluster, or to launch interceptors based with it, like the escorts the U.S. Space Force plans for its own satellites~\cite{easley2025_ussf_framework}. In September 2026, the Secretary of the Air Force said that the United States has weapons in orbit, without saying what they are~\cite{ap2026_meink_space_weapons}. Whether they include a defense of this kind is not public.

Latency decides which workloads go where. High Earth orbit at \qtyrange{20000}{80000}{\kilo\meter} adds \qtyrange{0.13}{0.53}{\second} of round-trip delay and still gives \qty{0.5}{\hour} to \qty{14}{\hour} of warning, so it suits latency-sensitive inference. AI training and batch inference, including long-running coding agents, can go to the Lagrange points. The \qtyrange{2.6}{10}{\second} round trip there is far inside the \qty{24}{\hour} turnaround that batch inference services already offer~\cite{anthropic_message_batches,openai_batch_api}. Sun--Earth L1 has no eclipses at all~\cite{nasa_lagrange_points}, but the James Webb Space Telescope's always sunny halo orbit at Sun--Earth L2 shows other libration-point orbits can also avoid ever falling into shadow~\cite{nasa_webb_orbit}. The cost is exposure to solar particle events outside the magnetosphere~\cite{li2019vanallen}. Suncatcher's proton test ran a commercial tensor processing unit to 15~krad(Si) without hard failures~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}.

Starship~\cite{starship} can reach high orbits, but near term its launch costs at best match the price at which orbital data centers compete with terrestrial ones. Starcloud's chief executive puts competitive pricing near \$500 per \si{\kilo\gram}~\cite{bennici2026_johnston}. SpaceX's IPO underwriters, who have reason to be optimistic~\cite{michaely1999_underwriter}, forecast \$183 (Goldman Sachs~\cite{pethokoukis2026_goldman}) and about \$500 (Morgan Stanley~\cite{investing2026_ms_spacex}) per \si{\kilo\gram} to low Earth orbit in 2030. Sun--Earth L1 lies a further \qtyrange{3.2}{4.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} out, paid for with propellant carried up on tanker flights. A data center is heavy enough to fill all \qty{1200}{\tonne} of the ship's tanks~\cite{gunter_starship}, which takes 12 tanker flights and spreads the ship's \qty{85}{\tonne} dry mass over \qtyrange{510}{790}{\tonne} of cargo. Each kilogram delivered then costs 2.5 to 3.3 kilograms launched to low Earth orbit~\cite{katz2026aim}, turning the forecasts into \$460--610 and \$1260--1670 per \si{\kilo\gram}. Goldman's figure can just clear Starcloud's \$500, and no case reaches Suncatcher's \$200. Whether that much cargo fits is less clear. Starship lists \qty{150}{\tonne} to low Earth orbit~\cite{gunter_starship}, and we have found no source for how densely a data center packs. Capped at \qty{150}{\tonne}, each delivered kilogram costs 3.3 to 4.3 launched, or \$610--780 and \$1660--2120~\cite{katz2026aim}. High circular orbits are generally harder still. Circularizing at \qty{22000}{\kilo\meter} takes \qty{1.45}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, and leaving takes \qty{1.46}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, against about \qty{30}{\meter\per\second} to leave Sun--Earth L1~\cite{katz2026aim}.

These estimates are floors. They count the tanker propellant, including the reserve the ship needs to come home, but not the logistics and operations of orbital refueling, which SpaceX lists as a material challenge~\cite{spacex2026_s1a}. They treat the ship as reused, as the price to low orbit does, and charge nothing for its trip. A ship back from L1 enters at \qty{11.1}{\kilo\meter\per\second} rather than \qty{8.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, about 2.7 times the peak heating~\cite{katz2026aim}, on a heat shield SpaceX's chief executive called Starship's biggest challenge~\cite{musk2024_heatshield}. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} argues that PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can meet the \$500 per \si{\kilo\gram} target in high orbits.

\section{Launch Cost Speculation}\label{sec:launch_cost_speculation}
This section is the most speculative part of the paper. Apart from the ground launch, every price in it is a hypothesis, and so is the chance that the cycle works. The chamber efficiencies it rests on are requirements (\autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth}). It summarizes the parent paper's cost model, which the companion repository computes~\cite{katz2026aim,Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}.

\subsection{Selling A Stake In The Fleet}\label{sec:fleet_stake}
Someone has to pay for the first few cycles. The parent proposes selling a stake in the fleet counted in kilograms rather than dollars~\cite{katz2026aim}. An investor does not buy a launch slot. Their kilograms stay in the loop and multiply. The operator borrows against those subscriptions to send the first PuffSats toward Jupiter, and the fleet in transit is the collateral. A kilogram taken out as delivered cargo is a kilogram not sent back, so the fleet grows at its own rate less whatever holders withdraw. A working business compounds first and harvests later. The claim worth the most is the one with the most cycles still ahead of it.

What the stake earns is set by the departure hardware. Over ten years behind a 0.7 plate, with nothing withdrawn, the solved hydrogen chamber multiplies the fleet 75 times and the solved methane chamber 50 times. Methalox multiplies it 5.1 times, and a chamber at half its ceiling 4.0 to 5.0 times (\autoref{tab:growth_ledger_ten_year}). No financing structure moves the return that far. Only the chamber's efficiency does.

The collision that drives the cycle cannot be tested on the ground. The energy is not the obstacle. Seventy kilograms at \SI{70}{\kilo\meter\per\second} carries \SI{171}{\giga\joule}, about 41 tonnes of TNT, a large but ordinary test. Reaching that speed is the obstacle. Even a \SI{10}{\kilo\meter} barrel subjects the projectile to \num{25000}~g, and at sea level the air would press on its nose at about \SI{6}{\giga\pascal}~\cite{katz2026aim}. The efficiencies of \autoref{tab:growth_ledger_doubling} can therefore be measured only in flight, one cycle at a time.

That is why being early beats being rich. The cycles run 2.18 and 3.28 years apart, because that is how long Jupiter takes to return to a usable position~\cite{katz2026aim}. Each cycle returns one measurement and permits one redesign. A competitor with ten times the capital still waits 2.18 years for their next data point. A rival who starts five years late is two redesigns behind and stays two redesigns behind. The leader also builds more plates and chambers, and on the learning curve of \autoref{sec:l1_cost} each one costs less than the last.

None of this makes the stake safe. With every price at its pessimistic value at once, delivery costs \$410 to \$506 per kilogram, and no design repays the dearer of the two seed prices below \$500 per kilogram. The 50\% chance that the cycle works, used throughout, is an assumption rather than a measurement~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{What A Kilogram At L1 Costs}\label{sec:l1_cost}
The parent prices delivery to Sun--Earth L1 once the fleet runs level~\cite{katz2026aim,Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. The ground launch is a \textbf{lob}. A reusable booster lofts each \SI{1500}{\tonne} launch unit straight up to \SI{400}{\kilo\meter} and falls back. A returning wave pushes the unit to the \SI{10.82}{\kilo\meter\per\second} that coasts out to L1, where the \SI{150}{\tonne} plate stays with about \SI{800}{\tonne} of cargo. The lob is charged \$25 per kilogram lofted, or \$37.5 million a launch unit. Goldman's 2030 figure puts a whole Starship flight at \$18 million to \$27 million~\cite{pethokoukis2026_goldman}, so the booster alone is charged more than that. A Super Heavy as flown lofts \SIrange{1070}{1430}{\tonne}, so the launch unit assumes a booster 10 to 20\% larger, at the same price per kilogram. Both are the parent's assumptions~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Each kilogram sold pays for more than its own lob. Part of every returning wave has to go back to Jupiter, or the next cycle's fleet is smaller. A fleet that grows by a factor $G$ each cycle sends back $1/G$ of every return. That reinvested launch unit pays for its own lob and hardware, and its cost lands on the kilograms sold. Per delivered kilogram it comes to $C_g/(G-1)$, where $C_g$ is the cost of one growth launch unit. Methalox grows by a factor near 2 per cycle, so it reinvests about one launch unit for every one it sells. That is why it costs more per kilogram than a solved chamber, although its hardware is cheaper~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The plate and the chamber are expended in every launch unit and priced on Wright's law, the observation that unit cost falls by a fixed fraction each time cumulative production doubles. The cost of the $n$th unit is $T_1 n^b$, where $b = \log_2$ of that fraction. Wright found 80\% for airframe labor~\cite{wright1936_airplane_cost}. Each plate and chamber is charged at its own place in the count. By the harvest at 9.8 years the program has built 5 plates behind methalox and 29 to 38 behind the solved chambers~\cite{katz2026aim}. \autoref{tab:growth_prices} lists every price, and only the lob's has a source.

\subsection{Paying For The Seed}\label{sec:seed_finance}
The \textbf{seed} is the first batch of PuffSats, the one that must reach Jupiter before any wave comes back. The parent prices it on one route, chosen for the sake of argument~\cite{katz2026aim}. The seed flies the chain's first cycle, a three-synodic one leaving Earth in November 2026 with an excess speed of \SI{11.99}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. It rides a Starship that is refueled in low orbit, burns straight to that speed and is thrown away. A ship that never comes back needs no heat shield, no flaps and no landing propellant, so the parent flies a stripped ship of \SI{40}{\tonne} or \SI{60}{\tonne} dry against the stock ship's \SI{85}{\tonne}~\cite{gunter_starship}. Neither stripped mass has a source, so both are hypotheses. From \SI{200}{\kilo\meter} the burn is \SI{8.50}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. On three vacuum Raptors~\cite{spacex2024_raptor3} it lasts \SI{543}{\second} and loses \SI{117}{\meter\per\second} to steering. A full \SI{1200}{\tonne} load then drives about \SI{132}{\tonne} to departure speed. The \SI{40}{\tonne} ship sends \SI{92.1}{\tonne} of PuffSats and the \SI{60}{\tonne} ship \SI{72.1}{\tonne}, against \SI{50.5}{\tonne} for the stock ship~\cite{katz2026aim}. Gravity assists can send more on the same propellant at the cost of years, and \autoref{sec:seed_assists} returns to them.

Filling the ship takes 12 tanker flights of \SI{100}{\tonne}, so one seed ship costs 13 launches and one hull. The parent prices each launch three ways: Musk's \$2 million a flight~\cite{spacex_starship_cost}, Goldman Sachs's \$183 per kilogram~\cite{pethokoukis2026_goldman} and Morgan Stanley's \$500~\cite{investing2026_ms_spacex}. None of these includes a ship that is never recovered, and no citable source gives what one costs to build~\cite{spacex2026_s1a}. The stripped hull therefore enters as a hypothesis of \$5 million or \$20 million. The seed then costs \$337 to \$9293 per kilogram sent toward Jupiter~\cite{katz2026aim}. The low end is the \SI{40}{\tonne} ship at a \$5 million hull and Musk's price. The high end is the \SI{60}{\tonne} ship at a \$20 million hull and Morgan Stanley's price. Thirteen launches put \SI{1300}{\tonne} through low orbit to send 72 to \SI{92}{\tonne}, which multiplies every launch price by 14 to 18. Under the two bank prices the tankers are 85 to 92\% of the bill. The ship's payload matters more than its hull price.

The seed is not large. The fleet has to start with one launch unit's worth of PuffSats, enough for both returning waves. A \SI{1500}{\tonne} launch unit consumes 73 to \SI{87}{\tonne} on its first cycle (\autoref{tab:seed_amortization}). That is 0.79 to 0.94 of a stripped \SI{40}{\tonne} ship, or \$25 million to \$806 million across the price sweep. A chamber cycle needs 14 to 19\% more seed than methalox, because it also sends the departure wave~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The seed and the lob are two different kinds of cost. The seed is paid once. Each launch unit's lob is paid on every cycle, and so is the departure hardware the unit expends. Those costs grow with the fleet and stay roughly flat per kilogram. Only the seed and the research and development shrink per kilogram as the fleet grows, each as one over the growth multiple. After ten years the seed is \$66 to \$1833 per kilogram of fleet behind methalox, which grows 5.1 times. Behind the solved hydrogen chamber, which grows 75 times, it is \$4 to \$124~\cite{katz2026aim}.

That difference is the near-term choice, and neither side of it means no growth. The \textbf{plate-only cycle} pushes on the plate and departs on methalox. It has no pulsed chamber to develop, and it still doubles in \SI{4.1}{\year}. Its seed is spread over little mass, so the seed stays a large share of its cost for decades. The \textbf{chamber cycle} doubles in 1.6 to \SI{1.7}{\year}, and the seed's share falls fast. In exchange it pays to develop the chamber, and \autoref{sec:fleet_stake} explains why that development proceeds one flight cycle at a time. Methalox also expends hardware in every unit, two Raptor~3 engines, but that is a known engine with a price rather than a development program.

Wright's law cuts against the chamber's share of the bill. Starting from one launch unit, four cycles finish within ten years. Over them the solved hydrogen cycle builds 33 chambers and the solved methane cycle 25. The count trails the fleet by the last cycle's growth, so it is 5.0 doublings of cumulative production rather than the 6.2 a 75-fold fleet would suggest. At Wright's 80\%, the thirty-third chamber costs 0.33 of the first, and the average chamber 0.46~\cite{katz2026aim}.

An investor pays dollars for the seed and is repaid in kilograms later. A cycle that multiplies the fleet by $G$ in $T$ years is worth flying again only while $G > (1+r)^T$. Here $r$ is the investor's cost of capital, the return the same money could earn elsewhere at similar risk. Damodaran's January 2026 table puts the cost of capital of 79 United States aerospace and defense firms at 7.6\%~\cite{damodaran2026_wacc}. Surveyed venture capitalists who set a target return require a median of 30\% a year~\cite{gompers2020_vc_decisions}. At 7.6\% every cycle of every design passes, except 27\% of the cycles behind the hydrogen chamber at half its ceiling. At 30\% the solved chambers pass on every cycle, the half-ceiling chambers on 18\% of them, and methalox on none~\cite{katz2026aim}.

A single venture rate values the whole program badly. The 30\% bundles the time value of money with the chance that the idea fails. Compounding it every year charges that failure risk again for every year of waiting, and waiting does not make the physics more likely to fail. The parent therefore prices time and risk separately~\cite{katz2026aim}. Time is worth 10\% a year, about what a risky bond yields. The cycle has a 50\% chance of working, settled once at the \textbf{first growth return}. That is the first time a launch unit pushed by returning PuffSats comes home from Jupiter, at 5.46 years for the chambers and 6.55 years for methalox. Money spent before then is spent either way. Every flow after it is weighted by the odds, and the cycle is then assumed to keep working, which is optimistic. Most venture-backed bets fail, so 50\% is not conservative, and \autoref{tab:seed_return} also prints 25\% and 100\%. The \textbf{break-even} is the sale price at L1 at which the expected value of every sale repays the seed and every growth cost.

Kilograms become dollars when a returning wave pushes cargo to L1 instead of PuffSats toward Jupiter. At a \$500 sale price each returning PuffSat kilogram pushes 9.3 to 12.6 kilograms of cargo~\cite{katz2026aim}. The holder can take that value in two ways. \textbf{Liquidation} sends the whole wave returning at 9.83 years, the last return inside ten years, to L1 and stops. The \textbf{steady state} sends $1/G$ of every return back to Jupiter and sells the rest, cycle after cycle. Holding the fleet beats liquidating exactly when $G > (1+r)^T$, the same test as whether to keep growing. At 30\% methalox would rather liquidate, and the solved chambers would rather hold the fleet~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Seeding Without A Refueled Starship}\label{sec:seed_assists}
The seed can also be bought more cheaply with time. A route that makes the seed $k$ times cheaper per kilogram but returns $\Delta t$ years later pays when $k(1.1)^{-\Delta t} > 1$. The chance that the cycle works is the same whichever way the seed travels, so it cancels, and only the 10\% time value remains. A two-year delay has to buy a seed 1.21 times cheaper. A \textbf{gravity assist} passes that test. A planetary flyby bends the ship's path and changes its speed relative to the Sun without propellant, so the ship can leave Earth slower and let the planet supply the rest. The companion searched routes with and without Earth and Venus flybys, with the planets where they really are, freeing the launch date within six years of November 2026~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. Its best direct seed leaves Earth at an excess speed of \SI{11.3}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. One \textbf{Earth loop}, which swings past Earth once before heading to Jupiter, leaves at \SI{6.8}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and burns \SI{2.6}{\kilo\meter\per\second} of methalox between flybys. It sends 1.64 times the direct route's seed on the \SI{60}{\tonne} ship and 1.43 times on the \SI{40}{\tonne} ship, and returns 2.18 years later. That is 1.33 and 1.16 times the seed per dollar. A Venus--Earth--Earth route, the one Galileo flew~\cite{damario1992galileo}, does 1.19 times on the cheap ship and 1.04 times on the dear one. Routes that burn several kilometers per second between flybys lose. On the dear seed the Earth loop lowers solved hydrogen's break-even from \$194 to \$179 per kilogram and solved methane's from \$222 to \$201. On the cheap seed it moves them by \$1 at most, because the seed is a small share of that bill~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Gravity assists matter more if Starship is not available. Every seed price above assumes a Starship refueled in low orbit, and a technical setback or a refusal of access could take that away. The direct seed needs \SI{11.3}{\kilo\meter\per\second} of excess speed, because it has to come back from Jupiter at the cycle's collision speed. Conventional expendable rockets deliver little mass at that speed. An Earth loop needs \SI{6.8}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. A Venus--Earth route can leave at \SI{4.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, though it then burns \SI{5.6}{\kilo\meter\per\second} between flybys. Lower launch energy is why Galileo and Juno flew assists~\cite{damario1992galileo,bolton2017juno}. Without a refueled Starship, the plan is a few conventional launches on assisted routes. The seed may then cost far more per kilogram, and the program can carry that. The seed is paid once, and it needs to be only one launch unit's first-cycle consumption, 73 to \SI{87}{\tonne}. After that, growth divides it. Behind the solved hydrogen chamber, ten years cut the seed's cost per kilogram of fleet by a factor of 75 (\autoref{tab:seed_amortization}). A program that can start only on one provider's refueled vehicle depends on that provider, and assisted routes are the hedge. The parent has not priced them, since the companion models no launcher other than Starship~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Four caveats sit next to these numbers~\cite{katz2026aim}. The sale price and the lob price are held fixed, while launch prices are forecast to fall. Morgan Stanley's \$500 per kilogram in 2030 becomes less than \$150 by 2040~\cite{investing2026_ms_spacex}. A kilogram delivered later may therefore sell for less, and holding the price fixed flatters every row. The same forecast makes waiting for a cheaper seed pass the 10\% test, narrowly. Ten years must buy a seed 2.6 times cheaper, and the forecast gives 3.3. The case against a decade's wait rests on competitors and on the cost of carrying a team for ten years, not on the discount rate. Demand also caps the fleet. When the fleet grows faster than money compounds, the model's value grows without bound, so a real steady state is set by how much cargo customers buy. The plate's slug ratio stops at 10, the top of the range the parent has checked. Last, every plate and chamber is expended. Recovering them could cut the bill, and the parent has not priced it.

\section{Oberth Maneuvers Near The Sun}\label{sec:oberth_solar_maneuvers}
The Jupiter loop never goes near the Sun. The parent paper adds a solar dive to it, which it calls the \textbf{Jovian dive cycle}~\cite{katz2026aim}. The payload leaves Earth for Jupiter, and an unpowered flyby drops its perihelion to 4 solar radii, the depth of the 2005 Solar Probe design~\cite{mccomas2005solar}. There a pulse boosts it by \SI{35.04}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. The Oberth effect makes the boost cheap, because a fixed escape surplus costs less impulse the faster the periapsis where it is bought. The payload climbs out of the Sun's pull with about \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second} to spare and crosses Earth at \SI{157.82}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. Jupiter still sets the clock. Three synodic periods (\SI{3.28}{\year}) is the only multiple that closes, since two cannot bend far enough at Jupiter and four cannot fly slowly enough~\cite{katz2026aim,Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. The opposing projectiles go to Jupiter too, and the flybys there split the two streams onto mirror-image dives (\autoref{fig:jovian_dive_loop}).

The two meet head-on at the node, closing at \SI{617}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, and the payload gains \SI{35.04}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. It climbs back out across \SI{1}{\astronomicalunit} (dashed) and meets Earth \SI{3.2761}{\year} after it left, three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods, crossing at \SI{157.82}{\kilo\meter\per\second} to push the next payload. The impactors are one-way and are consumed at the node \cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}.}
    \label{fig:jovian_dive_loop}
\end{figure}

The Earth end resembles the Jupiter loop's (\autoref{sec:three_synodic_cadence}). The cycle departs Earth at a hyperbolic excess speed of \SI{10.54}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, a little below the Jupiter loop's \qtyrange{12.0}{14.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second}~\cite{katz2026aim}. The growth wave overtakes the payload, so its momentum adds to the push. Each PuffSat arriving at \SI{158}{\kilo\meter\per\second} rather than \SI{57}{\kilo\meter\per\second} carries nearly three times the momentum per kilogram, and fewer kilograms do the same lift. The departure wave arrives at \ang{89.9} to the thrust, where the cosine is 0.002, so its arriving momentum neither helps nor hurts~\cite{katz2026aim}. In the Jupiter loop the departure wave arrives at \ang{150.4} and its momentum is charged against the burn. The plate and the chambers have been checked only at the Jupiter loop's speeds, and the returning stream here is about twice as fast.

The perihelion pulse is where this variant departs from the parent's reference design. The parent takes that pulse in a magnetic nozzle, whose coil must hold near \SI{30}{\kelvin} inside \SI{3.93}{\mega\watt\per\square\meter} of sunlight and which tolerates radiative escape below about a tenth of a percent~\cite{katz2026aim}. This section uses the parent's alternative, a \textbf{pass-through X-ray pusher} that works like Orion's pulse unit. The ship is a ring with a clear bore along its axis and a pusher plate at its stern. Before each pulse it sets a pulse unit \SIrange{5}{10}{\meter} astern, on the line the next projectile will fly (\autoref{fig:pass_through_pusher}a). The projectile arrives from ahead at a closing speed of \SI{618}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, threads the bore, and strikes a carbon-foam target inside the pulse unit. Its momentum never touches the ship, so none of it is charged against thrust~\cite{katz2026aim}. A heavy-metal radiation case around the target traps the X-rays, which drive a filler and a propellant plate toward the pusher as they did in Orion~\cite{balcomb1970nuclear,projorion} (\autoref{fig:pass_through_pusher}b). Orion capped the speed at which propellant may strike the plate at \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}~\cite{balcomb1970nuclear}.

Foam is what lets a kinetic pulse do a bomb's job. The collision heats carbon to about \SI{470}{\electronvolt}, far cooler than a fission device and close to the soft X-ray cavities of inertial fusion~\cite{lindl1995}. A dense fireball at that temperature traps its own light and expands as a gas before it can radiate. Foam at \SIrange{10}{30}{\kilogram\per\cubic\meter} spreads the same mass over a fireball about \SI{23}{\centi\meter} in radius. That is thin enough for radiation to empty the heat before expansion does. Foam also stretches the pulse. The projectile takes about \SI{0.75}{\micro\second} to cross a \SI{46}{\centi\meter} target, so the case has to hold its radiation a hundred times longer than an inertial-fusion cavity, at a much lower temperature~\cite{katz2026aim}. The parent has computed neither the opacity of carbon at \SI{470}{\electronvolt} nor a case that light.

The ship and the projectiles share one thermal design, and it is Parker's carried deeper. Each sits behind a cone pointed at the Sun, the shield the 2005 design chose~\cite{mccomas2005solar} (\autoref{fig:sun_cone}a). A flank at a \ang{15} half-angle intercepts $\sin\ang{15}$ of the flux, which cuts \SI{3.9}{\mega\watt\per\square\meter} to \SI{1.0}{\mega\watt\per\square\meter}. An alumina skin there runs near \SI{1590}{\kelvin}, and \SI{2060}{\kelvin} if sunlight blackens it fully, both below alumina's melting point. Behind the skin sits a carbon-foam core like the one in Parker's shield~\cite{parker_thermal_protection}. A small tungsten insert caps the apex, the one spot that faces the Sun squarely. A dust strike could blunt a graphite point there into a hot spot, and tungsten's \SI{3695}{\kelvin} melting point clears the \SI{2890}{\kelvin} worst case (\autoref{fig:sun_cone}b). Electronics ride in the cone's shadow, as the 2005 design's whole bus did~\cite{katz2026aim}. The projectile has to be foam light enough for the pusher while still carrying its guidance and this shield.

The payload and the opposing projectiles both go to Jupiter, and they split there. Neither makes a separate deep dive from Earth. The payload's flyby bends it into a prograde dive. The projectiles' flyby bends them into a retrograde dive on the mirror image of the payload's ellipse, so the two reach the same perihelion from opposite sides and close at twice the perihelion speed (\autoref{fig:jovian_dive_loop}). The projectiles cannot fly out alongside the payload, though. An unpowered flyby turns the arrival velocity without changing its size. Reaching 4 solar radii retrograde from Jupiter takes an arrival excess of \SI{14.16}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, and the payload's own \SI{12.17}{\kilo\meter\per\second} falls short of it. The projectiles therefore fly their own faster leg out to Jupiter, leaving Earth 6.7 days before the payload at \SI{11.06}{\kilo\meter\per\second} of excess~\cite{katz2026aim,Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. They are one-way and expendable, so their trajectory only has to exist and be timed. It does not have to close on the synodic clock.

The pusher's price is projectiles. The parent prices it on a \SI{36}{\kilo\meter\per\second} boost, close to this cycle's, with a quarter of the ship arriving. That needs \SI{2650}{\second} of specific impulse, which the pusher reaches only if the case weighs no more than about \SI{5}{\kilogram} per kilogram of projectile. It then spends \SI{0.20}{\kilogram} of projectile per kilogram of final mass, three times the magnetic nozzle's \numrange{0.064}{0.068}~\cite{katz2026aim}. Those figures assume that 60\% of the collision heat radiates, that the case turns 80\% of that into motion, and that the plate catches 80\% of the forward momentum. All three are requirements. In exchange the ship carries no cryogenic magnet near the Sun and needs no budget for radiative escape. The parent has run the Jovian dive through its growth ledger only with the magnetic nozzle. That version multiplies the payload 23 times per cycle, doubles in \SI{0.724}{\year}, and pays for its own ground launch with 4\% to spare~\cite{katz2026aim}. Spending three times the projectiles at the node, the X-ray version will grow more slowly, and the parent has not computed how much more slowly or whether it still pays for its launch.
