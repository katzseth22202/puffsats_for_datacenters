\begin{abstract}
Project Orion envisioned spacecraft that would combine high thrust with high specific impulse by using nuclear explosions for pulsed momentum transfer. This paper introduces \textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion}, a non-nuclear kinetic analogue to Orion. In this architecture, small, low-cost satellites (``PuffSats'') execute a rapid Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop, returning on retrograde orbits at Earth-relative speeds of \qtyrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second} in just 2.2 or 3.3 years (two or three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods). At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds the specific chemical energy released by methalox combustion by more than two orders of magnitude. As each PuffSat approaches the target vehicle, its working mass disperses into a gas cloud that mixes with propellant released by the target. The resulting fireball either strikes the target's pusher plate or is captured in a pulsed nozzle chamber that can produce thrust in any direction, including head-on toward the incoming PuffSats. We argue that because launched payloads can include new PuffSats, this mechanism can sustain exponential growth in launch capacity.  Once initiated, this launch growth cycle requires no further orbital-class chemical boosters. Finally, we evaluate a PuffSat architecture optimized for launching orbital data centers.  We find that, under the stated assumptions, PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion could undercut near-term fully reusable launch systems such as SpaceX Starship on monetary cost.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} is a proposed non-nuclear propulsion system that uses the kinetic energy of small, low-cost satellites to accelerate a receiving vehicle. Each satellite, called a PuffSat, flies an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop and returns on a retrograde heliocentric orbit, moving around the Sun opposite to Earth. The return speed relative to Earth is \qtyrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. The loops take 2.2 or 3.3 years, corresponding to two or three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods, the interval between successive alignments of the two planets. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds the specific chemical energy released by methane--oxygen combustion by more than a hundredfold.

The concept builds on Project Orion~\cite{projorion}, which proposed using nuclear explosions to deliver repeated impulses to a spacecraft, and on pellet-beam propulsion~\cite{singer1979interstellar,willis1994momentum,nordley2001interstellar,nordley2015massbeam,kare2001sailbeam,davoyan2023pelletbeam,whitmire_jackson1977laser_ramjet,matloff1979runway,kare1997buzzbomb,greason2020runway,greason2022windpellet,winglee_magbeam,landis2004particlebeam,katz2026aim}. PuffSats obtain their extreme velocities through gravity assists rather than giant accelerators. As each PuffSat approaches the receiving vehicle, its working mass disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the vehicle. The collision converts relative kinetic energy into hot gas. This gas either strikes a pusher plate, transferring an impulse to the vehicle, or enters a chamber and expands through a pulsed nozzle. The nozzle directs the exhaust to produce thrust, including thrust toward the incoming PuffSats. The architecture combines repeated high-thrust pulses with high specific impulse, a measure of impulse delivered per unit propellant weight. Its performance depends on how efficiently the collision and receiving hardware convert the incoming energy and momentum into useful thrust.

Each PuffSat must preserve its payload and electronics through the interplanetary flight despite radiation, micrometeorites, and temperature extremes. Its structural components must avoid striking the receiving vehicle. \autoref{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture} sketches a satellite design addressing these requirements, and \autoref{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists} describes the Jupiter trajectories. The receiving hardware must survive repeated pulses while capturing the arriving flow. \autoref{sec:pusher_plates} and \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles} discuss pusher plates and pulsed nozzles.

Launched payloads can include replacement PuffSats as well as useful cargo. If each returning generation enables a greater mass of PuffSats to be sent onto new Jupiter trajectories, the returning mass grows exponentially from cycle to cycle. Under that condition, arriving PuffSats propel both cargo and the next generation, allowing the cycle to continue without further orbital-class chemical boosters after conventional launches seed it. The initial launch expense is then spread over an increasing delivered mass. \autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth} examines this growth condition. The 2.2- or 3.3-year return time sets the pace of expansion.

Large orbital data centers provide a potential near-term market for very heavy lift. Their destinations must balance communication delay to Earth against survivability. Greater distance increases communication delay but also provides more time to respond to an incoming interceptor. \autoref{sec:data_center_attacks} examines this siting tradeoff. Because destination choice sets the transport requirement, the cost comparison must place PuffSat vehicles and fully reusable rockets such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship} on routes serving the same destinations. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} examines whether a growing supply of returning PuffSats can reduce delivery costs below those of repeated reusable-rocket launches. The comparison depends on propulsion performance, the mass returned each cycle, and production and operating costs. Under the stated assumptions, PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion could lower the monetary cost per kilogram launched below that of fully reusable rockets.

Accurate delivery requires navigation and control at two distinct scales. Each swarm must maintain its formation, and the formation as a whole must deliver its gas clouds onto the receiving vehicle. PRISMA demonstrated autonomous relative navigation and control in orbit~\cite{damico2011prisma_safe}, while the VISORS mission develops these capabilities for a precision CubeSat formation~\cite{guffanti2023autonomous}. PuffSat formations would be much larger. Formation control alone does not establish that they can hit a receiving vehicle at the required closing speeds. \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation} addresses this targeting problem and the vehicle corrections needed between successive pulses.

The companion white paper~\cite{katz2026aim} was originally written to establish the technical groundwork for hard-science-fiction world-building. This paper examines the narrower application of delivering orbital data centers. Its purpose is to make the architecture and its assumptions explicit so that experts can assess the propulsion requirements, growth cycle, and launch-cost argument.

\section{Data Centers, Anti-Satellite Weapons, And Launch Costs}\label{sec:data_center_attacks}
For the data-center application, the launch-cost comparison depends on the destination, which must
satisfy both latency and survivability requirements.
Iran's drone strikes on Amazon's Gulf data centers in March 2026 established that data centers are
wartime targets~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. Those were drones against
buildings, so they say nothing about orbital reach. China's destruction of the FY-1C weather
satellite at \SI{865}{\kilo\meter} in January 2007 says a great deal~\cite{kelso2007asat,nasa_odqn2007_fengyun}.
A direct-ascent interceptor~\cite{kan2007_asat} arrives at sun-synchronous altitude in minutes, which leaves no window to
disperse a cluster or to engage the inbound vehicle. A transfer to an Earth-Moon Lagrange point takes
days, at a cost of \SI{2.6}{\second} of round-trip light time to an Earth customer. Distance does not
hide a data center, and a published halo orbit is as predictable as a sun-synchronous one. What
distance buys is the time an active response needs.
