\begin{abstract}
PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion is a non-nuclear kinetic analogue of Project Orion's nuclear pulse propulsion. Small, low-cost satellites (``PuffSats'') fly an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop that closes in 2.2 or 3.3 years. They return on retrograde orbits at Earth-relative speeds of \qtyrange{57}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds methalox's specific combustion energy by more than a hundredfold.

A suborbital rocket lifts the target vehicle above the atmosphere, and PuffSat pulses supply the remaining $\deltav$. Each arriving PuffSat disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the target. The hot gas either strikes a pusher plate or expands through a pulsed nozzle that can thrust in any direction. We argue that each target vehicle can send more new PuffSat mass toward Jupiter than the incoming mass it consumes. Repeated across generations, this surplus would sustain exponential growth in launch capacity with no further orbital-class chemical boosters. We also sketch a variant that adds an Oberth maneuver (a velocity change at perihelion, where high speed multiplies the energy gained) and returns PuffSats at Earth-relative speeds above \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}.

Finally, we apply the architecture to orbital data centers. Data centers are now wartime targets, so we argue they belong in orbits distant enough that an attack takes days rather than minutes to arrive. AI training and coding inference tolerate the seconds of light delay to Earth that this adds. Reaching those orbits takes a large $\deltav$, and under the stated assumptions PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can deliver mass there more cheaply than fully reusable rockets such as SpaceX Starship.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} is a proposed non-nuclear propulsion system that uses the kinetic energy of small, low-cost satellites to accelerate a target vehicle. Each satellite, called a PuffSat, flies an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop and returns on a retrograde heliocentric orbit. The return speed relative to Earth is \qtyrange{57}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds methalox's specific combustion energy by more than a hundredfold. The Jupiter flyby reverses each PuffSat's orbital direction and sends it sunward. The loop therefore closes in 2.2 or 3.3 years (two or three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods), rather than the 5.5 years of two minimum-energy legs. PuffSats depart with an Earth-relative hyperbolic excess speed of \qtyrange{12.0}{14.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. New Horizons had an excess speed of \SI{12.6}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and reached Jupiter in 404 days~\cite{guo2008newhorizons_design}, and Ulysses had an excess speed of \SI{11.3}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and reached it in 16 months~\cite{marsden1992ulysses_jupiter}. \autoref{fig:jupiter_loop} draws the loop, \autoref{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists} gives the trajectory details, and the calculations are in the companion code repository~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}.

The concept builds on Project Orion~\cite{projorion}, which proposed using nuclear explosions to deliver repeated impulses to a spacecraft, and on pellet-beam propulsion~\cite{singer1979interstellar,willis1994momentum,nordley2001interstellar,nordley2015massbeam,kare2001sailbeam,davoyan2023pelletbeam,whitmire_jackson1977laser_ramjet,matloff1979runway,kare1997buzzbomb,greason2020runway,greason2022windpellet,winglee_magbeam,landis2004particlebeam}. PuffSats reach their extreme velocities through gravity assists, which are flight-proven, rather than through nuclear explosions or through beam launchers that have not been built. A conventional suborbital rocket lifts the target vehicle above the atmosphere, and PuffSat pulses supply the remaining $\deltav$. As each PuffSat approaches, its working mass disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the target vehicle. The collision converts relative kinetic energy into hot gas. This gas either strikes a pusher plate, transferring an impulse to the target vehicle, or enters a chamber and expands through a pulsed nozzle. The nozzle directs the exhaust to produce thrust, including thrust toward the incoming PuffSats. 

Each PuffSat must preserve its payload and electronics through the interplanetary flight despite radiation, micrometeorites, and temperature extremes. Its structural components must avoid striking the target vehicle. \autoref{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture} sketches a satellite design addressing these requirements. The target vehicle's hardware must survive repeated pulses while capturing the arriving flow. \autoref{sec:pusher_plates} and \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles} discuss pusher plates and pulsed nozzles.

PuffSat streams are designed to fly as tight formations, so that a target vehicle meeting the first PuffSat needs only small corrections to meet the rest. A formation that holds its shape can still arrive off target, so the target vehicle must measure each PuffSat's offset and correct between successive impacts. \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation} addresses these guidance, navigation, and control problems.

Launched payloads can include replacement PuffSats as well as useful cargo. If each returning generation enables a greater mass of PuffSats to be sent onto new Jupiter trajectories, the returning mass grows exponentially from cycle to cycle. Under that condition, arriving PuffSats propel both cargo and the next generation, allowing the cycle to continue without further orbital-class chemical boosters after conventional launches seed it. The initial launch expense is then spread over an increasing delivered mass. \autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth} examines this growth condition.

Large orbital data centers provide a potential near-term market for very heavy lift~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher,nvidia2025_starcloud,foust2026_starcloud_fcc}. Iran's 2026 strikes on terrestrial data centers~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike} show that data centers are military targets. China's 2007 destruction of the FY-1C weather satellite~\cite{nasa_odqn2007_fengyun,kan2007_asat} confirms that direct-ascent suborbital missiles can reach sun-synchronous orbit in minutes. In \autoref{sec:data_center_attacks}, we argue for orbits distant enough that an attack takes days to arrive, leaving time to respond. Because each cluster is co-located~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}, only its link to Earth slows down, and AI training and long-running coding inference can tolerate the added round-trip latency of \qtyrange{2.6}{10}{\second}~\cite{anthropic_message_batches,openai_batch_api}. Reaching them takes more $\deltav$, which favors PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion over conventional rockets. Launch must also be cheap enough for orbital computing to compete with data centers on the ground. Starcloud's chief executive puts that price near \$500 per kilogram~\cite{bennici2026_johnston}. Google's Suncatcher team sets a stricter bar, finding that at \$200 per kilogram, launch spread over a satellite's life costs about as much as a terrestrial data center's energy~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} argues that PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can deliver mass to these orbits for less than fully reusable rockets such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship}.

\autoref{sec:oberth_solar_maneuvers} sketches a variant that adds an Oberth maneuver near the Sun to the Earth--Jupiter loop, producing Earth-relative speeds above \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. This paper complements the long-term conceptual roadmap of \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim} by focusing on near-term applications.

\section{PuffSat Satellite Architecture}\label{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture}
\subsection{Deployment Rocket}\label{sec:deployment_rocket}
PuffSats do not fly the Jupiter loop alone. A large deployment rocket carries them out, through the Jupiter flyby, and back toward Earth, and releases them only during the final approach to Earth. Until then it supplies their power and keeps their water liquid.

Sunlight can do that job at Jupiter. Juno has run on about \SI{500}{\watt} from three \SI{9}{\meter} arrays since it arrived in 2016~\cite{bolton2017juno,juno_solar_record}. A rocket carrying many PuffSats needs proportionally less. Heat leaks through its surface, which grows as the square of its size, while the mass it keeps warm grows as the cube. Its panels can therefore be a smaller fraction of its mass than Juno's. The flyby does pass through Jupiter's shadow, so the rocket carries a battery to keep its electronics and the PuffSats' water warm until it returns to sunlight.

Radiation is the harder problem. The flyby turns the trajectory within Jupiter's orbital plane, and Jupiter's equator is tilted only \ang{3.1} from that plane. The rocket therefore crosses the equatorial radiation belts that Juno's polar orbit was designed to pass under~\cite{bolton2017juno,katz2026aim}. The PuffSats themselves are the shield. Their mass is mostly water and other light volatiles, enough to surround the electronics with \qtyrange{50}{100}{\centi\meter} of material. Electrons, and the bremsstrahlung X-rays they emit as they stop, set most of the dose~\cite{roussos2021jovian_belts,katz2026aim}. Water also slows the neutrons that energetic protons and photons knock out of nuclei. Boron carbide at about \SI{0.5}{\percent} of the shield's mass then captures them without the penetrating \SI{2.2}{\mega\electronvolt} gamma ray that hydrogen capture emits~\cite{katz2026aim}. No full dose budget for the pass has been computed, so the shield thickness is a requirement rather than a result.

Solar cells are the part of the power system the belts damage most, because a panel has to face open space to work. At Jupiter's distance sunlight delivers about \SI{50}{\watt\per\square\meter}, a twenty-seventh of its flux at Earth. A non-imaging concentrator, a mirror or lens that funnels light from a wide aperture onto a small target without forming an image, shrinks the cell area by its concentration ratio~\cite{winston2005nonimaging}. A ratio of 27 puts Earth-strength sunlight back on the cell. Seen from Jupiter, the Sun is a disk only \ang{0.05} in radius, so the concentrated beam stays narrow. Even with \ang{1} of pointing error the focused light stays within about \ang{5} of its axis~\cite{winston2005nonimaging,katz2026aim}. A beam that narrow can enter a mirrored cavity and turn two corners on its way to cells inside the shield (\autoref{fig:mirrored_light_cavity}).

The bends do the shielding, because only light turns the corners. Jupiter's magnetic field cannot steer a particle around one. Even at the equatorial cloud tops, where the dipole field is \SI{0.417}{\milli\tesla}~\cite{connerney2018jrm09}, a \SI{10}{\mega\electronvolt} electron circles with a radius of about \SI{85}{\meter} and strays only about \SI{1}{\centi\meter} over a \SI{3}{\meter} cavity~\cite{katz2026aim}. X-rays from outside are stopped too, because a metal reflects them only below a glancing angle of about \ang{0.24}~\cite{alsnielsen2011xray}. Some electrons do scatter back off the first mirror, \SI{9}{\percent} of them at \SI{1}{\mega\electronvolt} on aluminum at normal incidence~\cite{miller1970backscatter}. Those that stop in it radiate bremsstrahlung. The second bend hides the cells from the first mirror, so this secondary radiation must scatter again to reach them. Each aluminum fold reflects about nine tenths of visible light~\cite{hass1961aluminum}, so two folds pass about \SI{81}{\percent}, and the concentration ratio is raised to cover the loss. Neither the cavity's throughput, including mirror darkening under the belt dose, nor the dose at the cells has been computed, so both are requirements rather than results.

The rocket releases the PuffSats as far as \SI{1.3}{\astronomicalunit} from the Sun. From there the coast to Earth lasts about 16 days~\cite{katz2026aim}. Each PuffSat is a bag of water around an inner bag of explosive emulsion, and the water holds the emulsion's temperature through the coast. Just before release, a deployment mechanism spins and orients each PuffSat and kneads the emulsion. Kneading mixes in the sensitizers, which stay out until late in flight so that the charge is hard to set off by accident~\cite{maranda2025comprehensive}. It also mixes in slow-acting thickeners that lock the sensitizing bubbles in place, and it lets each PuffSat fly without a pump.

\subsection{Pusher-Plate PuffSats}\label{sec:pusher_plate_puffsats}
After release, each PuffSat spins. Redundant Kevlar~49 wires, each \SI{100}{\micro\meter} across, tether its electronics to the working mass. Three of them hold each sphere, and together they break at \SI{71}{\newton} against the \SI{20}{\newton} the spin pulls~\cite{kevlar_technical_guide,katz2026aim}. The wires are long enough that the spin holds the electronics outside the target vehicle's diameter, so the electronics pass it by and only the wires cross the plate (\autoref{fig:tethered_necklace}). The wires run centimeters apart, so freeing the electronics takes a separate micrometeoroid cut on every wire, about once in \num{e11} over a sixteen-day coast~\cite{forward_hoyt1995hoytether,katz2026aim}. Thin wires also load the plate less. Each strikes it as a narrow line of low areal density, and a locally thicker ablative coating where the wires will hit protects the plate from them~\cite{katz2026aim}. The working mass is a necklace of spheres, oriented to explode into a disk of gas ahead of the pusher plate, the shape the plate couples to best~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026,katz2026aim}. Each sphere is a bag of water around an inner bag of explosive emulsion, shaped to detonate into that disk. Each sphere carries a clock that fires a small primary charge. A one-gram ice bullet fired by the target vehicle can also set it off. Its shock reaches the emulsion at about \SI{2}{\giga\pascal}, twice what sets off a sensitive secondary explosive~\cite{souers_thresholds,katz2026aim}.

The spin axis points along the stream, so the Sun sits \SIrange{44}{50}{\degree} off it for the whole coast. Side heating across the spin's effective gravity drives a slow convective loop that evens out each sphere's temperature. A pattern of high- and low-emittance patches sets the mean~\cite{katz2026aim} (\autoref{fig:sidewall_convection}).

Each sphere sits inside a multilayer blanket of the aluminum--Mylar--aluminum laminate that Echo~II flew~\cite{nasa_echo_ii_1966}. The blanket stays folded inside the deployment rocket until release. It is then stretched over the interior bags at a standoff of about a centimeter. Stretching yields the laminate's aluminum, so the blanket holds its shape without gas pressure, as Echo~II did~\cite{nasa_echo_ii_1966,katz2026aim}. The emittance pattern is printed on its outer sheet. The blanket is also a Whipple shield, a thin sacrificial layer that breaks up a particle before it reaches the wall behind~\cite{whipple_shield}. NASA's shield-design rule sets the bumper mass that shatters a grain of given size and density~\cite{jsc_mmod_handbook}. By that rule, a grain smaller than about \SI{185}{\micro\meter} shatters on the laminate's \SI{37}{\gram\per\square\meter}, and its debris spreads out before it reaches the bag~\cite{jsc_mmod_handbook,katz2026aim} (\autoref{fig:sphere_layers}a). Each further sheet shocks the debris cloud again~\cite{cour_palais1990multishock}.

Larger micrometeoroids still punch through, so some PuffSats will be lost. Grün's flux model puts a grain above \SI{185}{\micro\meter} into about one \SI{10}{\kilogram} sphere in 170 over a sixteen-day coast~\cite{grun1985collisional,katz2026aim}. A loss found early costs little, because the formation adjusts and a spare moves into the empty slot~\cite{katz2026aim}. A loss found too close to impact for that falls to the target vehicle. It makes up the missing momentum with an unplanned burn from a propellant reserve, which keeps it on the trajectory the remaining PuffSats are aimed at. That loss rate sizes the reserve~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Each PuffSat also flies through gas left by the pulses before it. At closing speeds near \SI{60}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, even this thin gas heats the sphere. It needs no heat shield, because the dose arrives late (\autoref{fig:plume_timeline}). The blanket and the bag may fail in the last few hundred milliseconds, at no cost. A freed sphere drifts only \SI{9}{\centi\meter} outward before impact, and the necklace's center stays put (\autoref{fig:tethered_necklace})~\cite{katz2026aim}. The gas is too thin to steer the sphere. In a ballistic model, drag moves its landing point on the plate by under \SI{20}{\micro\meter}~\cite{katz2026aim}. The wires fare worse. They lose their strength about \SI{240}{\milli\second} before impact, before the charges fire~\cite{kevlar_technical_guide,katz2026aim}, so nothing they carry may be needed once the gas arrives.

Blanket shreds that survive the gas must still cross the water the plate sprays ahead of itself. That spray is sized to stop the whole dispersed PuffSat, with five to ten times its mass over the same footprint~\cite{Katz_water_injected_plate_2026}. A fragment loses half its speed each time it sweeps up about its own mass per unit area of gas. The blanket is a laminate of about \SI{37}{\gram\per\square\meter}~\cite{nasa_echo_ii_1966}, far less than the spray over the same area, so a shred slows many times over inside the spray rather than at the plate. Stopping it turns its kinetic energy, about \SI{1.8}{\giga\joule\per\kilogram} at \SI{60}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, into heat in the shocked gas around it. The fragment vaporizes if it keeps a few percent of that heat. Fragment survival through the spray has not been computed, so how much solid reaches the plate is not yet known.

Closed-cell foam, possibly polyethylene, fills the outer bag~\cite{rodriguez_perez2005_polyolefin_foams}. The foam's acoustic impedance is far below water's, so the blast cavitates the water at the foam strands~\cite{cole1948underwater} (\autoref{fig:sphere_layers}b). Gas held in crevices on the foam's surface seeds the cavitation~\cite{atchley1989crevice}. This spreads the blast through the bag and breaks the water into small droplets. The droplets then vaporize as they mix with propellant released by the pusher plate~\cite{Katz_water_injected_plate_2026,katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Nozzle PuffSats}\label{sec:nozzle_puffsats}
A PuffSat bound for a pulsed nozzle keeps the tethered electronics of \autoref{sec:pusher_plate_puffsats}, so its electronics again pass outside the target vehicle. Its working mass changes. It must enter the nozzle's chamber through a narrow port, so it is a long, thin, solid rod rather than a necklace of bags. A \SI{10}{\kilogram} polyethylene rod ten times as long as it is wide is \SI{1.10}{\meter} long and \SI{11}{\centi\meter} across~\cite{crc_handbook,katz2026aim}. The port is \SI{15}{\centi\meter} across, which leaves \SI{2}{\centi\meter} of clearance all round (\autoref{fig:rod_port_plug}).

That clearance shapes the rod. The pusher plate needed a disk of gas, and an explosive to make one. The chamber confines the gas itself, so the rod carries no explosive and only has to arrive. It carries no liquid either, because liquid sloshes in its bag and moves the working mass away from where the tethers hold it. A solid rod stays centered in the circle its spinning electronics packages trace. \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation} takes up how the rod is steered into the port. The rod is metallized under a clear coat, and its spin averages the sunlight over its surface. It sits near \SI{299}{\kelvin}, where polyethylene holds its shape~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Past the port the rod strikes a plug of frozen methane. The chamber's gas alone is too thin to stop it, and the rod could reach the far wall intact. A projectile erodes into a target over about its own length times the square root of their density ratio~\cite{birkhoff1948lined}, and that sets the plug's depth. The plug is twice the rod's width, which leaves \SI{5.5}{\centi\meter} for aim error. It is grown porous, as frost is, at about half the solid's density. That makes it lighter and deeper, \SI{18.8}{\kilogram} and \SI{2.35}{\meter}. A shock that crushes a porous solid does extra work closing the voids, so the plug ends hotter and more of it joins the gas as vapor rather than droplets~\cite{zeldovich_raizer,katz2026aim}. The penetration law was derived for dense targets, so the porous plug's depth is an extrapolation that needs a shock-physics simulation.

Everything the rod and plug bring into the chamber leaves through the nozzle as exhaust. The wall caps the gas temperature, and at a fixed temperature exhaust speed falls as the inverse square root of the gas's mean particle mass. The materials are therefore chosen from light, non-toxic elements, hydrogen and carbon in the rod, the plug and the chamber's fill. Methane also adds no oxygen, which would burn a carbon hot face into carbon monoxide. It raises the mean particle mass by \SI{4.8}{\percent}, which costs about \SI{2.3}{\percent} of exhaust speed~\cite{nist_webbook_methane,katz2026aim}. The \SI{2}{\centi\meter} clearance and the plug's size are requirements rather than results. The rod's motion on its tethers has not been simulated.

\section{Jupiter Gravity-Assist Details}\label{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists}
PuffSats fly a trajectory that loops from Earth out to Jupiter and back, and each new departure must find Earth and Jupiter where the last one found them. That forces the round trip to last a whole number of Earth--Jupiter \textbf{synodic periods}, the \SI{1.092}{\year} Earth takes to lap Jupiter and restore the same alignment~\cite{katz2026aim}. Three synodic periods (\SI{3.28}{\year}) and two (\SI{2.18}{\year}) both close, and \autoref{fig:jupiter_loop} draws both loops. A third option flies part of the outbound leg faster and parks it near Earth. If the departure burn is methalox, only the three-synodic cycle is practical. The other two need the higher specific impulse that PuffSat nozzles may reach (\autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}). The trajectory numbers below come from an eleven-cycle chain flown on real planetary ephemerides in the companion code repository~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}, and the parent paper works through them~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Three Synodic Periods}\label{sec:three_synodic_cadence}
Each returning batch of PuffSats splits into two waves. One wave cannot do both jobs. A pusher plate is pushed the way the PuffSats travel, and they return retrograde, against Earth's orbital motion (\autoref{fig:jupiter_loop}). The payload must leave for Jupiter prograde. So the growth wave lifts it the wrong way, and a second wave, met head-on, sends it the right way. The \textbf{growth wave} arrives first and pushes a payload lifted from the ground into a highly eccentric Earth orbit. It meets the payload at \SI{400}{\kilo\meter}, where the air still leaves its tethers cool~\cite{katz2026aim}. The chain flies a ten-day head start, which is the period of that orbit. A twenty-day orbit does within 0.7\% as well~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}, and the burns below are priced on it~\cite{katz2026aim}. The \textbf{departure wave} arrives one orbit later and supplies the burn that sends the payload back toward Jupiter.

Between the two waves the payload has to turn around. The growth wave pushed it retrograde, the way its PuffSats travel, and the departure wave arrives from roughly the opposite direction. So the payload coasts to apoapsis, the orbit's far point, and burns there. At \SI{613000}{\kilo\meter} from Earth it moves at only about \SI{0.12}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so turning is cheap. It then returns to periapsis still moving at nearly escape speed, so the growth push is not lost to the turn. The departure burn starts where the payload is fastest, which is where the Oberth effect makes each unit of $\deltav$ buy the most energy. The same burn lifts its periapsis, the orbit's closest point, to \SI{600}{\kilo\meter}. The growth push had to maximize what reaches orbit on the first lift. The departure leg does not, so it passes higher, where drag cannot disturb the steered PuffSats. The whole reversal costs about 6\% of the departing mass in carried propellant~\cite{katz2026aim}. Panel (a) of \autoref{fig:earth_turnaround} draws the sequence.

The departure wave then meets the payload almost head-on at periapsis. In the payload's frame, the PuffSats' velocity makes an angle of \ang{150.4} with the thrust, where \ang{180} would be exactly head-on. Treating the collision as head-on misstates the delivered mass by 0.9\%~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. Because the payload arrives at periapsis already near escape speed, the four three-synodic cycles in the chain need a burn of only \SIrange{5.32}{5.54}{\kilo\meter\per\second}~\cite{katz2026aim}. That figure is at the parent paper's \SI{200}{\kilo\meter} reference altitude. At \SI{600}{\kilo\meter} each burn needs \SIrange{107}{110}{\meter\per\second} more~\cite{katz2026aim}. A methalox stage at \SI{380}{\second} of specific impulse has an exhaust speed of \SI{3.73}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so it keeps $e^{-\deltav/3.73}$ of the vehicle, or 23 to 24\%. That is expensive but workable. The PuffSats come home at \SIrange{56.53}{57.43}{\kilo\meter\per\second} relative to Earth~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Two Synodic Periods}\label{sec:two_synodic_cadence}
The shorter loop needs a harder turn at Jupiter, so the flyby passes much closer (\autoref{fig:jupiter_loop}). The tightest passes sit at the \SI{4000}{\kilo\meter} altitude floor the chain allows, \num{1.056} Jupiter radii from the planet's center~\cite{katz2026aim}. They pass deeper into the radiation belts that \autoref{sec:deployment_rocket} shields against. In a model with both planets on circular orbits in one plane, a two-synodic cycle always closes~\cite{katz2026aim}. Real orbits are eccentric and inclined, and there Jupiter sometimes cannot bend the path far enough. The departure wave then needs a \textbf{deep-space maneuver}, a burn made in mid-coast rather than at a planet. The chain takes the two-synodic return whenever that maneuver stays under \SI{50}{\meter\per\second} and falls back to three synodic periods otherwise. Four of its eleven cycles fell back. Over two hundred years of real Earth--Jupiter geometry, only 45 of 91 two-synodic windows clear the altitude floor~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The mechanics repeat those of \autoref{sec:three_synodic_cadence}. The departure wave arrives at \ang{160.6} to the thrust, even closer to head-on, and treating it as head-on costs 0.5\%~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. The burn is larger, \SIrange{6.84}{7.17}{\kilo\meter\per\second} across the seven two-synodic cycles~\cite{katz2026aim}. On methalox that keeps 15 to 16\% of the vehicle, which is marginal. The largest burn shows what the Oberth effect buys. It leaves the payload \SI{14.39}{\kilo\meter\per\second} beyond Earth's pull, twice the burn~\cite{katz2026aim}. The PuffSats return faster, at \SIrange{61.83}{65.13}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so they push harder on the next outbound leg~\cite{katz2026aim}.

That burn needs an engine with a higher specific impulse than methalox. PuffSat nozzles (\autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}) supply it. A nuclear thermal rocket could make the head-on burn instead, though it does better on the three-synodic cycle (\autoref{sec:ntr_departure}).

\subsection{Fly And Park}\label{sec:fly_and_park_cadence}
The third option sends the outbound leg on two trajectories. The departure wave keeps the three-synodic clock. The growth wave flies a shorter, faster route and arrives months early. It pushes the payload into an eccentric orbit, and the payload waits there for the departure wave. We call this \textbf{fly-and-park}, following the parent paper~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Left on the twenty-day orbit, the parked payload would dip through the Van Allen radiation belts on every pass~\cite{li2019vanallen}. So a burn at apoapsis raises its periapsis above the outer belt, to about \SI{60000}{\kilo\meter} from Earth's center, for \SI{0.22}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. Before the departure wave arrives, a second apoapsis burn lowers the periapsis to \SI{600}{\kilo\meter}. The same burn turns the orbit so that the payload meets the incoming PuffSats nearly head-on. It costs \SIrange{0.22}{0.46}{\kilo\meter\per\second} depending on the turn. The park therefore adds \SIrange{0.44}{0.68}{\kilo\meter\per\second} in all~\cite{katz2026aim}. Panel (b) of \autoref{fig:earth_turnaround} draws the park. A park that lasts months has time to pay that with solar-electric engines or a lunar flyby.

The park buys two things. The growth wave need not arrive all at once, because each payload can wait in the park for the departure wave. The ground launches it pushes can then be spread over months rather than crowded into the days around a single wave. The faster route also returns PuffSats at about \SI{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so each one pushes more mass outbound. The best case returns 1.27 times the mass of a plain three-synodic cycle~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The faster route costs a larger departure burn, a median \SI{1.04}{\kilo\meter\per\second} more. On methalox that misses breaking even by about 5\%, and fly-and-park beats the plain three-synodic cycle at only 1 of 11 departure phases. At \SI{1200}{\second} of specific impulse it wins at 16 of 30, and at \SI{2214}{\second} it wins at 25 of 30~\cite{katz2026aim}. Those specific impulses are therefore requirements for the PuffSat nozzles of \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}, not results.

\section{Pusher Plates}\label{sec:pusher_plates}
A pusher plate works as Project Orion's did. Gas strikes a plate at the back of the vehicle and pushes it forward~\cite{projorion}. The plate pushes only the way the gas travels, so it suits the overtake, where PuffSats catch the vehicle from behind and their momentum already points forward. Met head-on, a plate would reverse that momentum and brake the vehicle. That is why the departure burn uses the nozzles of \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Spraying Water Into The Pulse}\label{sec:plate_water_spray}
Our plate differs from Orion's in the way that sets its performance. The vehicle sprays its own water into the arriving PuffSat gas ahead of the plate (\autoref{fig:plate_stack})~\cite{Katz_water_injected_plate_2026,katz2026aim}. Write $k$ for the slug ratio, the kilograms of carried water per kilogram of arriving PuffSat, and $w$ for the closing speed. A PuffSat of mass $m$ that bounced perfectly off a bare plate would deliver an impulse of $2mw$. With the water added, the mixture's center of mass approaches the plate at only $w/(1+k)$, but the collision has heated it. The hot gas expands off the plate as well as bouncing, and the two motions add as energies rather than as speeds. If all the incoming kinetic energy leaves as backward exhaust at one speed, that speed is $w/\sqrt{1+k}$ and the impulse is~\cite{katz2026aim}
\begin{equation}
    J_{\max} = mw\left(1+\sqrt{1+k}\right).
    \label{eq:plate_ceiling}
\end{equation}
At $k=8.5$, the parent paper's working figure, the ceiling is $4.1\,mw$, about twice a bare bounce~\cite{katz2026aim}. The exhaust leaves at about a third of the closing speed, so the exhaust's specific impulse falls. That trade is worth making because the scarce mass is the PuffSat returning from Jupiter, not water lifted from the ground. The value $k=8.5$ comes from the parent's launch-mass budget rather than from optimizing the plate~\cite{katz2026aim}.

No real plate reaches the ceiling. Some gas spills past the rim, and some energy leaves as radiation. The parent writes the delivered impulse as $mw(\eta_{\mathrm{jet}}\sqrt{1+k}+1)$, where the jet efficiency $\eta_{\mathrm{jet}}$ collects those losses. Its performance target of $\eta_{\mathrm{jet}}=0.775$ gives $3.39\,mw$ per PuffSat~\cite{katz2026aim}. That target is a requirement, not a result.

Water is the most convenient spray, because it is cheap and stores without refrigeration. The collision heats the mixture enough to split water into hydrogen and oxygen, and energy held in broken bonds pushes nothing. We assume that energy comes back. The gas is still dense as it cools, and recombination needs three bodies to meet, so its rate per molecule rises as the square of the density. Atoms that recombine while the gas still presses on the plate return their bond energy to the push. This is an assumption. The parent's finite-rate calculation, at the slowest closing speed of \SI{45.58}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and $k=8.5$, returned 23 to 31\% of the stored bond energy by \SI{250}{\micro\second}~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026,katz2026aim}. That calculation layered the water against a flat wall. Mixing ahead of the plate changes how the gas cools, and which way it moves the return has not been computed.

If the energy does not come back, argon is the alternative~\cite{katz2026aim}. A single atom has no bonds to break, and argon is harmless in the atmosphere. It costs more to handle, because it is carried as liquid argon. It also runs hotter at the plate. Temperature measures energy per particle, while the closing speed fixes energy per kilogram. Split water averages 6 atomic mass units per particle, since each molecule becomes two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen, and an argon atom has 40. A kilogram of argon therefore shares the same energy among about a sixth as many particles~\cite{katz2026aim}. Each atom arrives carrying about six times the energy of a water fragment, as a bowling ball carries more than a tennis ball thrown at the same speed. When the gas stops against the plate, that energy becomes heat, and the argon ends hotter.

\subsection{A Thin Ablative Film}\label{sec:plate_ablative_film}
As on Orion, a liquid ablative film is sprayed onto the plate before each pulse, and the hot gas consumes it instead of the steel beneath~\cite{projorion,ga5009_vol3}. Orion's film was about \SI{150}{\micro\meter} thick, matched to bomb plasma arriving at \SIrange{100}{200}{\kilo\meter\per\second}~\cite{ga5009_vol3,katz2026aim}. The parent paper sets the thickness by opacity instead of by ablated mass. The film must hold enough dispersed carbon to stop the pulse's light before it reaches the metal. At a fifth carbon by mass, a \SIrange{1}{2}{\micro\meter} coat has an optical depth $\tau$ of 1.5 to 3 at \SI{550}{\nano\meter}~\cite{bond2006_lac_absorption,katz2026aim}. Optical depth is unitless, and a coat passes a fraction $e^{-\tau}$ of the light, so this one passes \SIrange{5}{22}{\percent}.

We take about \SI{5}{\micro\meter}, and the margin is not the main reason. Between pulses, the vehicle shines near-infrared light on the plate and measures what the metal reflects back through the film (\autoref{fig:plate_stack}). A thinner remaining film returns more light, so the image maps where the pulse wore the coat away, and the sprayer refills only those zones~\cite{katz2026aim}. Carbon absorbs less at longer wavelengths~\cite{bond2006_lac_absorption}, so infrared light can cross a film a few microns thick and return. Much thicker films return too little light to read. The \SI{5}{\micro\meter} figure is a requirement set by that readout, not a measured limit.

The thin film is an optimization, and the plate does not depend on it. The parent's reference plate is \SI{5}{\meter} across, sized for a vehicle like a small private jet in its 200-mile case~\cite{katz2026aim}. A data-center vehicle may need a larger plate, and this paper does not size one. The parent's \SI{25}{\square\meter} reference plate meets pulses of about \SI{100}{\kilogram}~\cite{katz2026aim}. A \SI{5}{\micro\meter} film on it is about \SI{125}{\gram} of liquid, near 0.1\% of the pulse. Orion's full \SI{150}{\micro\meter} film would be about \SI{4}{\kilogram}, or 4\%. A larger plate carries more film, but it also meets heavier pulses, so the film's share should hold as long as pulse mass grows with plate area. The thin film shows what modern sensing adds to Orion's scheme. Orion's more conservative film works too, at a few percent of each pulse.

\subsection{Pulse Rate And Plate Mass}\label{sec:plate_pulse_rate}
The gas each pulse leaves behind limits the pulse rate. The next PuffSat flies through it, and the dose on its surface rises as the square of the rate, from \SI{75.6}{\kilo\joule\per\square\meter} at \SI{2}{\hertz} to \SI{302}{\kilo\joule\per\square\meter} at \SI{4}{\hertz}~\cite{katz2026aim}. At \SI{4}{\hertz} the PuffSats survive it, because most of the dose arrives in the last \SI{0.3}{\second} (\autoref{sec:pusher_plate_puffsats}).

A higher rate buys a lighter plate. Each pulse kicks the plate, which rides back toward the vehicle on its shock absorbers until the next one. For a vehicle of mass $M$ and mean acceleration $a$, pulsed at rate $\nu$, the plate's mass $m$ and the stroke $s$ it travels satisfy $ms\sim Ma/\nu^2$~\cite{katz2026aim}. At a fixed stroke, plate mass falls as the square of the pulse rate. It can fall only to the mass the plate needs to survive each pulse, of order tens of kilograms per square meter. At \SI{4}{\hertz} and a stroke of a few meters, the dynamics hold the plate near a tenth of the vehicle's mass, well above that survival floor~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Aiming Between Pulses}\label{sec:plate_aiming}
Between pulses the vehicle must line up on the next PuffSat (\autoref{sec:guidance_navigation}), and the plate can help steer it. The parent's impact simulation finds that the plate couples best when the gas arrives as a flat disk~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026}. The disk can be only so narrow. In the parent's geometry sweep at \SI{16}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, the best survivable footprint covered about half the plate's radius. Squeezing it to three tenths gained almost nothing and loaded the plate past its strength~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026,katz2026aim}.

Tilting the plate slightly sends the rebounding gas off axis, and its recoil pushes the vehicle sideways. On its own, that push would also turn the vehicle. Moving the impact point off the plate's center cancels the turn, because the total force then passes through the center of mass (\autoref{fig:plate_aiming})~\cite{katz2026aim}. Shifting mass inside the vehicle or firing its reaction-control thrusters can do the same job. Pulse strength gives a second control. The flattest disk bounces best, so firing a PuffSat's charges slightly early weakens that pulse. Thrusters on the vehicle make the remaining corrections. They are required when a PuffSat is lost too late for the formation to fill its slot (\autoref{sec:pusher_plate_puffsats}).

\subsection{Plate Construction}\label{sec:plate_construction}
The plate can be made of ordinary steel. Its stroke already sets its mass well above what survival needs, so a lighter metal saves nothing~\cite{katz2026aim}. Steel conducts heat better than titanium, so the same pulse heats a steel face less, and the face cracks less over thousands of pulses~\cite{katz2026aim}. The parent paper builds the plate from separate petals that are clamped rather than welded. A weld halves the stress swing steel survives for two million cycles~\cite{iiw_fatigue_2008}, and clamped petals leave each one room to grow as it heats~\cite{katz2026aim}. Most of a plate's cost is forming, machining, inspection, and latches rather than metal~\cite{katz2026aim}. Rolling and cutting steel plate already scales well, and unit costs generally fall as cumulative production grows, an effect first measured in aircraft~\cite{wright1936_airplane_cost}. How far the cost of plates would fall is speculation.

Behind the plate sit gas springs (\autoref{fig:plate_stack}), as on Orion, whose first stage was a set of toroidal pneumatic bags~\cite{projorion}. Modern fibers make the bag walls much lighter, because wall mass falls in proportion to the fiber's strength per unit density. Vectran HT breaks at \SI{3.2}{\giga\pascal} at a density of \SI{1.4}{\gram\per\cubic\centi\meter}, and a Vectran wall has a ninth the mass of a steel one~\cite{vectran_engineering_brochure,katz2026aim}. Vectran also resists creep, which keeps the springs tuned under a steady preload. It outlasted aramids in the maker's fold-endurance test~\cite{vectran_engineering_brochure}. It keeps half its strength only up to \SI{145}{\celsius}, so the bags sit behind ceramic felt, and Kevlar is the fallback if the back of the plate runs too hot~\cite{vectran_engineering_brochure,katz2026aim}.

A gas spring is nearly lossless. Each pulse reverses the plate's velocity symmetrically, so the spring passes momentum to the vehicle while dissipating almost no energy~\cite{katz2026aim}. With a plate a tenth of the vehicle, pulses at \SI{4}{\hertz}, and a mean of \SI{3}{g}, a tuned spring peaks at \SI{3.6}{g} over a \SI{2.5}{\meter} stroke~\cite{katz2026aim}. A crewed vehicle would need Orion's second stage to smooth the vibration. Uncrewed data-center hardware needs only a cap on peak load. A single stage meets the parent's \SI{10}{g} cap, which allows a mean near \SI{6.4}{g}~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Reusing The Plate}\label{sec:plate_reuse}
The first plates need not be reused. A reusable plate adds a replaceable facing of \SI{1}{\milli\meter} steel tiles under the ablative film (\autoref{fig:plate_stack})~\cite{katz2026aim}. The tiles take the particle damage, and each is inspected and replaced on its own between flights. On the parent's \SI{25}{\square\meter} reference plate, a full facing weighs \SI{196}{\kilogram}, about 6\% of the plate~\cite{katz2026aim}. A data-center plate would carry a heavier facing, and the share it adds depends on how that plate's own mass scales.

The tiles must not weld to the plate. Clean metal can cold-weld in vacuum, and fretting, the small repeated sliding of a loaded joint, raises the adhesion by breaking the surface oxide~\cite{merstallinger_cold_welding}. The parent's baseline is an interleaf of Vespel SP-3, a polyimide filled with molybdenum disulfide, which showed no adhesion to stainless steel in ESA's fretting tests~\cite{merstallinger_cold_welding,katz2026aim}. The interleaf may be ground to powder over many pulses, so it must not shed carcinogenic dust or fibers. An asbestos gasket would be ruled out. The constraint matters if people replace the tiles, and less if robots do.
\section{Pulsed Rocket Nozzles}\label{sec:rocket_nozzles}
The departure wave meets the payload nearly head-on (\autoref{sec:three_synodic_cadence}). A pusher plate would reverse that wave's momentum and brake the vehicle (\autoref{sec:pusher_plates}), so the departure burn needs a device that turns the collision's heat into thrust toward the incoming PuffSats. That device is a pulsed nozzle. Each rod of \autoref{sec:nozzle_puffsats} enters a chamber through a narrow port and turns to hot gas there, and the gas leaves through a throat at the back~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The wall sets the limit. A pusher plate meets a pulse for a moment, but in a chamber the hot gas presses against the wall until it has drained through the throat. That takes 25 to \SI{56}{\milli\second} per pulse~\cite{katz2026aim}, so the gas cannot be made arbitrarily hot. A magnetic nozzle, a shaped field that turns the plasma with no wall in contact, would lift that limit~\cite{ahedo2010magnetic}. It appears far from maturity. The published figures for its efficiency bound it from above rather than measure it. The measured impulses come from half-joule laser shots, and the two pendulum methods used to take them disagree~\cite{koba2025measurement,katz2026aim}. The field's structure also runs to tens of tonnes on the Jupiter pulses~\cite{katz2026aim}. We therefore take the nearer-term route of a material chamber.

We assume the gas peaks at \SI{10000}{\kelvin} in each pulse, a requirement rather than a result. Carlo Rubbia's Project 242, a fission-fragment rocket, is the closest precedent. It heats hydrogen at a few bar in steady flow behind a porous carbon wall that the incoming hydrogen cools, and it names a radiative ceiling near \SI{9500}{\kelvin}~\cite{augelli2013project242}. Our pulses last tens of milliseconds, two to four times a second, and reach about \SI{900}{\bar}. The higher pressure matters for a second reason. At these temperatures much of the gas's energy is stored in broken molecules, and it returns as thrust only if they recombine in the nozzle's expanding section. Recombination needs three particles to meet, so its rate climbs steeply with density~\cite{katz2026aim}. Project 242 already relies on it. Its \SI{2700}{\second} baseline needs \SI{351}{\mega\joule\per\kilogram}, while hot hydrogen at \SI{10000}{\kelvin} holds only \SI{206}{\mega\joule\per\kilogram} without the energy of dissociation, so about two thirds of that energy must return~\cite{augelli2013project242,katz2026aim}.

The wall is layered so that each job goes to a different material~\cite{katz2026aim}. A \SI{5}{\milli\meter} liner of GRCop-84, a copper alloy that NASA developed for rocket chambers, faces the gas and moves its heat into coolant passages cut into it. A \SI{25}{\milli\meter} Cr--Mo steel shell backs the liner. A carbon-fiber overwrap outside the steel carries most of the pressure, with a thin aluminum layer between them and a glass-fiber skin outside. The vessel is pressurized once past the steel's yield before service. That stretches the steel permanently, so the carbon holds it in compression and each pulse swings it from compression to tension. The pulse heat reaches only half a millimeter into the copper. The copper over each passage bends under the \SI{900}{\bar} peak, but it can be sized to take local spikes three times that at half its yield~\cite{katz2026aim}. Where heating is worst, around the throat and the entrance, replaceable inserts carry a graphite coating sprayed on between pulses, and each pulse sublimes part of it away. The wall's mass tracks the pressure times the volume it holds, and so the pulse energy. For hydrogen at \SI{75}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, the layered wall masses \SI{14.8}{\tonne} for a \SI{1}{\kilogram} rod and \SI{130}{\tonne} for a \SI{10}{\kilogram} one~\cite{katz2026aim}. The \SI{10}{\kilogram} rod carries \SI{28}{\giga\joule}, about \SI{7}{\tonne} of TNT, and its chamber outweighs the \SI{100}{\tonne} reference craft.

\autoref{tab:nozzle_isp} compares three working gases. The table gives effective specific impulse, which charges each pulse for the momentum its rod carried in, since the rod arrives head-on. Hydrogen leaves fastest, but it pays the largest charge. Its slug ratio, the mass of gas per mass of rod, is lowest, so the rod's momentum is shared among the fewest kilograms of exhaust. The debit is \SI{985}{\second} for hydrogen and \SI{391}{\second} for methane~\cite{katz2026aim}. All three gain 18 to 25\% from the narrower, higher-pressure throat. The narrow throat pays in wear. A \SI{2}{\square\meter} throat grows 10\% in area after 231 to 262 pulses and may need replacing within one burn~\cite{katz2026aim}.

We take methane as the baseline, though hydrogen doubles the fleet about 7\% faster at the narrow throat~\cite{katz2026aim}. Methane locks more of each pulse into chemical bonds, so less becomes pressure, 18.9\% of the pulse energy against hydrogen's 25.7\%. The wall is lighter in proportion. Methane also keeps pressurized hydrogen gas off the steel between pulses, and it carries no oxygen to burn the graphite. The same charge cools the wall on its way in. It is pumped through two rows of liner passages, the first against the steel, which keeps the overwrap's resin below its limit. It then carries the wall heat back into the chamber, where the pulse turns it into exhaust~\cite{katz2026aim}. Some of it boils doing so. The rest can enter as droplets sprayed just before impact. Drops of half a millimeter or more keep a third to three quarters of their mass. They can be placed to soften the shocks that reach the wall, and a heavier charge gives more mass to place~\cite{katz2026aim}. Thin films seal both openings between pulses, so the charge cannot leak out early. The arriving rod punctures the entrance film, and the pulse bursts the throat film~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Part of each pulse leaks back out the entrance. The entrance and the throat are both choked, so they divide the flow roughly by area. At \SI{10}{\kilogram} the entrance is 3.5 to 4.7\% of the throat~\cite{katz2026aim}. The next rod flies up that leak, but only the wasted gas lies in its path. At \SI{2}{\hertz} the leak has spread 8 to \SI{10}{\kilo\meter} upstream by the time the rod meets it. It then puts less than a ten-billionth of the rod's own mass per unit area in its path~\cite{katz2026aim}. The cost is aim. The rod must pass a \SI{15}{\centi\meter} port with \SI{2}{\centi\meter} to spare, far tighter than a pusher plate needs (\autoref{sec:guidance_navigation}). The parent paper also considers a fast-rotating disk that would partly close the entrance after the rod passes~\cite{katz2026aim}. We leave it out of this nearer-term baseline.

The chamber is also the moving mass of the shock absorber. A departure that carries only PuffSats has no crew to protect, and PuffSats can ride 10 to \SI{20}{g}. That allows a jerky ride at \SI{2}{\hertz}, which the parent paper calls burst and coast~\cite{katz2026aim}. Each pulse drives the chamber back against a gas spring. A latch catches it at full compression, and a damper bleeds off the stored energy before the next rod arrives. For a \SI{25}{\tonne} chamber on a \SI{100}{\tonne} craft, one \SI{10}{\kilogram} pulse sets the chamber moving at \SI{35}{\meter\per\second}. The spring then stores \SI{12}{\mega\joule} over a stroke of \SI{2.5}{\meter} at \SI{10}{g}, or \SI{1.2}{\meter} at \SI{20}{g}~\cite{katz2026aim}. A heavier chamber recoils more slowly and needs less stroke, so its mass does double duty. Burst and coast does not make the absorber lighter than smooth running. What it buys is control. Each pulse starts with the chamber at rest, so the port sits where the rod expects it. The ship measures each pulse's impulse and trims the next charge to correct it. The damper's heat is 0.04\% of the pulse, which the next charge carries away~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Pulse size is a trade~\cite{katz2026aim}. A smaller rod needs a lighter wall, since the wall's mass tracks the pulse energy. Other costs do not shrink with the rod. The entrance clearance is set by aim, so the entrance grows as a share of the throat, to about 16\% in the \SI{1}{\kilogram} chamber. More of each pulse then leaks forward. The \SI{250}{\gram} of guidance packages is 2.5\% of a \SI{10}{\kilogram} rod and 25\% of a \SI{1}{\kilogram} one. At a fixed thrust the wall takes the same average heat over less area, 4.6 times the flux per square meter for a rod ten times lighter. A \SI{1}{\kilogram} rod at the same thrust must also fire at \SI{20}{\hertz}, while the chamber takes 31 to \SI{56}{\milli\second} to empty. No calculation yet picks the best size. It lies between the two ends priced above, a chamber of \SI{15}{\tonne} that must cycle fast and one of \SI{130}{\tonne} that outweighs the craft.

Two unknowns set how well the chamber competes with the reactor of \autoref{sec:ntr_departure}. The chamber leaves on an escape trajectory, and PuffSats must brake it and bring it back for reuse, at a cost in PuffSats that has not been priced. Coupling has not been measured either. The simulation's losses are modeled, not tested, and a 10\% shortfall in exhaust speed costs the methane chamber 15\% of its effective specific impulse~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\section{A Nuclear Thermal Departure}\label{sec:ntr_departure}
The head-on departure burn can also be made by a nuclear thermal rocket, a fission reactor that heats hydrogen and expels it through an ordinary nozzle. Nothing collides, so there is no coupling problem to solve, and the engine's performance rests on ground tests rather than a simulation. The Rover and NERVA programs tested 22 reactors in the 1960s, though none has flown~\cite{nasem2021nuclear}. NASA's Mars reference engine is carried at \SI{906}{\second} of specific impulse and \SI{111}{\kilo\newton} of thrust~\cite{borowski2013ntp,katz2026aim}. A reactor needs no projectiles, so the departure wave goes away and every returning PuffSat can push~\cite{katz2026aim}. NASA has made space fission a priority. The reactor it announced in 2026, SR-1 Freedom, powers electric thrusters rather than heating propellant. The national initiative announced with it keeps thermal rockets in scope and directs work on components common to both~\cite{ans2026_sr1,smith2026_nuclear_initiative}.

We require low-enriched fuel, below 20\% uranium-235. United States policy allows highly enriched uranium only when a mission would not be viable with other fuels~\cite{spd6_2020}, and low-enriched fuel keeps the launch in the lower approval tier described below. It costs mass. NASA's reference engine assumes a 93\% enriched core~\cite{borowski2013ntp}, while General Atomics caps its low-enriched reactor at \SI{3500}{\kilogram} for \SI{57}{\kilo\newton}~\cite{ga_ntp}. No nuclear thermal core of this fuel has been tested~\cite{nasem2021nuclear}. The reactor's thrust is also lower than the chamber's. Enough engines to finish the burn in the chamber's five minutes would weigh 37\% of the stack, so the best burn lasts 42 to 53 minutes instead. Much of it happens away from periapsis, where the Oberth effect gives less, and that costs about \SI{600}{\meter\per\second}. The reactor therefore does best on the three-synodic cycle, whose \SI{5.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second} burn is the smaller one. With its engines and hydrogen tanks expended every cycle, a low-enriched reactor doubles the fleet in 2.15 to \SI{2.53}{\year}, depending on tank mass. Methalox on the same model takes \SI{3.64}{\year}~\cite{katz2026aim}. Those figures do not yet charge the cooldown hydrogen or the full kick allowance described below, which lengthen the doubling by about 5\% and 1.2\%~\cite{katz2026aim}. Whether the pulsed chamber beats the reactor turns on what it costs to bring the chamber back after each burn and on how well a real pulse couples. \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles} takes up both.

The reactor lights only once the stack has escaped Earth, so it never goes critical on a bound orbit. At its \SI{600}{\kilo\meter} periapsis the stack is \SI{60}{\meter\per\second} short of escape. The burn is centered on periapsis, though, so ignition comes 21 to 26 minutes earlier, where the stack moves at only 7.7 to \SI{8.3}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. There each meter per second adds less orbital energy, and a methalox kick needs 75 to \SI{80}{\meter\per\second} to reach escape. Above that floor the kick's size is a safety choice. Each extra meter per second leaves the stack further past escape if it explodes early in the burn. We carry an allowance of \SI{150}{\meter\per\second}, 3.9\% of the stack in methalox, and leave the exact size to the safety review~\cite{katz2026aim}. Before ignition the core is fresh uranium, which is barely radioactive. If the kick fails, the reactor stays cold, and a fresh core on a bound orbit is no worse than one on the launch pad. That holds only if a cold core cannot go critical by accident. The United Nations principles ask that a reactor stay subcritical through ``rocket explosion, re-entry, impact on ground or water, submersion in water or water intruding into the core''~\cite{un1992nps}, and we adopt that as a design requirement.

In nominal operation the spent stage falls into Jupiter. The burn aims the whole stack at the planet. After separation the payload, not the stage, makes a correction of \SIrange{2.3}{10}{\meter\per\second} that moves it out to its flyby. The stage fires nothing after the burn, so its safe outcome needs no second device to work. If the payload's correction fails, the payload falls into Jupiter too~\cite{katz2026aim}. Jupiter's gravity widens the target to a capture radius of about \SI{265000}{\kilo\meter}. On circular, coplanar orbits, the burn can end about \SI{6}{\meter\per\second} off along its direction and the stage still hits~\cite{katz2026aim}. The engine's cooldown is the largest threat to that margin. After shutdown the core keeps making heat as its fission products decay, about \SI{8}{\mega\watt} a minute later and 0.7 to \SI{0.8}{\mega\watt} after an hour~\cite{way1948decay,katz2026aim}. The engine has no radiator, so it keeps pumping hydrogen through the core for hours. NASA's Mars studies budget that cooldown hydrogen at 3\% of the usable propellant~\cite{borowski2012neo}. Leaving through the nozzle, it would push the spent stage 90 to \SI{150}{\meter\per\second} off its aim. We therefore vent it through opposed nozzles, so that their thrusts cancel. No nuclear thermal rocket has flown such a vent, so it is a proposal~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Galileo and Cassini ended the same way. NASA steered Galileo into Jupiter in 2003, with two plutonium-powered generators aboard, so that it could never strike Europa~\cite{nasa_galileo}. Cassini carried three plutonium-238 generators~\cite{nasa_cassini_rtg} and was sent into Saturn in 2017 so that it could not contaminate Enceladus or Titan~\cite{nasa_cassini_finale}. The international planetary protection policy puts Jupiter in its Category II, bodies where ``there is only a remote chance that contamination carried by a spacecraft could compromise future investigations''~\cite{cospar_pp_moon}. The Outer Space Treaty asks states to explore celestial bodies ``so as to avoid their harmful contamination'' and does not define the term~\cite{outer_space_treaty}. We found no ruling on sending a fission reactor into a gas giant. Both precedents carried radioisotope sources, and a spent core carries fission products instead. We assume the treaty permits it, as it permitted Galileo and Cassini, but no agency has said so.

Two accidents remain~\cite{katz2026aim}. The first is a burn that stops early, an ordinary engine failure. The stage is then past escape but short of Jupiter, on a solar orbit that crosses Earth's. It needs more than a year to come back, and more likely years to decades. The chance of a hit on any one pass has not been computed. A full burn leaves 100 to 140 curies of caesium-137 and strontium-90 in each engine. Counting the shorter-lived products, a core stopped late in its burn holds about 3,200 curies a year after shutdown and 200 after ten~\cite{katz2026aim}. For scale, the Chernobyl accident released 30,000 to 40,000 times one engine's caesium-137~\cite{unscear2000annexj,katz2026aim}. In 1964 the SNAP-9A generator of a Transit satellite burned up in the upper atmosphere and spread \num{17000} curies of plutonium-238 around the world~\cite{hardy1972snap9a}. A returning stage would be a local hazard rather than a global one. Much of it would burn up high in the atmosphere, and pieces could reach the ground, as some of the Soviet reactor satellite Cosmos 954 did over northern Canada in 1978~\cite{healthcanada_cosmos954,katz2026aim}.

The second accident is an explosion early in the burn. With the full allowance the stack is 70 to \SI{75}{\meter\per\second} past escape at ignition, and a fragment thrown backward faster than that stays bound to Earth. NASA's standard breakup model, fitted to explosions of rocket stages and satellites in orbit, puts the median fragment speed at 36 to \SI{56}{\meter\per\second}~\cite{johnson2001breakup}. Drawn from that model, 4 to 8\% of fragments stay bound after an explosion at ignition. The burn keeps adding speed, so the share falls to 0.5 to 2.3\% a minute in and to almost none after five minutes~\cite{katz2026aim}. The fission inventory grows the other way, from almost nothing at ignition, so the hazard sits in the first minute or two of the burn. This is the accident closest to this paper's subject. Bound fragments pass through low Earth orbit at every perigee, and most climb past the high orbits of \autoref{sec:data_center_attacks} on the way out. Where they end up after the Moon and Sun reshape their orbits has not been computed~\cite{katz2026aim}.

In the United States, launching the reactor would need authorization under the 2019 presidential memorandum on space nuclear systems, NSPM-20. Its stated policy is that the country ``shall develop and use space nuclear systems when such systems safely enable or enhance space exploration or operational capabilities.'' A low-enriched reactor falls in its second tier. The head of the sponsoring agency authorizes that launch after an interagency nuclear safety review. It moves to the third tier if the safety analysis puts the chance of exposing anyone to more than 25 rem at one in a million or more. A third-tier launch needs the President or the White House science office. A rem is a unit of radiation dose, and 100 rem is one sievert. The analysis covers accidents after launch, ``for instance, in an unplanned reentry from Earth orbit or during an Earth flyby''~\cite{nspm20_2019}. NASA's nuclear flight safety handbook adds that an accidental disposal caused by an operational failure should still meet the mission's tier~\cite{nasa_hdbk_8715_26}. A burn that stops short of Jupiter is that case. The two accidents above are therefore what the review would score, and how often a burn stops early or explodes has not been computed~\cite{katz2026aim}.
\section{Guidance And Navigation}\label{sec:guidance_navigation}
Release from the deployment rocket (\autoref{sec:deployment_rocket}) spaces the PuffSats so that they arrive over the few minutes of a push. A PuffSat released with a small velocity change drifts ahead of or behind its neighbors by that change times the coast time. Spreading a four-minute push this way takes at most \SI{4.4}{\meter\per\second} per PuffSat~\cite{katz2026aim}. The rocket pays that change, not the PuffSat. It can push PuffSats off in matched forward and backward pairs, so that its own momentum does not change, or burn between releases. Either way costs it under \SI{10}{\meter\per\second}. The stream is aimed at Earth, so the rocket must then leave it. A \SI{7}{\meter\per\second} burn at release moves its arrival \SI{10000}{\kilo\meter} to the side, clear of the atmosphere~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The same release spacing can open gaps of one to two seconds in the formation wherever a satellite would pass too close to it~\cite{katz2026aim}. After the push, most PuffSat debris, including the electronics that pass the plate, continues on heliocentric orbits that cross Jupiter's~\cite{katz2026aim}. Jupiter's gravity is more likely to perturb this debris than Earth is to meet it again, so a later strike on a satellite is unlikely.

\section{Data Centers, Anti-Satellite Weapons, And Launch Costs}\label{sec:data_center_attacks}
Data centers are now military targets. In March 2026, Iranian drones struck Amazon Web Services data centers in the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. Project Suncatcher~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher} and Starcloud~\cite{foust2026_starcloud_fcc} both plan sun-synchronous orbits, Starcloud in shells at \qtyrange{600}{850}{\kilo\meter}. In 2007, China destroyed the Fengyun-1C weather satellite at \qty{865}{\kilo\meter} with a ground-launched missile~\cite{kan2007_asat,nasa_odqn2007_fengyun}, and China and Russia both now field such weapons~\cite{dia2022_space_security}. A sun-synchronous orbit passes over nearly every latitude~\cite{nasa_orbit_catalog}, so attackers get repeated chances and the interceptor arrives within minutes.

One reason satellite attacks are rare is that the military benefits aren't worth the cost of hitting dispersed targets~\cite{samson2025_swf_counterspace,hallex2020_proliferated_constellations}. The director of the US Space Development Agency says ``it will cost more to shoot down a single satellite than it will cost to build that single satellite''~\cite{hadley2023_tournear}. An orbital data center cannot spread out that way, because its nodes need high-bandwidth links to each other. Suncatcher's illustrative cluster puts 81 satellites, \qty{2.3}{\mega\watt} of computing, within a \qty{1}{\kilo\meter} radius~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. Nor can it hide. Homing on radar and infrared returns is within reach of many countries~\cite{wright2005_physics_space_security}. Starcloud's panels would span about \qty{4}{\kilo\meter} on a side~\cite{nvidia2025_starcloud}, and a data center sheds nearly all its power as heat, so a seeker has a conspicuous radar and infrared target.

Distance does not hide a data center, but it buys warning time. Reaching a Lagrange point takes \qty{11.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} against \qty{3.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second} to reach \qty{650}{\kilo\meter}~\cite{katz2026aim}. Even at \qty{14}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, an interceptor needs half a day to reach Earth--Moon L4/L5 and two days to reach Sun--Earth L1. That is time to disperse a cluster, or to launch interceptors based with it, like the escorts the U.S. Space Force plans for its own satellites~\cite{easley2025_ussf_framework}. In September 2026, the Secretary of the Air Force said that the United States has weapons in orbit, without saying what they are~\cite{ap2026_meink_space_weapons}. Whether they include a defense of this kind is not public.

Latency decides which workloads go where. High Earth orbit at \qtyrange{20000}{80000}{\kilo\meter} adds \qtyrange{0.13}{0.53}{\second} of round-trip delay and still gives \qty{0.5}{\hour} to \qty{14}{\hour} of warning, so it suits latency-sensitive inference. AI training and batch inference, including long-running coding agents, can go to the Lagrange points. The \qtyrange{2.6}{10}{\second} round trip there is far inside the \qty{24}{\hour} turnaround that batch inference services already offer~\cite{anthropic_message_batches,openai_batch_api}. Sun--Earth L1 has no eclipses at all~\cite{nasa_lagrange_points}, but the James Webb Space Telescope's always sunny halo orbit at Sun--Earth L2 shows other libration-point orbits can also avoid ever falling into shadow~\cite{nasa_webb_orbit}. The cost is exposure to solar particle events outside the magnetosphere~\cite{li2019vanallen}. Suncatcher's proton test ran a commercial tensor processing unit to 15~krad(Si) without hard failures~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}.

Starship~\cite{starship} can reach high orbits, but launch costs near term are uncompetitive with terrestrial data centers. Starcloud's chief executive puts competitive pricing near \$500 per \si{\kilo\gram}~\cite{bennici2026_johnston}. SpaceX's IPO underwriters, who have reason to be optimistic~\cite{michaely1999_underwriter}, forecast \$183 (Goldman Sachs~\cite{pethokoukis2026_goldman}) and about \$500 (Morgan Stanley~\cite{investing2026_ms_spacex}) per \si{\kilo\gram} to low Earth orbit in 2030. Sun--Earth L1 lies a further \qtyrange{3.2}{4.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} out, paid for with propellant from two to four tanker flights. Each kilogram delivered there costs 3.5 to 4.6 kilograms launched to low Earth orbit~\cite{katz2026aim}, turning the forecasts into \$640--840 and \$1750--2300 per \si{\kilo\gram}. High circular orbits are generally harder still. Circularizing at \qty{22000}{\kilo\meter} takes \qty{1.45}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, and leaving takes \qty{1.46}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, against about \qty{30}{\meter\per\second} to leave Sun--Earth L1~\cite{katz2026aim}.

These estimates are floors. They count the tanker propellant but not the logistics and operations of orbital refueling, which SpaceX lists as a material challenge~\cite{spacex2026_s1a}. A ship back from L1 enters at \qty{11.1}{\kilo\meter\per\second} rather than \qty{8.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, about 2.7 times the peak heating~\cite{katz2026aim}, on a heat shield SpaceX's chief executive called Starship's biggest challenge~\cite{musk2024_heatshield}. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} argues that PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can meet the \$500 per \si{\kilo\gram} target in high orbits.
