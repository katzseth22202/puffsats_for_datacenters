\begin{abstract}\label{sec:abstract}
% Elevator pitch. The hook, then just enough context to know what the paper is.
% The siting argument, its citations, and the cost case belong in the body.
% See docs/brief_guide.md and CONTEXT.md.
High-thrust, high-specific-impulse propulsion is a long-standing goal of rocket design. Nuclear-pulse concepts such as Project Orion~\cite{projorion} showed in principle how externally supplied, high-energy pulses could shatter the limits of chemical rockets, but at the unacceptable cost of using nuclear explosives.

This paper introduces \textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion}, a non-nuclear analogue of Orion in which small satellites, dubbed PuffSats, are sent on Jupiter gravity-assist trajectories that return them to Earth at \SIrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, corresponding to \SIrange{1.5}{2.3}{\giga\joule\per\kilogram} of kinetic energy. Shortly before intercepting the target vehicle, each PuffSat disperses into gas, which strikes a pusher plate. Additional propellant carried by the target vehicle is injected into and mixed with the resulting plasma, increasing the momentum transferred per kilogram of returning PuffSat mass. Alternatively, the incoming material can mix with injected propellant inside a pulsed nozzle chamber, allowing the resulting expansion to produce directed thrust, including thrust toward the oncoming PuffSat.

We develop one candidate PuffSat architecture and evaluate its performance. As a motivating application, we consider the launch of large orbital data centers and examine whether such a system could achieve launch costs competitive with near-term fully reusable launch vehicles such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship}. The companion paper \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim} explores the broader architectural implications.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Pushing a spacecraft with mass supplied from outside it is an old idea. Pellet-beam propulsion uses external accelerators to direct high-speed mass toward a spacecraft. Project Orion employed nuclear explosions to drive plasma into a pusher plate. \textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} instead uses Jupiter gravity assists to accelerate small satellites, which return to Earth at \SIrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. Shortly before reaching the target vehicle, each satellite disperses into gas whose momentum and energy can be used for propulsion. The companion paper \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim} compares this approach with earlier pellet beam proposals.

Large orbital data centers motivate the application examined here. Their choice of orbit must account for both communication latency and exposure to attack. Iran's attacks on terrestrial computing infrastructure establish a precedent for targeting data centers in wartime.   Demonstrated anti-satellite capabilities establish the reach of terrestrial weapons into low Earth orbit (\autoref{sec:data_center_attacks}). Distant orbits provide additional warning and response time, enabling far greater resilience to deliberate assault. We compare speculative early PuffSat launch costs with those of a reusable rocket system serving the same destinations (see \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation}).

PuffSats returning to Earth at extreme speeds can bootstrap subsequent generations. If each returning generation delivers enough momentum to send a greater PuffSat mass onto new Jupiter trajectories, the available returning mass grows exponentially from cycle to cycle (\autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth}). Once seeded, this cycle would deliver mass without an orbital-class reusable rocket. The initial conventional launch costs become less relevant to overall costs over time.

PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion would build on ever improving satellite formation flying. PRISMA demonstrated autonomous relative navigation and control in orbit~\cite{damico2011prisma_safe}, while the VISORS mission develops these capabilities for a precision CubeSat formation~\cite{guffanti2023autonomous}. PuffSat formations would be much larger, though require less precision than science imaging formations like PRISMA.   PuffSats must also master the hit-to-target guidance between the PuffSat swarm and the target vehicle, as well as target vehicle adjustments between each impact (see \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation}.

Each PuffSat must carry a low-cost gas-generating substance through a long interplanetary trajectory, then release a plume shaped for useful momentum and energy transfer. Its nonvolatile hardware must miss the target vehicle. We describe a candidate spin-stabilized satellite in \autoref{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture} and the trajectory requirements in \autoref{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists}. At the receiving vehicle, a pusher plate or pulsed nozzle must convert the arriving flow into thrust.  Efficiency for both depends on shock formation, chemical energy losses, and material survivability (see \autoref{sec:pusher_plates} and \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}).

For context, the companion white paper was written to lay the technical groundwork for hard science fiction world-building. This paper explores whether a focused application of PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion makes sense for real-world space missions. Our goal is to invite critical analysis, feedback, and advice from qualified experts.

\section{Data Centers, Anti-Satellite Weapons, And Launch Costs}\label{sec:data_center_attacks}
For the data-center application, the launch-cost comparison depends on the destination, which must
satisfy both latency and survivability requirements.
Iran's drone strikes on Amazon's Gulf data centers in March 2026 established that data centers are
wartime targets~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. Those were drones against
buildings, so they say nothing about orbital reach. China's destruction of the FY-1C weather
satellite at \SI{865}{\kilo\meter} in January 2007 says a great deal~\cite{kelso2007asat,nasa_odqn2007_fengyun}.
A direct-ascent interceptor~\cite{kan2007_asat} arrives at sun-synchronous altitude in minutes, which leaves no window to
disperse a cluster or to engage the inbound vehicle. A transfer to an Earth-Moon Lagrange point takes
days, at a cost of \SI{2.6}{\second} of round-trip light time to an Earth customer. Distance does not
hide a data center, and a published halo orbit is as predictable as a sun-synchronous one. What
distance buys is the time an active response needs.
