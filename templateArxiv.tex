\begin{abstract}\label{sec:abstract}
% Elevator pitch. The hook, then just enough context to know what the paper is.
% The siting argument, its citations, and the cost case belong in the body.
% See docs/brief_guide.md and CONTEXT.md.
High-thrust, high-specific-impulse propulsion is a long-standing goal of rocket propulsion. Nuclear-pulse concepts such as Project Orion~\cite{projorion} showed in principle how externally supplied, high-energy pulses could escape the traditional chemical-propulsion tradeoff between thrust and specific impulse, but at the unacceptable cost of using nuclear explosives.

This paper introduces \textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion}, a non-nuclear analogue of Orion in which small satellites, dubbed PuffSats, are sent on Jupiter-gravity-assist trajectories that return them to Earth at \SIrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, corresponding to \SIrange{1.5}{2.3}{\giga\joule\per\kilogram} of kinetic energy. Shortly before intercepting the target vehicle, each PuffSat disperses into gas, which strikes a pusher plate. Additional propellant carried by the target vehicle is injected into and mixed with the resulting plasma, increasing the momentum transferred per kilogram of returning PuffSat mass. Alternatively, the incoming material can mix with injected propellant inside a pulsed nozzle chamber, allowing the resulting expansion to produce directed thrust, including thrust toward the oncoming PuffSat.

We develop one candidate PuffSat architecture and evaluate its performance. As a motivating application, we consider the launch of large orbital data centers and examine whether such a system could achieve launch costs competitive with near-term fully reusable launch vehicles such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship}.

The broader architectural implications of PuffSat propulsion are explored in the companion paper \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} uses a stream of small satellites ("PuffSats") moving at hypervelocity to achieve high-thrust high-specific-impulse rocket engines.  The PuffSats explode and their gas mixes with gas injected by the target rocket they are propelling.  This gas mixing transfers momentum and produces a high energy density fireball that pushes the target rocket.   The concept is inspired by Project Orion~\cite{projorion} and by pellet beam propulsion concepts, a family that runs from Singer's unguided pellet stream~\cite{singer1979interstellar} through Nordley's self-steering particles~\cite{nordley2001interstellar} and Kare's SailBeam~\cite{kare2001sailbeam} to Davoyan's laser-ablation pellet beam~\cite{davoyan2023pelletbeam}. Nordley and Crowl survey the wider mass-beam family~\cite{nordley2015massbeam}. The companion white paper~\cite{katz2026aim} compares PuffSats to each of these in its section ``Comparison To Pellet Beam Propulsion Prior Art,'' and that comparison is not repeated here.  

Primary challenges for \textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} include sending gas to a pusher plate without large solid shrapnel; navigation, guidance and control; formation flying a large swarm of small satellites; and efficient energy and momentum transfer to the plate.   

In this paper, we sketch a PuffSat architecture intended to launch large space orbital data centers at prices that are competitive with fully reusable systems.   We also observe that space data centers must be well above sun synchronous orbit if we must adapt to the precedent unfortunately established by Iran in 2026 that data centers will be targets in warfare~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}.  This has major implications on mission architecture, use cases, and the target launch cost we assume is competitive.   For a much more extended look at possible variations on PuffSat missions, see the companion white paper \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim}.
