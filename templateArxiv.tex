\begin{abstract}\label{sec:abstract}
% Elevator pitch. The hook, then just enough context to know what the paper is.
% The siting argument, its citations, and the cost case belong in the body.
% See docs/brief_guide.md and CONTEXT.md.
High-thrust, high-specific-impulse propulsion is a long-standing goal of rocket propulsion. Nuclear-pulse concepts such as Project Orion~\cite{projorion} showed in principle how externally supplied, high-energy pulses could escape the traditional chemical-propulsion tradeoff between thrust and specific impulse, but at the unacceptable cost of using nuclear explosives.

This paper introduces \textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion}, a non-nuclear analogue of Orion in which small satellites, dubbed PuffSats, are sent on Jupiter-gravity-assist trajectories that return them to Earth at \SIrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, corresponding to \SIrange{1.5}{2.3}{\giga\joule\per\kilogram} of kinetic energy. Shortly before intercepting the target vehicle, each PuffSat disperses into gas, which strikes a pusher plate. Additional propellant carried by the target vehicle is injected into and mixed with the resulting plasma, increasing the momentum transferred per kilogram of returning PuffSat mass. Alternatively, the incoming material can mix with injected propellant inside a pulsed nozzle chamber, allowing the resulting expansion to produce directed thrust, including thrust toward the oncoming PuffSat.

We develop one candidate PuffSat architecture and evaluate its performance. As a motivating application, we consider the launch of large orbital data centers and examine whether such a system could achieve launch costs competitive with near-term fully reusable launch vehicles such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship}.

The broader architectural implications of PuffSat propulsion are explored in the companion paper \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion uses small satellites sent on Jupiter gravity-assist trajectories to deliver high-speed gas pulses to a receiving spacecraft. It combines ideas from nuclear-pulse propulsion and external pellet-beam concepts. This paper sketches one candidate architecture and examines whether it could eventually launch large orbital data centers at costs competitive with fully reusable launch vehicles.

Pushing a spacecraft with mass supplied from outside it is an old idea, but a central challenge is how to accelerate that mass to extreme speeds. Singer proposed an unguided pellet stream accelerated by an electromagnetic mass driver in 1979~\cite{singer1979interstellar}. Nordley replaced the unguided pellets with self-steering particles~\cite{nordley2001interstellar}; Kare replaced the mass driver with laser-driven miniature sails~\cite{kare2001sailbeam}; and Davoyan is investigating the acceleration of pellets by laser ablation~\cite{davoyan2023pelletbeam}. Nordley and Crowl survey this broader family of concepts~\cite{nordley2015massbeam}. These proposals generally rely on dedicated acceleration systems sized for interstellar missions. The companion white paper, \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim}, compares the present architecture with earlier pellet-beam proposals in greater detail.

\textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} instead uses celestial mechanics (Jupiter gravity assists) to accelerate the incoming mass. Its feasibility therefore depends less on constructing a dedicated accelerator and more on achieving sufficiently inexpensive launch, repeatable trajectories, and precise spacecraft coordination. Progress in relative navigation and formation control provides reason to examine this approach now. PRISMA demonstrated autonomous relative navigation and control in orbit~\cite{damico2011prisma_safe}, while the VISORS mission develops these capabilities for a precision CubeSat formation~\cite{guffanti2023autonomous}. For PuffSats, individual terminal-guidance requirements may be less stringent than science based formation-flying missions, but the system must still coordinate a much larger formation with a few centimeters precision over a much larger swarm separation distance. We discuss these requirements in \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation}.

Each PuffSat must transport a low-cost gas-generating substance through a long interplanetary trajectory while maintaining acceptable thermal and safety margins. The nonvolatile dry mass hardware must miss the target rocket and the volatiles must form a plume with optimal shaping for efficient momentum and energy transfer. We describe one spin-stabilized PuffSat architecture intended to accomplish these functions in \autoref{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture}.

PuffSats must travel on repeatable trajectories to Jupiter and back. We discuss the choice of Jovian trajectories and the associated radiation, thermal, and $\Delta v$ requirements in \autoref{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists}. Candidate pusher-plate and pulsed-nozzle architectures are considered in \autoref{sec:pusher_plates} and \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}. Key questions for both nozzles and pusher plates are addressed, including chemical dissociation losses, shock coupling, and material survivability.   If returning PuffSats can ultimately launch more PuffSat mass onto subsequent Jupiter-assist trajectories than was required to launch them, the system admits a bootstrapping cycle in which available returning mass grows from one generation to the next. In that regime, the cost of the initial conventional launch becomes progressively less important. We examine this proposed growth cycle and its implications in \autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth}.

We then consider large orbital data centers as an initial application. We first examine the vulnerability of low-Earth-orbit data-center constellations to terrestrial anti-satellite capabilities, using Iranian attacks on terrestrial data-center infrastructure as the basis for considering the security of concentrated computing infrastructure; see \autoref{sec:data_center_attacks}. We then make a deliberately speculative comparison between early PuffSat launch costs and those of a reusable rocket system, assuming that neither has reached a fully mature post-2040s operating cadence; see \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation}.

Finally, \autoref{sec:conclusion} identifies the major unresolved questions and invites expert criticism and collaboration. For context, the companion white paper originally began as technical groundwork for hard science fiction. Further analysis suggested that narrower applications of the underlying concept might be worth examining as real engineering systems. The present work should therefore be read as a high-level feasibility proposal rather than a mature propulsion design. Its purpose is to expose the assumptions, identify potential failure modes, and solicit technical criticism from researchers with relevant expertise.


\section{Data Centers, Anti-Satellite Weapons, And Launch Costs}\label{sec:data_center_attacks}
For the data-center application, the launch-cost comparison depends on the destination, which must
satisfy both latency and survivability requirements.
Iran's drone strikes on Amazon's Gulf data centers in March 2026 established that data centers are
wartime targets~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. Those were drones against
buildings, so they say nothing about orbital reach. China's destruction of the FY-1C weather
satellite at \SI{865}{\kilo\meter} in January 2007 says a great deal~\cite{kelso2007asat,nasa_odqn_2007}.
A direct-ascent interceptor arrives at sun-synchronous altitude in minutes, which leaves no window to
disperse a cluster or to engage the inbound vehicle. A transfer to an Earth-Moon Lagrange point takes
days, at a cost of \SI{2.6}{\second} of round-trip light time to an Earth customer. Distance does not
hide a data center, and a published halo orbit is as predictable as a sun-synchronous one. What
distance buys is the time an active response needs.
