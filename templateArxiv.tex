\begin{abstract}\label{sec:abstract}
% Elevator pitch. The hook, then just enough context to know what the paper is.
% The siting argument, its citations, and the cost case belong in the body.
% See docs/brief_guide.md and CONTEXT.md.
High-thrust, high-specific-impulse propulsion is a long-standing goal of rocket design. Nuclear-pulse concepts such as Project Orion~\cite{projorion} showed in principle how externally supplied, high-energy pulses could escape the traditional chemical-propulsion tradeoff between thrust and specific impulse, but at the unacceptable cost of using nuclear explosives.

This paper introduces \textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion}, a non-nuclear analogue of Orion in which small satellites, dubbed PuffSats, are sent on Jupiter gravity-assist trajectories that return them to Earth at \SIrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, corresponding to \SIrange{1.5}{2.3}{\giga\joule\per\kilogram} of kinetic energy. Shortly before intercepting the target vehicle, each PuffSat disperses into gas, which strikes a pusher plate. Additional propellant carried by the target vehicle is injected into and mixed with the resulting plasma, increasing the momentum transferred per kilogram of returning PuffSat mass. Alternatively, the incoming material can mix with injected propellant inside a pulsed nozzle chamber, allowing the resulting expansion to produce directed thrust, including thrust toward the oncoming PuffSat.

We develop one candidate PuffSat architecture and evaluate its performance. As a motivating application, we consider the launch of large orbital data centers and examine whether such a system could achieve launch costs competitive with near-term fully reusable launch vehicles such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship}. The companion paper \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim} explores the broader architectural implications.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Pushing a spacecraft with mass supplied from outside it is an old idea.  Pellet beam propulsion uses giant external accelerators to hit a pusher plate with hypervelocity mass.   Project Orion slams tungsten plasma into a pusher plate with nuclear explosives.   \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim}, compares the present architecture with earlier pellet-beam proposals in greater detail.

\textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} instead uses celestial mechanics (Jupiter gravity assists) to accelerate the incoming mass. Small satellites return to Earth at \SIrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and disperse into gas shortly before reaching the target vehicle. Its feasibility therefore depends less on constructing a dedicated accelerator and more on achieving sufficiently inexpensive launch, repeatable trajectories, and precise spacecraft coordination. Progress in relative navigation and formation control provides reason to examine this approach now. PRISMA demonstrated autonomous relative navigation and control in orbit~\cite{damico2011prisma_safe}, while the VISORS mission develops these capabilities for a precision CubeSat formation~\cite{guffanti2023autonomous}. For PuffSats, individual terminal-guidance requirements may be less stringent than those of dedicated science-based formation-flying missions. However, the system must still coordinate a much larger ensemble and maintain \SIrange{1}{10}{\centi\meter} terminal precision at the target rocket despite distances spanning \SIrange{5}{50}{\kilo\meter} between successive PuffSats. Additional challenges include the hit-to-target intercept dynamics and ensuring the target rocket can suitably adjust its position between each PuffSat interception. We discuss these requirements in \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation}.

Because Jupiter accelerates PuffSats to extreme speeds, the system can in principle feed itself. If a returning generation of PuffSats delivers enough momentum to launch more PuffSat mass onto subsequent Jupiter gravity-assist trajectories than was needed to launch it, the available returning mass grows from one generation to the next.  The cost of the initial conventional launch matters progressively less (see \autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth}). 

Each PuffSat must transport a low-cost gas-generating substance through a long interplanetary trajectory while maintaining acceptable thermal and safety margins. The nonvolatile dry-mass hardware must miss the target rocket, and the volatiles must form a plume shaped for efficient momentum and energy transfer. One spin-stabilized PuffSat architecture intended to accomplish these functions is described in \autoref{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture}.

PuffSats must travel on repeatable trajectories to Jupiter and back, which sets the choice of Jovian trajectory and the radiation, thermal, and $\Delta v$ requirements that follow from it (\autoref{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists}). At the receiving end, the arriving gas must give up its momentum to either a pusher plate (\autoref{sec:pusher_plates}) or a pulsed nozzle (\autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}). The two share their hardest questions, which are chemical dissociation losses, shock coupling, and material survivability.

Large orbital data centers are the motivating application we focus on in this paper. Low-Earth-orbit data-center constellations are vulnerable to terrestrial anti-satellite capabilities.  Unfortunately, the 2026 Iranian attacks on terrestrial data centers establish a precedent that computing infrastructure will be targets, so orbital data centers must be in distant, defensible orbits (\autoref{sec:data_center_attacks}). A deliberately speculative comparison then puts early PuffSat launch costs against those of a reusable rocket system for these high $\Delta v$ orbits, assuming that neither has reached a fully mature post-2040s operating cadence (\autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation}). \autoref{sec:conclusion} identifies the major unresolved questions and invites expert criticism and collaboration.

For full context, the companion white paper began as an amateur endeavor to lay the technical groundwork for hard science fiction. That investigation led us to wonder if  narrower applications of the underlying concept might be worth examining as real engineering systems.  Read it as a high-level feasibility proposal rather than a mature propulsion design, written to solicit feedback, advice and improvments from qualified experts.

\section{Data Centers, Anti-Satellite Weapons, And Launch Costs}\label{sec:data_center_attacks}
For the data-center application, the launch-cost comparison depends on the destination, which must
satisfy both latency and survivability requirements.
Iran's drone strikes on Amazon's Gulf data centers in March 2026 established that data centers are
wartime targets~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. Those were drones against
buildings, so they say nothing about orbital reach. China's destruction of the FY-1C weather
satellite at \SI{865}{\kilo\meter} in January 2007 says a great deal~\cite{kelso2007asat,nasa_odqn2007_fengyun}.
A direct-ascent interceptor~\cite{kan2007_asat} arrives at sun-synchronous altitude in minutes, which leaves no window to
disperse a cluster or to engage the inbound vehicle. A transfer to an Earth-Moon Lagrange point takes
days, at a cost of \SI{2.6}{\second} of round-trip light time to an Earth customer. Distance does not
hide a data center, and a published halo orbit is as predictable as a sun-synchronous one. What
distance buys is the time an active response needs.
