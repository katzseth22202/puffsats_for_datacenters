\begin{abstract}
PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion is a non-nuclear kinetic analogue of Project Orion's nuclear pulse propulsion. Every impulse lands on a pusher plate. Each pulse is a low-cost satellite (``PuffSat'') striking a charge of inert propellant held a few meters off the plate, and the merged fireball expands against the plate.

Ten days after a ship reaches parking orbit, compact impactors meet it at \ang{130} to its thrust axis. They thread a \SI{1}{\meter} hole in a bowl and send the ship toward Jupiter. Jupiter bends the ship into a dive to \SI{9.86}{R_\odot}, the depth Parker Solar Probe has flown, while graphite disks take the mirror-image dive. Ship and disks meet head-on at about \SI{392}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, and the disks add \SI{9.8}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. The Oberth effect returns the ship across Earth's orbit \SI{3.28}{\year} after it left, and it releases its mass as Kapton sheets as wide as the plate. A reusable booster lofts the next launch unit above the atmosphere. The sheets overtake it at \SI{99}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and push it into a parking orbit.

On simulated impacts and a cost model that charges every cost, the fleet grows 3.36 times per cycle and doubles every \SI{1.87}{\year}. Two questions decide whether that holds, and this paper puts them to specialists. Can each PuffSat be aimed as precisely as the plates require? Can the plates and sheets survive the loads computed for them?
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion pushes a ship with a stream of guided impactors rather than with propellant it carries. Each impactor, a low-cost satellite called a PuffSat, strikes a charge of cheap propellant held a few meters off a pusher plate, and the merged fireball expands against the plate. The parent paper develops the whole architecture~\cite{katz2026aim}. This document restates its headline cycle and the choices behind it, so that a specialist can judge the two questions the cycle rests on.

\paragraph{A plate, not a nozzle.} Every impulse in the headline cycle lands on a plate. A chamber that exhausts through a throat has to enclose the blast, so its wall works in hoop tension all the way around and grows with the pulse it catches. The parent sizes that wall at about \SI{15}{\tonne} for a \SI{1}{\kilogram} pulse and \SI{130}{\tonne} for a \SI{10}{\kilogram} one, and finds that a \SI{2}{\square\meter} throat grows 10\% in area within a few hundred pulses. A plate takes the pressure on one face and passes it to the ship through a spring. A magnetic nozzle, a shaped field that turns the plasma with no wall in contact, has no wall to erode. It is still heated by the currents the plume carries, and a field cannot turn the light the fireball radiates. The parent keeps chambers and magnetic nozzles as backups.

\paragraph{How it differs from Orion.} Orion's bomb could not be set off against its plate. Its energy crossed a gap of tens of meters as soft X-rays, and a shaped tungsten slab turned that radiation back into a jet aimed at the ship~\cite{balcomb1970nuclear,dyson2002project_orion}. A guided impactor needs no such converter, because it delivers its energy where it is aimed. The parent expects the fireball to stay opaque, so that little of its energy leaves as light. The plates are shaped to their jobs. The push plate at Earth is flat. The departure plate is a bowl whose depth is half its diameter, with the charge at its focus. Both ride a nitrogen gas spring. Each pulse is a chosen mass, so the design can lower the acceleration per pulse to ease the spring's load. The push runs at two pulses a second and a mean of 1~g.

\paragraph{Why dive at the Sun.} The fleet could grow on Jupiter alone. Flown on the same plates with no dive, that loop grows 1.65 times per three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods (\SI{3.28}{\year}). Adding the dive raises the growth to 3.36 times. The ship meets the graphite disks deep in the Sun's gravity well, where the Oberth effect turns their \SI{9.8}{\kilo\meter\per\second} into a much larger gain in return speed. Deeper dives grow faster. At \SI{4}{R_\odot} the fleet grows 4.99 times per cycle and doubles in \SI{1.41}{\year}. The baseline stops at \SI{9.86}{R_\odot} because Parker Solar Probe has flown that depth and kept working~\cite{nasa2024_parker_survives}.

The cycle's numbers come from simulated impacts and a cost model that charges every cost. Two inputs cannot be settled on paper. One is whether each PuffSat can be aimed as precisely as the plates require. The other is whether the plates and sheets survive the loads computed for them. Those are the questions this paper puts to specialists.
