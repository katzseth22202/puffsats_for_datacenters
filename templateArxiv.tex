\begin{abstract}
PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion is a non-nuclear kinetic analogue of Project Orion's nuclear pulse propulsion. Small, low-cost satellites (``PuffSats'') fly an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop that closes in 2.2 or 3.3 years. They return on retrograde orbits at Earth-relative speeds of \qtyrange{57}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds methalox's specific combustion energy by more than a hundredfold.

A suborbital rocket lifts the target vehicle above the atmosphere, and PuffSat pulses supply the remaining $\deltav$. Each arriving PuffSat disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the target. The hot gas either strikes a pusher plate or expands through a pulsed nozzle that can thrust in any direction. We argue that each target vehicle can send more new PuffSat mass toward Jupiter than the incoming mass it consumes. Repeated across generations, this surplus would sustain exponential growth in launch capacity with no further orbital-class chemical boosters. We also sketch a variant that adds an Oberth maneuver (a velocity change at perihelion, where high speed multiplies the energy gained) and returns PuffSats at Earth-relative speeds above \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}.

Finally, we apply the architecture to orbital data centers. Data centers are now wartime targets, so we argue they belong in orbits distant enough that an attack takes days rather than minutes to arrive. AI training and coding inference tolerate the seconds of light delay to Earth that this adds. Reaching those orbits takes a large $\deltav$, and under the stated assumptions PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can deliver mass there more cheaply than fully reusable rockets such as SpaceX Starship.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} is a proposed non-nuclear propulsion system that uses the kinetic energy of small, low-cost satellites to accelerate a target vehicle. Each satellite, called a PuffSat, flies an Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop and returns on a retrograde heliocentric orbit. The return speed relative to Earth is \qtyrange{57}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds methalox's specific combustion energy by more than a hundredfold. The Jupiter flyby reverses each PuffSat's orbital direction and sends it sunward. The loop therefore closes in 2.2 or 3.3 years (two or three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods), rather than the 5.5 years of two minimum-energy legs. PuffSats depart with an Earth-relative hyperbolic excess speed of \qtyrange{12.0}{14.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. New Horizons had an excess speed of \SI{12.6}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and reached Jupiter in 404 days~\cite{guo2008newhorizons_design}, and Ulysses had an excess speed of \SI{11.3}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and reached it in 16 months~\cite{marsden1992ulysses_jupiter}. \autoref{fig:jupiter_loop} draws the loop, \autoref{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists} gives the trajectory details, and the calculations are in the companion code repository~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}.

The concept builds on Project Orion~\cite{projorion}, which proposed using nuclear explosions to deliver repeated impulses to a spacecraft, and on pellet-beam propulsion~\cite{singer1979interstellar,willis1994momentum,nordley2001interstellar,nordley2015massbeam,kare2001sailbeam,davoyan2023pelletbeam,whitmire_jackson1977laser_ramjet,matloff1979runway,kare1997buzzbomb,greason2020runway,greason2022windpellet,winglee_magbeam,landis2004particlebeam}. PuffSats reach their extreme velocities through gravity assists, which are flight-proven, rather than through nuclear explosions or through beam launchers that have not been built. A conventional suborbital rocket lifts the target vehicle above the atmosphere, and PuffSat pulses supply the remaining $\deltav$. As each PuffSat approaches, its working mass disperses into a gas cloud that collides with propellant released by the target vehicle. The collision converts relative kinetic energy into hot gas. This gas either strikes a pusher plate, transferring an impulse to the target vehicle, or enters a chamber and expands through a pulsed nozzle. The nozzle directs the exhaust to produce thrust, including thrust toward the incoming PuffSats. 

Each PuffSat must preserve its payload and electronics through the interplanetary flight despite radiation, micrometeorites, and temperature extremes. Its structural components must avoid striking the target vehicle. \autoref{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture} sketches a satellite design addressing these requirements. The target vehicle's hardware must survive repeated pulses while capturing the arriving flow. \autoref{sec:pusher_plates} and \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles} discuss pusher plates and pulsed nozzles.

PuffSat streams are designed to fly as tight formations, so that a target vehicle meeting the first PuffSat needs only small corrections to meet the rest. A formation that holds its shape can still arrive off target, so the target vehicle must measure each PuffSat's offset and correct between successive impacts. \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation} addresses these guidance, navigation, and control problems.

Launched payloads can include replacement PuffSats as well as useful cargo. If each returning generation enables a greater mass of PuffSats to be sent onto new Jupiter trajectories, the returning mass grows exponentially from cycle to cycle. Under that condition, arriving PuffSats propel both cargo and the next generation, allowing the cycle to continue without further orbital-class chemical boosters after conventional launches seed it. The initial launch expense is then spread over an increasing delivered mass. \autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth} examines this growth condition.

Large orbital data centers provide a potential near-term market for very heavy lift~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher,nvidia2025_starcloud,foust2026_starcloud_fcc}. Iran's 2026 strikes on terrestrial data centers~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike} show that data centers are military targets. China's 2007 destruction of the FY-1C weather satellite~\cite{nasa_odqn2007_fengyun,kan2007_asat} confirms that direct-ascent suborbital missiles can reach sun-synchronous orbit in minutes. In \autoref{sec:data_center_attacks}, we argue for orbits distant enough that an attack takes days to arrive, leaving time to respond. Because each cluster is co-located~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}, only its link to Earth slows down, and AI training and long-running coding inference can tolerate the added round-trip latency of \qtyrange{2.6}{10}{\second}~\cite{anthropic_message_batches,openai_batch_api}. Reaching them takes more $\deltav$, which favors PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion over conventional rockets. Launch must also be cheap enough for orbital computing to compete with data centers on the ground. Starcloud's chief executive puts that price near \$500 per kilogram~\cite{bennici2026_johnston}. Google's Suncatcher team sets a stricter bar, finding that at \$200 per kilogram, launch spread over a satellite's life costs about as much as a terrestrial data center's energy~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} argues that PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can deliver mass to these orbits for less than fully reusable rockets such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship}.

\autoref{sec:oberth_solar_maneuvers} sketches a variant that adds an Oberth maneuver near the Sun to the Earth--Jupiter loop, producing Earth-relative speeds above \SI{150}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. This paper complements the long-term conceptual roadmap of \textit{Aim Is All You Need}~\cite{katz2026aim} by focusing on near-term applications.

\section{PuffSat Satellite Architecture}\label{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture}
\subsection{Deployment Rocket}\label{sec:deployment_rocket}
PuffSats do not fly the Jupiter loop alone. A large deployment rocket carries them out, through the Jupiter flyby, and back toward Earth, and releases them only during the final approach to Earth. Until then it supplies their power and keeps their water liquid.

Sunlight can do that job at Jupiter. Juno has run on about \SI{500}{\watt} from three \SI{9}{\meter} arrays since it arrived in 2016~\cite{bolton2017juno,juno_solar_record}. A rocket carrying many PuffSats needs proportionally less. Heat leaks through its surface, which grows as the square of its size, while the mass it keeps warm grows as the cube. Its panels can therefore be a smaller fraction of its mass than Juno's. The flyby does pass through Jupiter's shadow, so the rocket carries a battery to keep its electronics and the PuffSats' water warm until it returns to sunlight.

Radiation is the harder problem. The flyby turns the trajectory within Jupiter's orbital plane, and Jupiter's equator is tilted only \ang{3.1} from that plane. The rocket therefore crosses the equatorial radiation belts that Juno's polar orbit was designed to pass under~\cite{bolton2017juno,katz2026aim}. The PuffSats themselves are the shield. Their mass is mostly water and other light volatiles, enough to surround the electronics with \qtyrange{50}{100}{\centi\meter} of material. Electrons, and the bremsstrahlung X-rays they emit as they stop, set most of the dose~\cite{roussos2021jovian_belts,katz2026aim}. Water also slows the neutrons that energetic protons and photons knock out of nuclei. Boron carbide at about \SI{0.5}{\percent} of the shield's mass then captures them without the penetrating \SI{2.2}{\mega\electronvolt} gamma ray that hydrogen capture emits~\cite{katz2026aim}. No full dose budget for the pass has been computed, so the shield thickness is a requirement rather than a result.

Solar cells are the part of the power system the belts damage most, because a panel has to face open space to work. At Jupiter's distance sunlight delivers about \SI{50}{\watt\per\square\meter}, a twenty-seventh of its flux at Earth. A non-imaging concentrator, a mirror or lens that funnels light from a wide aperture onto a small target without forming an image, shrinks the cell area by its concentration ratio~\cite{winston2005nonimaging}. A ratio of 27 puts Earth-strength sunlight back on the cell. Seen from Jupiter, the Sun is a disk only \ang{0.05} in radius, so the concentrated beam stays narrow. Even with \ang{1} of pointing error the focused light stays within about \ang{5} of its axis~\cite{winston2005nonimaging,katz2026aim}. A beam that narrow can enter a mirrored cavity and turn two corners on its way to cells inside the shield (\autoref{fig:mirrored_light_cavity}).

The bends do the shielding, because only light turns the corners. Jupiter's magnetic field cannot steer a particle around one. Even at the equatorial cloud tops, where the dipole field is \SI{0.417}{\milli\tesla}~\cite{connerney2018jrm09}, a \SI{10}{\mega\electronvolt} electron circles with a radius of about \SI{85}{\meter} and strays only about \SI{1}{\centi\meter} over a \SI{3}{\meter} cavity~\cite{katz2026aim}. X-rays from outside are stopped too, because a metal reflects them only below a glancing angle of about \ang{0.24}~\cite{alsnielsen2011xray}. Some electrons do scatter back off the first mirror, \SI{9}{\percent} of them at \SI{1}{\mega\electronvolt} on aluminum at normal incidence~\cite{miller1970backscatter}. Those that stop in it radiate bremsstrahlung. The second bend hides the cells from the first mirror, so this secondary radiation must scatter again to reach them. Each aluminum fold reflects about nine tenths of visible light~\cite{hass1961aluminum}, so two folds pass about \SI{81}{\percent}, and the concentration ratio is raised to cover the loss. Neither the cavity's throughput, including mirror darkening under the belt dose, nor the dose at the cells has been computed, so both are requirements rather than results.

The rocket releases the PuffSats as far as \SI{1.3}{\astronomicalunit} from the Sun. From there the coast to Earth lasts about 16 days~\cite{katz2026aim}. Each PuffSat is a bag of water around an inner bag of explosive emulsion, and the water holds the emulsion's temperature through the coast. Just before release, a deployment mechanism spins and orients each PuffSat and kneads the emulsion. Kneading mixes in the sensitizers, which stay out until late in flight so that the charge is hard to set off by accident~\cite{maranda2025comprehensive}. It also mixes in slow-acting thickeners that lock the sensitizing bubbles in place, and it lets each PuffSat fly without a pump.

\subsection{Pusher-Plate PuffSats}\label{sec:pusher_plate_puffsats}
After release, each PuffSat spins. Redundant Kevlar~49 wires, each \SI{100}{\micro\meter} across, tether its electronics to the working mass. Three of them hold each sphere, and together they break at \SI{71}{\newton} against the \SI{20}{\newton} the spin pulls~\cite{kevlar_technical_guide,katz2026aim}. The wires are long enough that the spin holds the electronics outside the target vehicle's diameter, so the electronics pass it by and only the wires cross the plate (\autoref{fig:tethered_necklace}). The wires run centimeters apart, so freeing the electronics takes a separate micrometeoroid cut on every wire, about once in \num{e11} over a sixteen-day coast~\cite{forward_hoyt1995hoytether,katz2026aim}. Thin wires also load the plate less. Each strikes it as a narrow line of low areal density, and a locally thicker ablative coating where the wires will hit protects the plate from them~\cite{katz2026aim}. The working mass is a necklace of spheres, oriented to explode into a disk of gas ahead of the pusher plate, the shape the plate couples to best~\cite{Katz_puffsat_impact_sim_2026,katz2026aim}. Each sphere is a bag of water around an inner bag of explosive emulsion, shaped to detonate into that disk. Each sphere carries a clock that fires a small primary charge. A one-gram ice bullet fired by the target vehicle can also set it off. Its shock reaches the emulsion at about \SI{2}{\giga\pascal}, twice what sets off a sensitive secondary explosive~\cite{souers_thresholds,katz2026aim}.

The spin axis points along the stream, so the Sun sits \SIrange{44}{50}{\degree} off it for the whole coast. Side heating across the spin's effective gravity drives a slow convective loop that evens out each sphere's temperature. A pattern of high- and low-emittance patches sets the mean~\cite{katz2026aim} (\autoref{fig:sidewall_convection}).

The spin's effective gravity points away from the axis, across that temperature difference. Water heated from the side has no threshold to cross, so it turns over in a slow loop around the inner bag.}
    \label{fig:sidewall_convection}

Each sphere sits inside a multilayer blanket of the aluminum--Mylar--aluminum laminate that Echo~II flew~\cite{nasa_echo_ii_1966}. The blanket stays folded inside the deployment rocket until release. It is then stretched over the interior bags at a standoff of about a centimeter. Stretching yields the laminate's aluminum, so the blanket holds its shape without gas pressure, as Echo~II did~\cite{nasa_echo_ii_1966,katz2026aim}. The emittance pattern is printed on its outer sheet. The blanket is also a Whipple shield, a thin sacrificial layer that breaks up a particle before it reaches the wall behind~\cite{whipple_shield}. NASA's shield-design rule sets the bumper mass that shatters a grain of given size and density~\cite{jsc_mmod_handbook}. By that rule, a grain smaller than about \SI{185}{\micro\meter} shatters on the laminate's \SI{37}{\gram\per\square\meter}, and its debris spreads out before it reaches the bag~\cite{jsc_mmod_handbook,katz2026aim} (\autoref{fig:sphere_layers}a). Each further sheet shocks the debris cloud again~\cite{cour_palais1990multishock}.

Larger micrometeoroids still punch through, so some PuffSats will be lost. Grün's flux model puts a grain above \SI{185}{\micro\meter} into about one \SI{10}{\kilogram} sphere in 170 over a sixteen-day coast~\cite{grun1985collisional,katz2026aim}. A loss found early costs little, because the formation adjusts and a spare moves into the empty slot~\cite{katz2026aim}. A loss found too close to impact for that falls to the target vehicle. It makes up the missing momentum with an unplanned burn from a propellant reserve, which keeps it on the trajectory the remaining PuffSats are aimed at. That loss rate sizes the reserve~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Each PuffSat also flies through gas left by the pulses before it. At closing speeds near \SI{60}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, even this thin gas heats the sphere. It needs no heat shield, because the dose arrives late (\autoref{fig:plume_timeline}). The blanket and the bag may fail in the last few hundred milliseconds, at no cost. A freed sphere drifts only \SI{9}{\centi\meter} outward before impact, and the necklace's center stays put (\autoref{fig:tethered_necklace})~\cite{katz2026aim}. The gas is too thin to steer the sphere. In a ballistic model, drag moves its landing point on the plate by under \SI{20}{\micro\meter}~\cite{katz2026aim}. The wires fare worse. They lose their strength about \SI{240}{\milli\second} before impact, before the charges fire~\cite{kevlar_technical_guide,katz2026aim}, so nothing they carry may be needed once the gas arrives.

The curve is sketched through those quoted points, not computed. The Kevlar wires lose their strength about \SI{240}{\milli\second} before impact, before the charges fire.     \label{fig:plume_timeline}

Blanket shreds that survive the gas must still cross the water the plate sprays ahead of itself. That spray is sized to stop the whole dispersed PuffSat, with five to ten times its mass over the same footprint~\cite{Katz_water_injected_plate_2026}. A fragment loses half its speed each time it sweeps up about its own mass per unit area of gas. The blanket is a laminate of about \SI{37}{\gram\per\square\meter}~\cite{nasa_echo_ii_1966}, far less than the spray over the same area, so a shred slows many times over inside the spray rather than at the plate. Stopping it turns its kinetic energy, about \SI{1.8}{\giga\joule\per\kilogram} at \SI{60}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, into heat in the shocked gas around it. The fragment vaporizes if it keeps a few percent of that heat. Fragment survival through the spray has not been computed, so how much solid reaches the plate is not yet known.

Closed-cell foam, possibly polyethylene, fills the outer bag~\cite{rodriguez_perez2005_polyolefin_foams}. The foam's acoustic impedance is far below water's, so the blast cavitates the water at the foam strands~\cite{cole1948underwater} (\autoref{fig:sphere_layers}b). Gas held in crevices on the foam's surface seeds the cavitation~\cite{atchley1989crevice}. This spreads the blast through the bag and breaks the water into small droplets. The droplets then vaporize as they mix with propellant released by the pusher plate~\cite{Katz_water_injected_plate_2026,katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Nozzle PuffSats}\label{sec:nozzle_puffsats}
A PuffSat bound for a pulsed nozzle keeps the tethered electronics of \autoref{sec:pusher_plate_puffsats}, so its electronics again pass outside the target vehicle. Its working mass changes. It must enter the nozzle's chamber through a narrow port, so it is a long, thin, solid rod rather than a necklace of bags. A \SI{10}{\kilogram} polyethylene rod ten times as long as it is wide is \SI{1.10}{\meter} long and \SI{11}{\centi\meter} across~\cite{crc_handbook,katz2026aim}. The port is \SI{15}{\centi\meter} across, which leaves \SI{2}{\centi\meter} of clearance all round (\autoref{fig:rod_port_plug}).

That clearance shapes the rod. The pusher plate needed a disk of gas, and an explosive to make one. The chamber confines the gas itself, so the rod carries no explosive and only has to arrive. It carries no liquid either, because liquid sloshes in its bag and moves the working mass away from where the tethers hold it. A solid rod stays centered in the circle its spinning electronics packages trace. \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation} takes up how the rod is steered into the port. The rod is metallized under a clear coat, and its spin averages the sunlight over its surface. It sits near \SI{299}{\kelvin}, where polyethylene holds its shape~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Past the port the rod strikes a plug of frozen methane. The chamber's gas alone is too thin to stop it, and the rod could reach the far wall intact. A projectile erodes into a target over about its own length times the square root of their density ratio~\cite{birkhoff1948lined}, and that sets the plug's depth. The plug is twice the rod's width, which leaves \SI{5.5}{\centi\meter} for aim error. It is grown porous, as frost is, at about half the solid's density. That makes it lighter and deeper, \SI{18.8}{\kilogram} and \SI{2.35}{\meter}. A shock that crushes a porous solid does extra work closing the voids, so the plug ends hotter and more of it joins the gas as vapor rather than droplets~\cite{zeldovich_raizer,katz2026aim}. The penetration law was derived for dense targets, so the porous plug's depth is an extrapolation that needs a shock-physics simulation.

Everything the rod and plug bring into the chamber leaves through the nozzle as exhaust. The wall caps the gas temperature, and at a fixed temperature exhaust speed falls as the inverse square root of the gas's mean particle mass. The materials are therefore chosen from light, non-toxic elements, hydrogen and carbon in the rod, the plug and the chamber's fill. Methane also adds no oxygen, which would burn a carbon hot face into carbon monoxide. It raises the mean particle mass by \SI{4.8}{\percent}, which costs about \SI{2.3}{\percent} of exhaust speed~\cite{nist_webbook_methane,katz2026aim}. The \SI{2}{\centi\meter} clearance and the plug's size are requirements rather than results. The rod's motion on its tethers has not been simulated.

\section{Jupiter Gravity-Assist Details}\label{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists}
PuffSats fly a trajectory that loops from Earth out to Jupiter and back, and each new departure must find Earth and Jupiter where the last one found them. That forces the round trip to last a whole number of Earth--Jupiter \textbf{synodic periods}, the \SI{1.092}{\year} Earth takes to lap Jupiter and restore the same alignment~\cite{katz2026aim}. Three synodic periods (\SI{3.28}{\year}) and two (\SI{2.18}{\year}) both close, and \autoref{fig:jupiter_loop} draws both loops. A third option flies part of the outbound leg faster and parks it near Earth. If the departure burn is methalox, only the three-synodic cycle is practical. The other two need the higher specific impulse that PuffSat nozzles may reach (\autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}). The trajectory numbers below come from an eleven-cycle chain flown on real planetary ephemerides in the companion code repository~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}, and the parent paper works through them~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Three Synodic Periods}\label{sec:three_synodic_cadence}
Each returning batch of PuffSats splits into two waves. The \textbf{growth wave} arrives first and pushes a payload lifted from the ground into a highly eccentric Earth orbit. It meets the payload at \SI{400}{\kilo\meter}, where the air still leaves its tethers cool~\cite{katz2026aim}. The chain flies a ten-day head start, which is the period of that orbit. A twenty-day orbit does within 0.7\% as well~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}, and the burns below are priced on it~\cite{katz2026aim}. The \textbf{departure wave} arrives one orbit later and supplies the burn that sends the payload back toward Jupiter.

Between the two waves the payload has to turn around. The growth wave pushed it along its own direction of travel, and the departure wave arrives from roughly the opposite direction. So the payload coasts to apoapsis, the orbit's far point, and burns there. At \SI{613000}{\kilo\meter} from Earth it moves at only about \SI{0.12}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so turning is cheap. The same burn lifts its periapsis, the orbit's closest point, to \SI{600}{\kilo\meter}. The growth push had to maximize what reaches orbit on the first lift. The departure leg does not, so it passes higher, where drag cannot disturb the steered PuffSats. The whole reversal costs about 6\% of the departing mass in carried propellant~\cite{katz2026aim}. Panel (a) of \autoref{fig:earth_turnaround} draws the sequence.

The departure wave then meets the payload almost head-on at periapsis. In the payload's frame, the PuffSats' velocity makes an angle of \ang{150.4} with the thrust, where \ang{180} would be exactly head-on. Treating the collision as head-on misstates the delivered mass by 0.9\%~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. The burn is deep in Earth's gravity well, where the payload already moves fast and each unit of $\deltav$ buys the most energy. The four three-synodic cycles in the chain need \SIrange{5.32}{5.54}{\kilo\meter\per\second} of it~\cite{katz2026aim}. That figure is at the parent paper's \SI{200}{\kilo\meter} reference altitude. At \SI{600}{\kilo\meter} each burn needs \SIrange{107}{110}{\meter\per\second} more~\cite{katz2026aim}. A methalox stage at \SI{380}{\second} of specific impulse has an exhaust speed of \SI{3.73}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so it keeps $e^{-\deltav/3.73}$ of the vehicle, or 23 to 24\%. That is expensive but workable. The PuffSats come home at \SIrange{56.53}{57.43}{\kilo\meter\per\second} relative to Earth~\cite{katz2026aim}.

\subsection{Two Synodic Periods}\label{sec:two_synodic_cadence}
The shorter loop needs a harder turn at Jupiter, so the flyby passes much closer (\autoref{fig:jupiter_loop}). The tightest passes sit at the \SI{4000}{\kilo\meter} altitude floor the chain allows, \num{1.056} Jupiter radii from the planet's center~\cite{katz2026aim}. They pass deeper into the radiation belts that \autoref{sec:deployment_rocket} shields against. In a model with both planets on circular orbits in one plane, a two-synodic cycle always closes~\cite{katz2026aim}. Real orbits are eccentric and inclined, and there Jupiter sometimes cannot bend the path far enough. The departure wave then needs a \textbf{deep-space maneuver}, a burn made in mid-coast rather than at a planet. The chain takes the two-synodic return whenever that maneuver stays under \SI{50}{\meter\per\second} and falls back to three synodic periods otherwise. Four of its eleven cycles fell back. Over two hundred years of real Earth--Jupiter geometry, only 45 of 91 two-synodic windows clear the altitude floor~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The mechanics repeat those of \autoref{sec:three_synodic_cadence}. The departure wave arrives at \ang{160.6} to the thrust, even closer to head-on, and treating it as head-on costs 0.5\%~\cite{Katz_aim_is_all_you_need_2025}. The burn is larger, \SIrange{6.84}{7.17}{\kilo\meter\per\second} across the seven two-synodic cycles~\cite{katz2026aim}. On methalox that keeps 15 to 16\% of the vehicle, which is marginal. The PuffSats return faster, at \SIrange{61.83}{65.13}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so they push harder on the next outbound leg~\cite{katz2026aim}.

That burn needs an engine with a higher specific impulse than methalox. A nuclear thermal rocket, which heats hydrogen in a fission reactor, would roughly match the pulsed chamber's performance~\cite{katz2026aim}. We rule it out on its waste. A failed burn can leave a spent core on an orbit that returns to Earth within years to decades. Even a nominal disposal orbit still reaches Jupiter's orbit, where later flybys can reshape it, so it cannot be shown clear of Earth for a million years. That is the span over which the United States requires a geologic repository to contain spent fuel~\cite{epa2008yucca,katz2026aim}. Every cycle would add another core to those orbits. This is why the departure burn falls to PuffSat nozzles (\autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}), whose potential specific impulse is higher.

\subsection{Fly And Park}\label{sec:fly_and_park_cadence}
The third option sends the outbound leg on two trajectories. The departure wave keeps the three-synodic clock. The growth wave flies a shorter, faster route and arrives months early. It pushes the payload into an eccentric orbit, and the payload waits there for the departure wave. We call this \textbf{fly-and-park}, following the parent paper~\cite{katz2026aim}.

Left on the twenty-day orbit, the parked payload would dip through the Van Allen radiation belts on every pass~\cite{li2019vanallen}. So a burn at apoapsis raises its periapsis above the outer belt, to about \SI{60000}{\kilo\meter} from Earth's center, for \SI{0.22}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. Before the departure wave arrives, a second apoapsis burn lowers the periapsis to \SI{600}{\kilo\meter}. The same burn turns the orbit so that the payload meets the incoming PuffSats nearly head-on. It costs \SIrange{0.22}{0.46}{\kilo\meter\per\second} depending on the turn. The park therefore adds \SIrange{0.44}{0.68}{\kilo\meter\per\second} in all~\cite{katz2026aim}. Panel (b) of \autoref{fig:earth_turnaround} draws the park. A park that lasts months has time to pay that with solar-electric engines or a lunar flyby.

The park buys two things. The growth wave need not arrive all at once, because each payload can wait in the park for the departure wave. The ground launches it pushes can then be spread over months rather than crowded into the days around a single wave. The faster route also returns PuffSats at about \SI{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, so each one pushes more mass outbound. The best case returns 1.27 times the mass of a plain three-synodic cycle~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The faster route costs a larger departure burn, a median \SI{1.04}{\kilo\meter\per\second} more. On methalox that misses breaking even by about 5\%, and fly-and-park beats the plain three-synodic cycle at only 1 of 11 departure phases. At \SI{1200}{\second} of specific impulse it wins at 16 of 30, and at \SI{2214}{\second} it wins at 25 of 30~\cite{katz2026aim}. Those specific impulses are therefore requirements for the PuffSat nozzles of \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}, not results.

Release from the deployment rocket (\autoref{sec:deployment_rocket}) spaces the PuffSats so that they arrive over the few minutes of a push. A PuffSat released with a small velocity change drifts ahead of or behind its neighbors by that change times the coast time. Spreading a four-minute push this way takes at most \SI{4.4}{\meter\per\second} per PuffSat~\cite{katz2026aim}. The rocket pays that change, not the PuffSat. It can push PuffSats off in matched forward and backward pairs, so that its own momentum does not change, or burn between releases. Either way costs it under \SI{10}{\meter\per\second}. The stream is aimed at Earth, so the rocket must then leave it. A \SI{7}{\meter\per\second} burn at release moves its arrival \SI{10000}{\kilo\meter} to the side, clear of the atmosphere~\cite{katz2026aim}.

The same release spacing can open gaps of one to two seconds in the formation wherever a satellite would pass too close to it~\cite{katz2026aim}. After the push, most PuffSat debris, including the electronics that pass the plate, continues on heliocentric orbits that cross Jupiter's~\cite{katz2026aim}. Jupiter's gravity is more likely to perturb this debris than Earth is to meet it again, so a later strike on a satellite is unlikely.

\section{Data Centers, Anti-Satellite Weapons, And Launch Costs}\label{sec:data_center_attacks}
Data centers are now military targets. In March 2026, Iranian drones struck Amazon Web Services data centers in the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. Project Suncatcher~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher} and Starcloud~\cite{foust2026_starcloud_fcc} both plan sun-synchronous orbits, Starcloud in shells at \qtyrange{600}{850}{\kilo\meter}. In 2007, China destroyed the Fengyun-1C weather satellite at \qty{865}{\kilo\meter} with a ground-launched missile~\cite{kan2007_asat,nasa_odqn2007_fengyun}, and China and Russia both now field such weapons~\cite{dia2022_space_security}. A sun-synchronous orbit passes over nearly every latitude~\cite{nasa_orbit_catalog}, so attackers get repeated chances and the interceptor arrives within minutes.

One reason satellite attacks are rare is that the military benefits aren't worth the cost of hitting dispersed targets~\cite{samson2025_swf_counterspace,hallex2020_proliferated_constellations}. The director of the US Space Development Agency says ``it will cost more to shoot down a single satellite than it will cost to build that single satellite''~\cite{hadley2023_tournear}. An orbital data center cannot spread out that way, because its nodes need high-bandwidth links to each other. Suncatcher's illustrative cluster puts 81 satellites, \qty{2.3}{\mega\watt} of computing, within a \qty{1}{\kilo\meter} radius~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}. Nor can it hide. Homing on radar and infrared returns is within reach of many countries~\cite{wright2005_physics_space_security}. Starcloud's panels would span about \qty{4}{\kilo\meter} on a side~\cite{nvidia2025_starcloud}, and a data center sheds nearly all its power as heat, so a seeker has a conspicuous radar and infrared target.

Distance does not hide a data center, but it buys warning time. Reaching a Lagrange point takes \qty{11.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} against \qty{3.4}{\kilo\meter\per\second} to reach \qty{650}{\kilo\meter}~\cite{katz2026aim}. Even at \qty{14}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, an interceptor needs half a day to reach Earth--Moon L4/L5 and two days to reach Sun--Earth L1. That is time to disperse a cluster, or to launch interceptors based with it, like the escorts the U.S. Space Force plans for its own satellites~\cite{easley2025_ussf_framework}. In September 2026, the Secretary of the Air Force said that the United States has weapons in orbit, without saying what they are~\cite{ap2026_meink_space_weapons}. Whether they include a defense of this kind is not public.

Latency decides which workloads go where. High Earth orbit at \qtyrange{20000}{80000}{\kilo\meter} adds \qtyrange{0.13}{0.53}{\second} of round-trip delay and still gives \qty{0.5}{\hour} to \qty{14}{\hour} of warning, so it suits latency-sensitive inference. AI training and batch inference, including long-running coding agents, can go to the Lagrange points. The \qtyrange{2.6}{10}{\second} round trip there is far inside the \qty{24}{\hour} turnaround that batch inference services already offer~\cite{anthropic_message_batches,openai_batch_api}. Sun--Earth L1 has no eclipses at all~\cite{nasa_lagrange_points}, but the James Webb Space Telescope's always sunny halo orbit at Sun--Earth L2 shows other libration-point orbits can also avoid ever falling into shadow~\cite{nasa_webb_orbit}. The cost is exposure to solar particle events outside the magnetosphere~\cite{li2019vanallen}. Suncatcher's proton test ran a commercial tensor processing unit to 15~krad(Si) without hard failures~\cite{aguera2026_suncatcher}.

Starship~\cite{starship} can reach high orbits, but launch costs near term are uncompetitive with terrestrial data centers. Starcloud's chief executive puts competitive pricing near \$500 per \si{\kilo\gram}~\cite{bennici2026_johnston}. SpaceX's IPO underwriters, who have reason to be optimistic~\cite{michaely1999_underwriter}, forecast \$183 (Goldman Sachs~\cite{pethokoukis2026_goldman}) and about \$500 (Morgan Stanley~\cite{investing2026_ms_spacex}) per \si{\kilo\gram} to low Earth orbit in 2030. Sun--Earth L1 lies a further \qtyrange{3.2}{4.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second} out, paid for with propellant from two to four tanker flights. Each kilogram delivered there costs 3.5 to 4.6 kilograms launched to low Earth orbit~\cite{katz2026aim}, turning the forecasts into \$640--840 and \$1750--2300 per \si{\kilo\gram}. High circular orbits are generally harder still. Circularizing at \qty{22000}{\kilo\meter} takes \qty{1.45}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, and leaving takes \qty{1.46}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, against about \qty{30}{\meter\per\second} to leave Sun--Earth L1~\cite{katz2026aim}.

These estimates are floors. They count the tanker propellant but not the logistics and operations of orbital refueling, which SpaceX lists as a material challenge~\cite{spacex2026_s1a}. A ship back from L1 enters at \qty{11.1}{\kilo\meter\per\second} rather than \qty{8.0}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, about 2.7 times the peak heating~\cite{katz2026aim}, on a heat shield SpaceX's chief executive called Starship's biggest challenge~\cite{musk2024_heatshield}. \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation} argues that PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion can meet the \$500 per \si{\kilo\gram} target in high orbits.
