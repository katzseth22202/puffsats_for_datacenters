\begin{abstract}
Project Orion envisioned spacecraft that would combine high thrust with high specific impulse by using nuclear explosions for pulsed momentum transfer. This paper introduces \textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion}, a non-nuclear kinetic analogue to Orion. In this architecture, small, low-cost satellites (``PuffSats'') execute a rapid Earth--Jupiter--Earth gravity-assist loop, returning on retrograde orbits at Earth-relative speeds of \qtyrange{55}{68}{\kilo\meter\per\second} in just 2.2 or 3.3 years (two or three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods). At these speeds, a PuffSat's kinetic energy per unit mass exceeds the energy per unit mass released by methalox combustion by more than two orders of magnitude. As each PuffSat approaches the target vehicle, its working mass disperses into a gas cloud that mixes with propellant released by the target. The resulting fireball either strikes the target's pusher plate or is captured in a pulsed nozzle chamber that can produce thrust in any direction, including head-on toward the incoming PuffSats. We argue that because launched payloads can include new PuffSats, this mechanism can sustain exponential growth in launch capacity that, once initiated, requires no further orbital-class chemical boosters. Finally, we evaluate a PuffSat architecture optimized for launching orbital data centers and find that, under the stated assumptions, it could undercut near-term fully reusable launch systems such as SpaceX Starship~\cite{starship} on cost.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Objects crossing Earth on retrograde heliocentric orbits carry specific kinetic energies roughly a hundred times the chemical energy densities of rocket fuel. \textit{PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion} converts this kinetic energy into high-specific-impulse, high-thrust propulsion. The concept builds on ideas from Project Orion and pellet-beam propulsion. Unlike most pellet-beam concepts (see \cite{katz2026aim}), PuffSats acquire their extreme velocities from natural orbital mechanics rather than hypothetical giant accelerators or nuclear explosions.

Large orbital data centers are a potential near-term market for very heavy lift. We compare speculative early PuffSat launch costs with those of a fully reusable rocket system serving the same destinations (see \autoref{sec:launch_cost_speculation}). If returning PuffSats can deliver enough momentum to send a greater mass of replacement PuffSats onto new Jupiter trajectories, the available returning mass can grow exponentially from cycle to cycle (\autoref{sec:exponential_mass_growth}). Once bootstrapped, this cycle could supply its own reaction mass without repeatedly launching that mass aboard an orbital-class reusable rocket. The initial conventional launch costs would therefore become progressively less important to the system's overall cost.

PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion builds on advances in autonomous satellite formation flying. PRISMA demonstrated autonomous relative navigation and control in orbit~\cite{damico2011prisma_safe}, while the VISORS mission develops these capabilities for a precision CubeSat formation~\cite{guffanti2023autonomous}. PuffSat formations would be much larger, although their formation-keeping requirements would be less precise than those of science missions such as VISORS. PuffSats must also solve the distinct problem of hit-to-target guidance between the PuffSat swarm and the receiving vehicle, together with target-vehicle corrections between successive impacts (see \autoref{sec:guidance_navigation}).

Each PuffSat must carry a low-cost gas-forming payload through a long interplanetary trajectory. The payload must survive radiation and micrometeorites, and it must keep its contents and electronics within thermal limits. The PuffSat's dry mass components must not hit the receiving vehicle. We sketch a design satisfying these constraints in \autoref{sec:puffsat_satellite_architecture}. The required Jovian trajectories are described in \autoref{sec:jupiter_gravity_assists}. At the receiving vehicle, a pusher plate or pulsed nozzle must convert the arriving flow into thrust. Pusher plates are analyzed in \autoref{sec:pusher_plates}, and nozzles are discussed in \autoref{sec:rocket_nozzles}.

The companion white paper was originally written to establish the technical groundwork for hard-science-fiction world-building. This paper asks the narrower question of whether a focused application of PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion could make sense for real-world space missions. Its purpose is to expose the concept and its assumptions to critical analysis and to solicit feedback from experts in the relevant fields.

\section{Data Centers, Anti-Satellite Weapons, And Launch Costs}\label{sec:data_center_attacks}
For the data-center application, the launch-cost comparison depends on the destination, which must
satisfy both latency and survivability requirements.
Iran's drone strikes on Amazon's Gulf data centers in March 2026 established that data centers are
wartime targets~\cite{cnbc2026_aws_strike,gatech2026_aws_strike}. Those were drones against
buildings, so they say nothing about orbital reach. China's destruction of the FY-1C weather
satellite at \SI{865}{\kilo\meter} in January 2007 says a great deal~\cite{kelso2007asat,nasa_odqn2007_fengyun}.
A direct-ascent interceptor~\cite{kan2007_asat} arrives at sun-synchronous altitude in minutes, which leaves no window to
disperse a cluster or to engage the inbound vehicle. A transfer to an Earth-Moon Lagrange point takes
days, at a cost of \SI{2.6}{\second} of round-trip light time to an Earth customer. Distance does not
hide a data center, and a published halo orbit is as predictable as a sun-synchronous one. What
distance buys is the time an active response needs.
