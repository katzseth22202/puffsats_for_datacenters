\begin{abstract}
PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion is a non-nuclear kinetic analogue of Project Orion's nuclear pulse propulsion. Every impulse lands on a pusher plate. Each pulse is a low-cost satellite (``PuffSat'') striking a charge of inert propellant held a few meters off the plate, and the merged fireball expands against the plate.

A reusable booster lofts a launch unit above the atmosphere. Kapton sheets as wide as the plate, returning from the previous cycle, overtake it at \SI{99}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and push it into a parking orbit. Ten days later, compact impactors meet it at \ang{130} to its thrust axis. They thread a \SI{1}{\meter} hole in a bowl and send the ship toward Jupiter. Jupiter bends the ship into a dive to \SI{9.86}{R_\odot}, the depth Parker Solar Probe has flown, while graphite disks take the mirror-image dive. Ship and disks meet head-on at about \SI{392}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, and the disks add \SI{9.8}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. The Oberth effect returns the ship across Earth's orbit \SI{3.28}{\year} after it left, and it releases its mass as the next cycle's sheets.

On simulated impacts and a cost model that charges every cost, the fleet grows 3.36 times per cycle and doubles every \SI{1.87}{\year}. Two questions decide whether that holds, and this paper puts them to specialists. Can each PuffSat be aimed as precisely as the plates require? Can the plates and sheets survive the loads computed for them?
\end{abstract}
