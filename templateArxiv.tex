\begin{abstract}
PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion is a non-nuclear kinetic analogue of Project Orion's nuclear pulse propulsion. Each pulse comes from a low-cost satellite (``PuffSat'') striking a charge of inert propellant held a few meters off a pusher plate, and the merged fireball expands against the plate. A gravity assist at Jupiter and an Oberth maneuver near the Sun return PuffSats to Earth at up to \SI{99}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. At that speed the returning PuffSats push more mass off Earth than their own, and that mass flies the loop again, so the fleet grows exponentially. Three plates catch three streams of PuffSats, two at Earth and one at the Sun, and every PuffSat impulse lands on a plate rather than in a nozzle.

Three questions decide whether that growth holds, and this paper puts them to specialists. Can each PuffSat be aimed as precisely as the plates require? Can a PuffSat's energy couple efficiently into a charge much heavier than itself? Can the plates and the PuffSats survive the loads computed for them?
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
PuffSat Pulsed Propulsion pushes a ship with a stream of guided impactors accelerated by Oberth maneuvers near the Sun and planetary gravity assists to extreme velocities. Each impactor, a low-cost satellite called a PuffSat, strikes a charge of cheap propellant held a few meters off a pusher plate, and the merged fireball expands against the plate. The parent paper develops the whole architecture~\cite{katz2026aim}. \autoref{sec:three_burns} describes the three plates and the three burns they make.

\paragraph{A plate, not a nozzle.} Pusher plates work best in the overtake direction where the rocket moves in the same direction the impactors were moving.  Our solar Oberth maneuvers and outbound thrust towards Jupiter require head-on thrust opposite the impactor's incoming motion.  A rocket nozzle would help, but we avoid it here because a pulsed nozzle is heavy and temperature limited compared to the pusher plate.  Magnetic nozzles require cryogenics for magnets, which would be hard near the Sun.  The parent considers nozzles but we opt instead for bowl-shaped plates that hold the charge at their focus, with a hole for the impactor to pass where it arrives from the ship's side.  As long as the mass carried on the rocket is large compared to the impactor mass, an efficiently coupled fireball will still hit the plate at high speeds.

\paragraph{How it differs from Orion.} Orion's bomb could not be set off against its plate. Its energy crossed a gap of tens of meters as soft X-rays, and a shaped tungsten slab turned that radiation back into a jet aimed at the ship~\cite{balcomb1970nuclear,dyson2002project_orion}. A guided impactor needs no such converter, because it delivers its energy where it is aimed. The parent expects the fireball to stay opaque, so that little of its energy leaves as light. Each pulse is a chosen mass, so the design can lower the acceleration per pulse to ease the load on the plate's spring.

\paragraph{Why dive at the Sun.} The fleet could grow on Jupiter alone. Flown on the same plates with no dive, that loop grows 1.65 times per three Earth--Jupiter synodic periods (\SI{3.28}{\year}). Adding the dive raises the growth to 3.36 times. The ship meets the graphite disks deep in the Sun's gravity well, where the Oberth effect turns their \SI{9.8}{\kilo\meter\per\second} into a much larger gain in return speed. Deeper dives grow faster. At \SI{4}{R_\odot} the fleet grows 4.99 times per cycle and doubles in \SI{1.41}{\year}. The baseline stops at \SI{9.86}{R_\odot} because Parker Solar Probe has flown that depth and kept working~\cite{nasa2024_parker_survives}.

The cycle's numbers come from simulated impacts and a preliminary model that charges every mass cost, counted in kilograms rather than dollars. Three questions need to be put to specialists. The first is whether each PuffSat can be aimed as precisely as the plates require. The second is whether a PuffSat's energy couples efficiently into a charge much heavier than itself. The third is whether the plates and the PuffSats survive the loads computed for them.

\section{Three Plates, Three Burns}\label{sec:three_burns}
The cycle runs on three plates, and each catches one stream of PuffSats. Every PuffSat strikes a polyethylene charge held off the plate rather than the plate itself. The figures below are the parent's, at a \SI{9.86}{R_\odot} perihelion and a \SI{60}{\kilo\meter\per\second} return~\cite{katz2026aim}. The return is the ship's speed relative to the Sun after it climbs back out of the Sun's well. \autoref{tab:three_burns} lists the three burns.

\paragraph{The push.} A reusable booster lofts a \SI{1500}{\tonne} launch unit above the atmosphere. At \SI{400}{\kilo\meter}, returning Kapton sheets about \SI{70}{\micro\meter} thick and as wide as the plate overtake it at \SI{99}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. Each strikes a charge behind a flat push plate of about \SI{63}{\tonne}, which rides a nitrogen gas spring on an \SI{8}{\meter} stroke. The pulses push the unit from rest into a 10-day parking orbit whose apoapsis lies at lunar distance. At apoapsis a methalox stage burns \SI{0.33}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. That swings the orbit from the push axis onto the departure axis and raises periapsis to \SI{600}{\kilo\meter}. It costs about 9\% of the stack, and no stream is present there to do it.

\paragraph{The departure.} Ten days after the push, the rest of the returning stream meets the ship at periapsis, at \ang{130} to its thrust axis and from the ship's side. The ship now flies the second face of the same sprung assembly, a bowl whose depth is half its diameter. Compact impactors thread a hole about \SI{1}{\meter} across and strike a charge at the bowl's focus. A flat plate struck from the side keeps about a third of its ideal impulse, because the fireball slides along it. The bowl turns 60 to 73\% of the expansion's momentum back along its axis, and the hole costs 0.5 to 0.6\%. The pulses are slow so that each impactor has time to be aimed through the hole. The burn takes \SI{4.21}{\kilo\meter\per\second} and loses another \SI{0.46}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, because its thrust holds one direction while the ship swings around Earth. The ship leaves Earth at \SI{10.24}{\kilo\meter\per\second} of excess speed. The push plate and bowl, about \SI{70}{\tonne} with their spring, are later steered into Jupiter's atmosphere.

The two Earth streams close at different speeds because they come home at different times. The ships whose sheets make the push return ten days ahead of the rest, on a faster climb out of the Sun's well, \SI{87}{\kilo\meter\per\second} against \SI{60}{\kilo\meter\per\second}.

\paragraph{Jupiter, unpowered.} The ship passes Jupiter at 7.97 Jupiter radii and falls into a prograde dive toward the Sun. The graphite disks, \SI{7.2}{\tonne} that rode out with the ship, must take the mirror-image retrograde dive. A carrier burns \SI{1.67}{\kilo\meter\per\second} at a 6.5 Jupiter radius perijove to put them there, spending about 1\% of the ship in propellant.

\paragraph{The Sun boost.} The disks meet the ship head-on at perihelion, closing at about \SI{392}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. They strike charges in a third bowl, like the departure's but with no hole, since the disks arrive along its axis. The boost is \SI{9.8}{\kilo\meter\per\second}. Bought at a perihelion speed of \SI{196}{\kilo\meter\per\second}, it leaves the \SI{60}{\kilo\meter\per\second} return. The ship then releases \SI{392}{\tonne} as Kapton sheets, which cross Earth \SI{3.28}{\year} after the ship left and become the next cycle's push and departure streams. The Sun bowl is kept as dead mass.

Only two burns in the cycle use carried propellant, the methalox turn at apoapsis and the carrier burn at Jupiter. Every impulse a PuffSat delivers lands on a plate.
